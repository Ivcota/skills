# Part 2 — Six Ways to Make People Like You

These are the rapport principles. The thesis: relationships built before you need anything are infinitely cheaper than relationships built when you do.

> "You can make more friends in two months by becoming genuinely interested in other people than you can in two years by trying to get other people interested in you." (Part 2, Ch. 1)

## 2.1 — Become genuinely interested in other people

### The argument

> "It is the individual who is not interested in his fellow men who has the greatest difficulties in life and provides the greatest injury to others. It is from among such individuals that all human failures spring."
> — Alfred Adler (quoted Part 2, Ch. 1)

Carnegie cites the New York Telephone Company study: in 500 conversations, the most-used word was "I" (3,900 times). People are interested in themselves. The few who genuinely turn that around — Theodore Roosevelt, Howard Thurston the magician — become the rare exception, and the world rewards them disproportionately.

### Examples

- **Roosevelt** sat up the night before a meeting reading on whatever the visitor cared about. His valet James Amos noted he greeted every White House servant by name; even after leaving office, he'd return and ask the kitchen maid if she still made the cornbread he loved.
- **Thurston the magician** told Carnegie that before each performance he repeated to himself: "I love my audience. I love my audience." Thurston grossed nearly $2 million in profit over 40 years; rivals with equal technical skill never broke through.
- **Edward Sykes** of Johnson & Johnson retained an account by routinely greeting the soda clerk and sales clerk before going to the owner. The day he was about to be dropped, those clerks vouched for him to the owner, who reversed and doubled the order.

### How to apply

1. **Research before contact.** Read what they've written, posted, built. Bring one specific thing to ask about.
2. **Greet everyone.** The assistant, the receptionist, the junior on the call. Carnegie's evidence: it's not just kind — it materially changes outcomes (Sykes, Roosevelt).
3. **Make notes.** Carnegie kept a birthday book and sent a card to every contact. The principle generalizes: capture what they care about, surface it next time.

### Copy patterns

- "Before we start — I read your post on X, and I had a question about [specific thing you actually want to know]."
- "How's [thing they mentioned last time]?"

## 2.2 — Smile

### The argument

> "Actions speak louder than words, and a smile says, 'I like you. You make me happy. I am glad to see you.'" (Part 2, Ch. 2)

The chapter's evidence is heavy on workplace examples. Carnegie cites a New York stockbroker who reported, after applying the principle for two months, that his marriage and business both improved. A computer department manager (Robert Cryer) reported that smiling helped him recruit a sought-after PhD because the candidate said: "Your smile gave me the impression that I was welcome."

The principle extends to *voice*: many companies, Carnegie notes, train their telephone operators to smile while greeting callers because the smile is audible. "Say 'Hello' in tones that bespeak how pleased you are to have the person call."

Carnegie quotes Frank Bettger: "A smile that comes from the heart is the kind that brings a good price in the marketplace."

### How to apply

- **In person and on video:** smile when you greet, when you listen (briefly), when you say goodbye.
- **On the phone:** literally smile while talking. The voice changes.
- **In writing:** the textual equivalent is warmth — "Glad to hear from you," "Looking forward to this," genuine exclamation rather than corporate flatness.

### Caveat

> "It must be a real smile, a heart-warming smile, a smile that comes from within … not the kind of mechanical grin that fools nobody." (Part 2, Ch. 2)

A forced smile reads as worse than no smile. The fix is upstream: think of something that genuinely makes you glad about this person before the interaction.

## 2.3 — Remember that a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language

### The argument

> "A person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language."

Andrew Carnegie's signature method: as a boy he caught a rabbit; she had a litter; he had no food for them, so he told the neighborhood children that if they pulled clover for the rabbits he'd name a baby after each of them. The children threw themselves at the task. Decades later, competing with George Pullman for a sleeping-car contract from the Pennsylvania Railroad, Carnegie proposed a merger. Pullman asked: "What shall we call our new company?" Carnegie said: "The Pullman Palace Car Company." Pullman's face lit up; the merger went through. (Part 2, Ch. 3)

### How to apply

1. **Get the name right.** Spell it twice. Pronounce it the way they pronounce it.
2. **Use it early in the conversation.** Once at the start, again at a meaningful moment, once at the close.
3. **Use the name they prefer.** Robert vs. Bob. Liz vs. Elizabeth. Ask if unsure.
4. **In writing:** address them by name in the salutation; refer to them by name (rather than "you") at one or two key points; sign with your own name (not just "Best,").

### What not to do

Don't overdo it — name-dropping the name in every sentence reads as sales-y manipulation, the inverse of the principle. Twice or three times in a 20-minute conversation is enough.

## 2.4 — Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves.

### The argument

> "If you aspire to be a good conversationalist, be an attentive listener. To be interesting, be interested." (Part 2, Ch. 4)

Carnegie tells of meeting a botanist at a dinner party. He listened, asked questions, listened more — for hours. At evening's end, the botanist told the host Carnegie was "a most interesting conversationalist." Carnegie: "I had said hardly anything at all."

### Examples

- **Edward Bok** (later editor of *Ladies' Home Journal*) started his career at thirteen as a Western Union messenger boy. He wrote letters asking famous people about their lives — Garfield, Grant, Emerson, Longfellow — and they wrote back. He made himself one of the most successful magazine editors in American history by being a good listener at scale.
- **C.M. Knaphle** spent four years failing to sell fuel to a chain-store buyer. He visited once after Carnegie's course and asked the buyer to teach him about the chain-store business. The buyer talked for nearly two hours. As Knaphle was leaving, the buyer offered him an order without being asked.
- **Lincoln** during the Civil War summoned an old friend to Washington — talked at him for hours about the slavery proclamation, then sent him home without asking his opinion. "He had wanted merely a friendly, sympathetic listener to whom he could unburden himself."

### How to apply

1. **Ask one more question than feels natural.** Most people stop one question short of where the interesting answer lives.
2. **"Tell me more about that."** The single most useful sentence.
3. **Don't formulate your response while they're talking.** Listen to understand, not to reply.
4. **Don't interrupt.** Even when you think you have the answer.

### Carnegie's warning

> "Bores intoxicated with their own egos … People who talk only of themselves think only of themselves." (Part 2, Ch. 4)

The cost of being a poor listener isn't just lost information — it's that you become uninteresting to people whose interest you most need.

## 2.5 — Talk in terms of the other person's interests

### The argument

The royal road to a person's heart is to talk about the things they treasure most.

### Examples

- **William Lyon Phelps**, age 8, met a New York lawyer who talked enthusiastically about boats — Phelps's obsession at the time. Phelps later learned the lawyer cared nothing about boats. "Why then did he talk all the time about boats?" "Because he is a gentleman. He saw you were interested in boats, and he talked about the things he knew would interest and please you. He made himself agreeable."
- **Edward Chalif** wanted a corporate president to fund a Boy Scout's European trip. He'd heard the man had once written a million-dollar check and framed it. Chalif opened the meeting by asking to see the framed check, then to hear the story. The president eventually offered five boys instead of one, plus letters of introduction, plus a tour of Paris.
- **Henry Duvernoy** of a NY bakery had pitched a hotel for four years with no result. He learned the manager belonged to the Hotel Greeters of America and was its president. Duvernoy met him and talked about the Greeters. The next day the steward called: "I don't know what you did to the old boy, but he sure is sold on you!"

### How to apply

1. **Find the topic before the meeting.** What do they post about? What's their last project? What's their hobby?
2. **Open there.** Get them talking about it, with real interest, before you raise your topic.
3. **Don't fake it.** Phelps's lawyer didn't fake interest in boats — he asked questions and let Phelps's enthusiasm carry the conversation. You're not pretending to be a boat enthusiast; you're pretending to be a boat *student*.

## 2.6 — Make the other person feel important — and do it sincerely

### The argument

> "There is one all-important law of human conduct. If we obey that law, we shall almost never get into trouble … The law is this: *Always make the other person feel important*." (Part 2, Ch. 6)

Carnegie names this the Golden Rule applied: give to others what we ourselves want — recognition, importance, the sense that we matter.

### Examples

- **Carnegie at the post office:** waiting in line, he noticed the bored clerk weighing envelopes. He looked for something to honestly admire and noted the clerk's hair. "I certainly wish I had your head of hair." The clerk lit up, told him many people had complimented his hair, and the entire transaction transformed. Carnegie's reflection: he wanted nothing from the clerk except the feeling of having brightened a stranger's day.
- **Hall Caine**, blacksmith's son, became the best-selling novelist of his era because he wrote a fan essay to Dante Gabriel Rossetti, who then invited him to London as his secretary — opening the literary world.
- **The classroom case:** a teacher (Ronald Rowland) had a shy fourteen-year-old student named Chris. He asked Chris to join the advanced class. Chris, holding back tears: "Who am I, Mr. Rowland? Am I good enough?" Rowland: "Yes, Chris, you are good enough." The boy walked out "two inches taller." Rowland made a sign for his classroom: "**you are important**."
- **The little aunt's Packard:** Carnegie's student Mr. R— admired a lonely widow's house, her treasures, her late husband's classic car. She insisted on giving him the car. The story's point: "this lady, left all alone in a big house with her paisley shawls, her French antiques, and her memories, was starving for a little recognition."
- **George Eastman and the chairs:** James Adamson got a $90,000 order from Kodak's George Eastman by complimenting Eastman's office woodwork. Eastman, despite a busy schedule, talked for hours and the two became close friends.

### How to apply

The application is in the small courtesies. Carnegie cites them as "the hallmark of good breeding":

> "Little phrases such as 'I'm sorry to trouble you,' 'Would you be so kind as to ___?', 'Won't you please?', 'Would you mind?', 'Thank you' — little courtesies like these oil the cogs of the monotonous grind of everyday life." (Part 2, Ch. 6)

Specifically:
1. **Greet the people no one greets.** The intern, the support agent, the assistant.
2. **Use their title or expertise.** "You're the one who'd know — what's the trade-off here?" gives the expert the floor they're owed.
3. **Disraeli's rule (quoted by Carnegie):** "Talk to people about themselves and they will listen for hours."

### The ethical edge

> "The unvarnished truth is that almost all the people you meet feel themselves superior to you in some way, and a sure way to their hearts is to let them realize in some subtle way that you recognize their importance, and recognize it sincerely." (Part 2, Ch. 6)

Carnegie quotes Emerson: "Every man I meet is my superior in some way. In that, I learn of him." The principle works because it's *true* — not as a manipulation tactic.

## In a nutshell — Part 2

> Principle 1 — Become genuinely interested in other people.
> Principle 2 — Smile.
> Principle 3 — Remember that a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language.
> Principle 4 — Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves.
> Principle 5 — Talk in terms of the other person's interests.
> Principle 6 — Make the other person feel important — and do it sincerely.

(HTWFIP, Part 2, "In a Nutshell")

These are sequenced: 2.1 produces real interest, 2.4 lets them feel heard, 2.5 keeps the conversation in their territory, and 2.6 closes by making them feel important. Smile (2.2) and name (2.3) are the small high-frequency cues that anchor the larger principles.
