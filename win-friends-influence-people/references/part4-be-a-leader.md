# Part 4 — Be a Leader: How to Change People Without Giving Offense or Arousing Resentment

This is the criticism framework. When correction is genuinely necessary, run through these nine in order.

> "A leader's job often includes changing your people's attitudes and behavior."
> — HTWFIP, Part 4 nutshell

## 4.1 — Begin with praise and honest appreciation

> "It is always easier to listen to unpleasant things after we have heard some praise of our good points." (Part 4, Ch. 1)

The dentist's Novocaine: the patient still gets a drilling, but the anesthetic makes it survivable.

**The Coolidge case:** President Calvin Coolidge to a secretary: "That's a pretty dress you are wearing this morning, and you are a very attractive young woman. Now, don't get stuck up. I just said that to make you feel good. From now on, I wish you would be a little bit more careful with your punctuation." (Part 4, Ch. 1)

**The Lincoln-Hooker letter** (April 26, 1863): Lincoln needed to correct General Hooker's grave faults. He opened by acknowledging Hooker's bravery, skill, ambition, and self-confidence — all praised explicitly — *before* turning to the criticism. The letter is short but its structure is the model: praise specific, criticism direct, no equivocation.

**How to apply:**
1. Find something *real* to praise. Not generic, not invented. Something you specifically value.
2. State it. Don't trail it.
3. Then — separately — state the correction.

## 4.2 — Call attention to people's mistakes indirectly

The "but" trap, explicitly named (Part 4, Ch. 2):

> Bad: "We're really proud of you, Johnnie, for raising your grades this term. **But** if you had worked harder on your algebra, the results would have been better."
>
> Good: "We're really proud of you, Johnnie, for raising your grades this term, **and** by continuing the same conscientious efforts next term, your algebra grade can be up with all the others."

The first invalidates the praise; "but" tells Johnnie the praise was a setup. The second extends the praise forward into the desired behavior.

**The hard hat case** (Part 1, Ch. 1, applied here): George Johnston, safety coordinator, used to tell workers without hard hats they had to comply, with a lot of authority. He got sullen acceptance and the hats came off as soon as he left. He switched to asking if the hats were uncomfortable or didn't fit, and reminded them in a pleasant tone that the hat was for their protection. Compliance went up; resentment vanished.

**Application:** If you can correct without explicit criticism — by changing the question, by modeling the behavior, by changing the system — do that. Direct criticism is the last resort, not the first.

## 4.3 — Talk about your own mistakes before criticizing the other person

Carnegie correcting his niece Josephine, age nineteen, after she had blundered:

> "You've made a mistake, Josephine, but the Lord knows, it's no worse than many I have made. You were not born with judgment. That comes only with experience, and you are better than I was at your age. I have been guilty of so many stupid, silly things myself, I have very little inclination to criticize you or anyone." (Part 4, Ch. 3)

> "It isn't nearly so difficult to listen to a recital of your faults if the person criticizing begins by humbly admitting that he, too, is far from impeccable."

**Application:** Before correcting, tell the analogous mistake you've made. The criticism becomes "we both have done X" rather than "you have done X."

The book's Prince Bernhard von Bülow / Kaiser Wilhelm II case is the negative example: von Bülow, asked by the Kaiser to take blame for the Kaiser's own errors, instead implied the Kaiser couldn't possibly have made such mistakes. The Kaiser exploded. Von Bülow's recovery was to praise the Kaiser exhaustively before any correction — but he'd already lost ground by failing to lead with his own humility.

## 4.4 — Ask questions instead of giving direct orders

> "Owen D. Young never said, for example, 'Do this or do that,' or 'Don't do this or don't do that.' He would say, 'You might consider this,' or 'Do you think that would work?' Frequently he would say, after he had dictated a letter, 'What do you think of this?'" (Part 4, Ch. 4)

The book's contrast case: an instructor at a Wyoming vocational school stormed into a classroom and screamed at a student about a wrongly parked car: "Move that car and move it right now, or I'll wrap a chain around it and drag it out of there." The car got moved — and every student in the class spent the rest of the term making the instructor's life harder.

A friendly question — "Whose car is in the driveway? If it could be moved, others can get in and out" — would have produced the same compliance with no resentment.

**The Macdonald case** (Ian Macdonald, Johannesburg): manager of a precision-machine plant, faced with an order he was sure his shop couldn't fill on time. Instead of pushing his people, he gathered them, explained the situation, and asked questions:
- "Is there anything we can do to handle this order?"
- "Can anyone think of different ways to process it through the shop that will make it possible to take the order?"
- "Is there any way to adjust our hours or personnel assignments that would help?"

The employees came up with ideas, insisted he take the order, approached it with a "We can do it" attitude. The order was delivered on time.

**Application:** "Could you…" "What if we…" "Have you considered…" beats "Do this." Always.

## 4.5 — Let the other person save face

> "Even if we are right and the other person is definitely wrong, we only destroy ego by causing someone to lose face. The legendary French aviation pioneer and author Antoine de Saint-Exupéry wrote: 'I have no right to say or do anything that diminishes a man in his own eyes. What matters is not what I think of him, but what he thinks of himself. Hurting a man in his dignity is a crime.'" (Part 4, Ch. 5)

**Application:**
- **Fire / let go in private.** Never in front of the team.
- **When refusing,** explain in terms that don't humiliate: "this isn't the right fit for what you're best at" rather than "you're not good enough."
- **When correcting in a meeting,** address the *issue* not the person. "We need to revisit this number" not "Sarah's number is wrong."

## 4.6 — Praise the slightest improvement and praise every improvement. Be "hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise."

The book's foundational quote, repeated from Part 1, Ch. 2 (Charles Schwab) and Part 2, Ch. 6: this principle is the affirmative version of "Don't criticize."

> "B.F. Skinner, the world-famous psychologist, proved through his experiments that an animal rewarded for good behavior will learn much more rapidly and retain what it learns far more effectively than an animal punished for bad behavior. Later studies have shown that the same applies to humans." (Part 1, Ch. 1)

**Praise must be specific.** "Great job!" doesn't work. "The way you structured the second paragraph — that's the move; it carried the whole argument" works.

**Praise must be timely.** Praise within hours of the behavior; let it lapse and the reinforcement weakens.

**The Pete Barlow / dog trainer case** (Part 4, Ch. 6): Barlow trained dogs and pigs; his approach was the moment the animal showed any improvement, he praised, patted, and rewarded it. The same applies to people.

## 4.7 — Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to

> "If you want to improve a person in a certain respect, act as though that particular trait were already one of his outstanding characteristics." (Part 4, Ch. 7)

Shakespeare: "Assume a virtue, if you have it not."

**The Henry Henke case:** Henke, owner of a Detroit auto repair shop, had a mechanic whose work had been declining. Instead of complaining, Henke called him in: "Bill, you are an excellent mechanic. You have been doing this kind of work for many years. You have repaired many cars to customers' satisfaction. I think you ought to know that I am pleased with your work." The mechanic's work went back up.

**Modern application:** When someone is underperforming on a trait you need from them — reliability, attention to detail, judgment — *publicly assign that trait to them* (genuinely, in their hearing, without the irony of "see, I'm trying to motivate you"). They will tend to live up to the assigned identity.

## 4.8 — Use encouragement. Make the fault seem easy to correct.

> "Tell your child, your spouse, or your employee that he or she is stupid or dumb at a certain thing, has no gift for it, and is doing it all wrong, and you have destroyed almost every incentive to try to improve. But use the opposite technique — be liberal with your encouragement, make the thing seem easy to do, let the other person know that you have faith in his ability to do it … and he will practice until the dawn comes in at the window in order to excel." (Part 4, Ch. 8)

This is the inverse of the discouragement pattern. The fault is real but the framing matters: "this is hard, I get why it's slipping, here's the small adjustment that gets you there" preserves agency. "You can't do this" removes it.

**Copy pattern:**
- "The hard part is X, and I see you've already done it. The remaining piece is mostly Y, which is mechanical — let's do it together once and you'll have it."

## 4.9 — Make the other person happy about doing the thing you suggest

The capstone. Carnegie gives an explicit six-step recipe (Part 4, Ch. 9):

1. **Be sincere.** Do not promise anything that you cannot deliver. Forget about the benefits to yourself and concentrate on the benefits to the other person.
2. **Know exactly what it is you want the other person to do.**
3. **Be empathetic.** Ask yourself what it is the other person really wants.
4. **Consider the benefits that person will receive from doing what you suggest.**
5. **Match those benefits to the other person's wants.**
6. **When you make your request, put it in a form that will convey to the other person the idea that he personally will benefit.**

The book's example: a curt order — "John, we have customers coming in tomorrow and I need the stockroom cleaned out. So sweep it out, put the stock in neat piles on the shelves and polish the counter" — vs. the same request restructured around John's likely interest in the company image — "John, we have a job that should be completed right away. *If it is done now, we won't be faced with it later*. I am bringing some customers in tomorrow to show our facilities … if you could sweep it out, put the stock in neat piles on the shelves, and polish the counter, it would make us look efficient and *you will have done your part to provide a good company image*."

Same task. Different framing. Higher chance of cooperative effort and lower chance of resentment.

**Napoleon's titles:** Carnegie cites Napoleon creating the Legion of Honor — 15,000 crosses distributed, 18 generals named "Marshals of France," the army renamed the "Grand Army." Critics called it giving "toys" to veterans. Napoleon: "Men are ruled by toys."

The principle is not cynical — it's that the experience of contributing is more motivating than the contribution itself. Give people a name, a title, a stake, an ownership of the outcome, and they will cooperate gladly.

> Principle 1 — Begin with praise and honest appreciation.
> Principle 2 — Call attention to people's mistakes indirectly.
> Principle 3 — Talk about your own mistakes before criticizing the other person.
> Principle 4 — Ask questions instead of giving direct orders.
> Principle 5 — Let the other person save face.
> Principle 6 — Praise the slightest improvement and praise every improvement. Be "hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise."
> Principle 7 — Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.
> Principle 8 — Use encouragement. Make the fault seem easy to correct.
> Principle 9 — Make the other person happy about doing the thing you suggest.

(HTWFIP, Part 4, "In a Nutshell")

## How to use Part 4 in practice — the criticism script

Most real-world criticism is two minutes of conversation. The full Part 4 script in two minutes:

1. **Praise specific** (4.1): "The way you handled X was strong — specifically, [thing]."
2. **Own version first** (4.3): "I've made the same mistake before — [your version]."
3. **Indirect / ask** (4.2 + 4.4): "What do you think went sideways with Y?" — let them name it.
4. **Save face** (4.5): If they don't see it, surface it with no audience.
5. **Make the fix small** (4.8): "The fix is mostly Z — should be quick once we've done it once."
6. **Reputation forward** (4.7): "I see you as the person who closes the loop on these — that's why I'm asking you specifically."
7. **Reframe their benefit** (4.9): "Doing it now means [benefit to them], not [cost they want to avoid]."

Run that ladder, in order, and most "this is going to be a hard conversation" turn into normal conversations.
