# Part 1 — Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

Three principles. These are preconditions: violate any of them and the rest of the book cannot rescue you.

## Principle 1.1 — Don't criticize, condemn or complain

### The argument

Carnegie opens the book with three killers — "Two Gun" Crowley, Al Capone, Albert Fall (Teapot Dome scandal) — to make a single point: **people don't blame themselves**. Even murderers and embezzlers regard themselves as misunderstood. "Crowley didn't blame himself for anything … Capone didn't condemn himself … Fall, condemned as few men in public life have ever been — did he repent? Never!" (Part 1, Ch. 1).

> "Ninety-nine times out of a hundred, people don't criticize themselves for anything, no matter how wrong it may be." (p. 5)

If hardened criminals don't criticize themselves, the colleague who botched the deploy won't either. Criticism doesn't induce ownership; it induces justification.

### The mechanism

> "Criticism is futile because it puts a person on the defensive and usually makes him strive to justify himself. Criticism is dangerous, because it wounds a person's precious pride, hurts his sense of importance, and arouses resentment." (Part 1, Ch. 1)

Carnegie cites B.F. Skinner: animals (and humans) rewarded for good behavior learn faster and retain longer than those punished for bad behavior. And Hans Selye: "As much as we thirst for approval, we dread condemnation."

### The Lincoln/Meade case study

After Gettysburg (July 1863), General Meade let Lee's army escape across the Potomac despite Lincoln's explicit order to attack. Lincoln, furious, sat down and wrote Meade the angriest letter of his presidency:

> "I do not believe you appreciate the magnitude of the misfortune involved in Lee's escape … the war will be prolonged indefinitely … Your golden opportunity is gone, and I am distressed immeasurably because of it." (p. 11)

He never sent the letter. It was found in his papers after his death. Lincoln's reasoning, as Carnegie reconstructs it: sending it would "make Meade try to justify himself … impair all his further usefulness as a commander, and perhaps force him to resign from the army."

The book closes the chapter with Carnegie's own youthful blunder — sending Richard Harding Davis a note signed "Dictated but not read" to seem busy and important — and the response that haunted him for ten years: "Your bad manners are exceeded only by your bad manners."

### How to apply

1. **Write the angry message. Do not send it.** Lincoln's discipline. The act of writing discharges the emotion; the act of withholding preserves the relationship.
2. **Distinguish past and future.** If the situation is past correction, criticism only relieves your feelings — at the cost of the relationship. Skip it.
3. **If correction is needed, jump to Part 4.** This principle does not say "never correct." It says "criticism by itself is useless." The Part 4 framework is *how* to correct.

### Copy patterns to drop

- "You should have…" / "Why didn't you just…" / "I told you…" / "You always / you never…"
- Public correction in any form (group chat, meeting, email cc'd to the team)

### Copy patterns to use

- Curiosity instead of accusation: "I'm trying to understand the call here — what was the trade-off as you saw it?"
- Forward-looking: "If we hit this again, what would we want to be true?"
- Confucius (quoted by Carnegie, p. 13): "Don't complain about the snow on your neighbor's roof when your own doorstep is unclean."

## Principle 1.2 — Give honest and sincere appreciation

### The argument

> "There is only one way under high heaven to get anybody to do anything … by making the other person want to do it." (Part 1, Ch. 2)

What do people want? Carnegie cites Freud (sex urge + desire to be great), Dewey (the desire to be important), and William James — whom he treats as the keystone:

> "The deepest principle in human nature is the craving to be appreciated."

Note Carnegie's emphasis: not the *wish*, not the *desire*, but the *craving*. He repeats it in italics. This is the spine of the entire book.

### Schwab as exhibit A

Charles Schwab was paid a million dollars a year as president of Andrew Carnegie's steel company in 1921 — equivalent to roughly $14M in 2025. Asked why, Schwab attributed it to people skills:

> "I consider my ability to arouse enthusiasm among my people the greatest asset I possess, and the way to develop the best that is in a person is by appreciation and encouragement. There is nothing else that so kills the ambitions of a person as criticisms from superiors. I never criticize anyone … I am hearty in my approbation and lavish in my praise." (Part 1, Ch. 2)

That phrase — *"hearty in my approbation and lavish in my praise"* — is the line Carnegie uses to anchor Principle 4.6 in Part 4. The book's structure is built around it.

### Appreciation vs. flattery

This is the most important distinction in Part 1. Carnegie:

> "The difference between appreciation and flattery? That is simple. One is sincere and the other insincere. One comes from the heart out; the other from the teeth out. One is unselfish; the other selfish. One is universally admired; the other universally condemned." (Part 1, Ch. 2)

Test for flattery: would the line work on anyone? If yes, it's flattery. Real appreciation names something specific that *only this person* did or is.

### How to apply

1. **Find one specific behavior.** Not "you're great" — "the way you de-escalated the customer call this morning, specifically when she said X and you said Y, that was the move."
2. **Say it in their language.** What do *they* take pride in? Praise that.
3. **Don't tie it to a request.** If appreciation is the lead-in to an ask, it reads as flattery. Separate them by hours or days when possible.
4. **Specific > superlative.** "Tighter than the draft" beats "amazing."

### Copy patterns

- "I noticed how you handled X — that was the part that mattered."
- "What you did with Y — I want to learn how you got there."
- "Thank you for [specific thing]. It changed [specific outcome]."

### Ethical boundary

Sincerity is non-negotiable. "If all we had to do was use flattery, everybody would catch on and we should all be experts in human relations" (Part 1, Ch. 2). If you can't find something genuine to appreciate, don't fake one — find a real one or skip the praise.

## Principle 1.3 — Arouse in the other person an eager want

### The argument

The hinge of all influence:

> "The only way I can get you to do anything is by giving you what you want." (Part 1, Ch. 3)

Carnegie quotes Henry Ford: "If there is any one secret of success, it lies in the ability to get the other person's point of view and see things from that person's angle as well as from your own."

### The Barbara Anderson letter

The cleanest case study (p. 44–45). Anderson, a NY bank employee, needed to relocate to Phoenix for her son's health. Her letter to twelve Phoenix banks did not lead with her need:

> "My ten years of bank experience should be of interest to a rapidly growing bank like yours.
> In various capacities in bank operations with the Bankers Trust Company in New York, leading to my present assignment as Branch Manager, I have acquired skills in all phases of banking including depositor relations, credits, loans and administration.
> I will be relocating to Phoenix in May and I am sure I can contribute to your growth and profit. I will be in Phoenix the week of April 3 and would appreciate the opportunity to show you how I can help your bank meet its goals."

Eleven of twelve banks invited her to interview. Carnegie: "Mrs. Anderson did not state what *she* wanted, but wrote in the letter how she could help them, and focused on *their* wants, not her own."

### The bed-wetting boy

The chapter's other case (p. 49–51): a boy who wet his bed nightly. Scolding, shaming, and pleading had failed. The parents asked: "How can we make this boy *want* to stop wetting his bed?" His wants: pajamas like Daddy's (instead of his grandmother's nightgown), and a bed of his own. They got him both — let him pick out the bed himself in the store, treated by the salesgirl like "a little gentleman who would like to do some shopping." The bed-wetting stopped. His pride was now invested in the outcome.

### How to apply

Before any persuasive ask, run this:

1. Stop. Don't write the request yet.
2. **What does this person want?** Money, recognition, status, time, autonomy, relief from a specific pain, a feeling of importance — be specific.
3. **What problem are they trying to solve?** Even better: what's already on their mind that you can attach to?
4. **Connect:** how does what I'm proposing help them with that?
5. Write the ask. Lead with their angle. Mention yours late or not at all.

### Copy pattern: cold outreach

- Bad: "I'd love to chat about a potential opportunity at our company."
- Good: "I saw your post on [specific problem they wrote about]. I solved it twice — once at [company], once at [company]. Happy to walk you through what worked, no strings."

### Copy pattern: internal persuasion

- Bad: "I want to migrate us to Postgres."
- Good: "We've had three on-call pages this quarter from [problem with current DB]. Postgres handles the failure mode without paging — which means our weekends back. Want me to write up the migration?"

### The ethical line

Carnegie is explicit (p. 47):

> "Looking at the other person's point of view and arousing in him an eager want for something is *not* to be construed as manipulating that person so that he will do something that is only for your benefit and his detriment. Each party should gain from the negotiation."

Test: if the person saw your full reasoning and incentive, would they still want to do this? If yes, you're influencing. If no, you're manipulating, and the principles will eventually backfire.

## In a nutshell — Part 1

> Principle 1 — Don't criticize, condemn or complain.
> Principle 2 — Give honest and sincere appreciation.
> Principle 3 — Arouse in the other person an eager want.

(HTWFIP, Part 1, "In a Nutshell")

These three are *preconditions*. None of Parts 2, 3, or 4 work on top of a pattern of criticism, flattery, or me-first asks. Get these right first.
