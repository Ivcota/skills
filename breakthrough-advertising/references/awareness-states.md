# State of Awareness — Reference Deep-Dive

> Source of truth: Eugene M. Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (Boardroom 2004 reissue of the 1966 original), Ch. 2 "State of Awareness" (pp. 13–36). All citations refer to **book** pages, not PDF pages. Section-header language preserves Schwartz's exact wording — the popular labels "Solution-aware" and "Problem-aware" are *not* in the book and are not used here.

The awareness scale is the second of Schwartz's two foundational diagnostics (after mass desire). Once the strategist has chosen the dominant desire, the awareness state determines exactly where the headline must begin.

---

## 1. The Headline's Real Job

The headline does not close. It does not even have to mention the product or the appeal. Its only function is to buy the next sentence.

> "Your headline does not need to sell at all. It does not have to mention your product. It does not even have to mention your main appeal." (p. 14)
>
> "Your headline has only one job — to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence of your ad. In exactly the same way, your second sentence has only one job — to force him to read the third sentence of your ad. And the third sentence — and every additional sentence in your ad — has exactly the same job." (pp. 14–15)
>
> "It is simply common sense that the more of your story you can force your prospect to read, the more thoroughly you can sell him." (p. 15)

The single exception (p. 15): a prospect already actively seeking the client's specific brand-name product, paired with a special price reduction. Then — and only then — the headline can do the full selling job.

The headline begins at the prospect's "precise point of awareness" (p. 15). Locating that point is the diagnostic task of Ch. 2, organized around three questions (p. 14):

1. "What is the mass desire that creates this market?"
2. "How much do these people know today about the way your product satisfies this desire? (Their State of Awareness.)"
3. "How many other products have been presented to them before yours? (Their State of Sophistication.)"

## 2. The Five States — Schwartz's Exact Section-Header Language

Schwartz walks the scale from most-aware (mechanical) to least-aware (creative): "We'll start at the Most Aware — the most mechanical selling job — and proceed to more and more difficult problems, requiring more and more creative solutions." (p. 16)

| # | Schwartz's section header (verbatim) | Page | Headline starts with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The Most Aware | p. 16 | Product name + price |
| 2 | The Customer Knows of the Product But Doesn't Yet Want It | p. 16 | Product + statement of superiority |
| 3 | How to Introduce New Products | p. 19 | The desire (or its solution) |
| 4 | How to Introduce Products That Solve Needs | p. 21 | The need / problem |
| 5 | How to Open Up a Completely Unaware Market | p. 23 | The market itself (identification) |

Schwartz also calls the fifth "the fifth stage" (pp. 25, 34). These five labels — and only these — are the canonical ones used in the book.

### Stage 1 — The Most Aware (p. 16)

> "The customer knows of your product — knows what it does — knows he wants it. At this point, he just hasn't gotten around to buying it yet." (p. 16)

Headline strategy: "Your headline — in fact, your entire ad — need state little more except the name of your product and a bargain price." (p. 16) Recap, store/coupon, close.

> "Here the copy writer is nothing more than the merchandise manager's phrase-maker. The price is the most important part of his headline. There is nothing creative about his job, and he should receive the lowest possible scale of pay." (p. 16)

Example: "Revere Zomar Lens, Electric Eye Camera — Formerly $149.50 — Now Only $119.95." (p. 16)

### Stage 2 — The Customer Knows of the Product But Doesn't Yet Want It (pp. 16–19)

The bulk of all advertising. "Here you are dealing with a product which is known — which has established a brand name — which has already-linked itself with an acknowledged public desire, and has proven that it satisfied that desire." (p. 17)

The structural rule: "You display the name of the product — either in the headline or in an equally large logo — and use the remainder of the headline to point out its superiority. The body of the ad is then an elaboration of that superiority — including visualization, documentation, mechanization. When you have finished weaving in every strand of your product's superiority, your ad is done." (p. 17)

### Stage 3 — How to Introduce New Products (pp. 19–21)

> "The prospect either knows, or recognizes immediately, that he wants what the product does; but he doesn't yet know that there is a product — your product — that will do it for him." (p. 19)

Three steps: "Name the desire and/or its solution in your headline. Prove that that solution can be accomplished. And show that the mechanism of that accomplishment is contained in your product." (p. 20)

The copywriter's required attributes: "First, analysis... Second, intuition... And third, verbal creativity... The ability to give a name to the still-undefined. To capture a feeling, a hope, a desire, a fear in words." (p. 20)

### Stage 4 — How to Introduce Products That Solve Needs (pp. 21–23)

> "The prospect has — not a desire — but a need. He recognizes the need immediately. But he doesn't yet realize the connection between the fulfillment of that need and your product." (p. 21)

Strategy: "Name the need and/or its solution in your headline. Then dramatize the need so vividly that the prospect realizes just how badly he needs the solution. And then present your product as the inevitable solution." (pp. 21–22)

### Stage 5 — How to Open Up a Completely Unaware Market (pp. 23–35)

> "The prospect is either not aware of his desire or his need — or he won't honestly admit it to himself without being lead [sic] into it by your ad — or the need is so general and amorphous that it resists being summed up in a single headline — or it's a secret that just can't be verbalized." (p. 23)
>
> "This is the outer reach of the awareness scale. These are the people who are still the logical prospects for your product; and yet, in their own minds, they are hundreds of miles away from accepting that product. It is your job to bridge that gap." (p. 23)

## 3. The Seven-Task Headline Grid for Stage 2

Schwartz lists exactly seven jobs the Stage-2 headline can perform (p. 17). Pick one — only one — per ad.

| # | Task | Schwartz's wording | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | Reinforce desire | "To reinforce your prospect's desire for your product" | "Steinway — The Instrument of the Immortals." (p. 17) |
| b | Sharpen image of fulfillment | "To sharpen his image of the way your product satisfies that desire" | "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in a Rolls Royce is the electric clock." (p. 18) |
| c | Extend image of where/when | "To extend his image of where and when your product satisfies that desire" | "Anywhere you go, Hertz is always nearby." (p. 18) |
| d | New proof / documentation | "To introduce new proof, details, documentation of how well your product satisfies that desire" | "9 out of 10 screen stars use Lux Toilet Soap for their priceless smooth skins." (p. 18) |
| e | New mechanism — does it better | "To announce a new mechanism in that product to enable it to satisfy that desire ev[en] better" | "Hoover's new invention washes floors and vacuums up the scrub water." (p. 19) |
| f | New mechanism — eliminates limitation | "To announce a new mechanism in your product that eliminates former limitations" | "A new Zenith hearing aid — inconspicuous beyond belief." (p. 19) |
| g | Reposition out of the category | "To completely change the image or the mechanism of that product, in order to remove it from the competition of other products claiming to satisfy the same desire." | (Treated in full in Ch. 3, p. 19) |

Body-copy job in all seven cases: "an elaboration of that superiority — including visualization, documentation, mechanization." (p. 17)

## 4. The Psychological-Wall Principle

Each awareness stage is sealed off from the adjacent ones by a wall.

> "Each of these stages is separated from the others by a psychological wall. On one side of that wall is indifference; on the other, intense interest. A headline that will work wonders in the first stage — for example, 'Dial Soap — 9¢ a cake' — will fail completely when addressed to a third-stage market where your prospect doesn't even realize that soaps can be made with built-in deodorants. And a third-stage headline — for example 'Who else wants a whiter wash — with no hard work?' — will be old-hat, no-news to today's housewife, who has been barraged by whiter-than-white advertising for twenty years." (pp. 23–24)
>
> "To sum up, then: a headline which will work to a market in one stage of awareness will not work to a market in another stage of awareness. Nor will it work, even to a market in which it has been successful, once that market passes on to a new stage of awareness." (p. 24)

Two operational consequences:

- **Diagnose before drafting.** A perfect Stage-1 headline shown to a Stage-3 market is invisible. A clever Stage-3 headline shown to a Stage-2 market is "old-hat, no-news."
- **Last year's winner expires.** Markets advance along the scale; when an appeal stops working, the answer is usually to drop one stage further out — not to write the same appeal louder (p. 24).

When a product hits the wall, Schwartz lists the symptoms (p. 24): "When a product begins to slip... when volume falls off, even though advertising budgets are increased... when the name of the product no longer sells as much... when a direct statement of the product's function no longer sells as much... when a direct statement of the desire or the need that the product fulfills no longer sells as much — then that product needs to be reborn, and its problem is the problem of opening up an unaware market."

## 5. The Three Negative Rules for Stage 5

Stage 5 is "first of all a process of elimination" (p. 24). Three things are forbidden in the headline (p. 25):

| # | Rule | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Eliminate price.** | "Price means nothing to a person who does not know your product, or want your product. Therefore, eliminate all mention of price, or price reduction, in your headline or prime display type." (p. 25) |
| 2 | **Eliminate the product name.** | "The name of your product means nothing to a person who has never seen it before, and may actually damage your ad if you have had a bad model the year before, or if it is now associated with the antiquated, the unfashionable, or the unpleasant. Therefore, keep your product out of the headline, and be extremely wary about breaking the mood or disguise of your ad with a prominent logo." (p. 25) |
| 3 | **Eliminate direct statement of function or desire.** | "At this stage of your market, a direct statement of what your product does, what desire it satisfies, or what problem it sokes [solves — OCR], simply will not work... You are not faced here with a problem of sophistication, but one of complete indifference, or unacceptability. Therefore, the performance of your product, and the desire it satisfies, can only be brought in later. You cannot mention them in your headline." (p. 25) |

What remains is the market itself:

> "What you are doing essentially in this fifth stage is calling your market together in the headline of your ad. You are writing an identification headline. You are selling nothing, promising nothing, satisfying nothing. Instead, you are echoing an emotion, an attitude, a [dis]satisfaction that picks people out from the crowd and binds them together in a single statement." (pp. 25–26)
>
> "In this type of headline, you are telling them what they are. You are defining them for themselves. You are giving them the information they need and want, about a problem still so vague that you are the first to put it into words." (p. 26)

The cardinal rule (p. 35): "Your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it. It must be his headline, his problem, his state of mind at that particular moment. It must pick out the product's logical prospects — and reject as many people as it attracts."

The standing warning (p. 35): "It cannot simply be a startler, or an attention-getter, or humorous, or cute. Nor can it mask the fact that it has no headline behind a pretty picture. Most copy writers use a fifth stage problem to write an empty headline, and are therefore simply wasting their client's money."

## 6. The Seven Named Fifth-Stage Tactics

Each tactic is formatted: **What it does → Problem it solved → Headline → Body opener.** All seven appear in pp. 26–34.

### Tactic 1 — Giving Words to a Hidden Dream (pp. 26–27)

**What it does.** Names a hope the prospect has not yet articulated, then casts the product as the response to that named hope.

**Problem.** Expand the market for home correspondence courses beyond what "Earn more money" and "Gain more skill" headlines could capture (p. 26).

**Headline.** "THE UNIVERSITY OF THE NIGHT" (p. 27)

**Body opener.** "The young Lincoln, poring over borrowed school-books far into the night... here is an example which has inspired the man who strives against the odds of circumstances to make his place in the world." (p. 27)

### Tactic 2 — Exploiting a Hidden Fear (pp. 27–28)

**What it does.** Surfaces a dread the prospect has not let himself name, then anchors the product as the escape.

**Problem.** Re-vitalize Postum after health/pep/taste headlines failed and the brand name was slipping (p. 27).

**Headline.** "WHY MEN CRACK..." (p. 28)

**Body opener.** "An authority of international standing recently wrote: 'You have overeaten and plugged your organs with moderate stimulants...'" / "You know them. Strong men, vigorous men, robust men — men who have never had a sick day in their lives. They drive. They drive themselves to the limit. They lash themselves over the limit with stimulants. They crack. Often, they crash." / "Borrowed Energy Must Be Repaid!" (p. 28)

### Tactic 3 — Leading Into an Unacceptable Problem by Starting With a Universally Accepted Image (pp. 28–29)

**What it does.** Takes a problem the prospect (or publisher) would reject if stated directly, opens with an image both find acceptable, and walks the reader inward to the problem.

**Problem.** "To gain both publisher and prospect acceptance for a woman's deodorant. A direct statement of the performance or product would not only offend, but would never be published." (p. 28)

**Headline.** "WITHIN THE CURVE OF A WOMAN'S ARM" (p. 29) — Odorono.

**Body opener.** "A frank discussion of a subject too often avoided. A woman's arm! Poets have sung of its grace; artists have painted its beauty. It should be the daintiest, sweetest thing in the world. And yet, unfortunately, it isn't, always." (p. 29)

### Tactic 4 — Projecting a Hidden Desire That Cannot Be Put Bluntly Into Words (pp. 29–30)

**What it does.** When verbal expression of the desire would be "absurd and offensive," project it through imagery.

**Problem.** "To capitalize on research findings that smoking cigarettes gives men a feeling of virility, importance, sexual strength. Any verbal expression of these themes, however, would be instantly rejected as absurd and offensive." (p. 29)

**Solution.** "The MARLBORO TATTOO AD: With its virile men (cowboys, racing car drivers, sky divers, etc.) whose appearance alone projected more of an image of raw virility than any number of words could ever convey." (pp. 29–30)

### Tactic 5 — Using a Common Resentment or Unvoiced Protest (pp. 30–31)

**What it does.** Broadens the market by tapping a shared grievance instead of asking a narrower DIY-capable group to act.

**Problem.** Sell a do-it-yourself TV-repair book. The direct headline "Save up to $100 a year on your TV repairs!" only catches handymen. Broaden by exploiting "the existing resentment against TV service contracts" (p. 30).

**Headline.** "WHY HAVEN'T TV OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS" (p. 30)

**Body opener.** "Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947? Then here is the full, uncensored story of how you can avoid those $15-$20 repair bills... How many times this week have you had to get up to fix a jumpy TV picture?... 90% of These Breakdowns Are Unnecessary!" (p. 30) Followed by a "Suppose you had a TV expert at your elbow 24 hours a day" sequence (p. 31).

### Tactic 6 — Projecting an Ultimate Triumph the Prospect Will Identify With (pp. 32–33)

**What it does.** Skips the direct benefit and stages a cinematic triumph the prospect projects himself into.

**Problem.** Sell music lessons by correspondence to a wider audience than would respond to "Play Real Tunes on the Piano in Five Days." (p. 32)

**Headline.** "THEY LAUGHED WHEN I SAT DOWN AT THE PIANO. BUT WHEN I STARTED TO PLAY!—" (p. 32)

**Body opener.** "Arthur had just played 'The Rosary.' The room rang with applause... 'Can he really play?' I heard a girl whisper to Arthur. 'Heavens, no!' Arthur exclaimed... Then I Started to Play. Instantly a tense silence fell on the guests. The laughter died on their lips as if by magic..." (pp. 32–33). Reveal: U.S. School of Music correspondence.

### Tactic 7 — Projecting the Result of a Problem Identified With by People Who Would Reject the Direct Statement (pp. 33–34)

**What it does.** Dramatizes the social *consequence* of an unflattering problem so the reader self-identifies via the result, not the cause.

**Problem.** Increase mouthwash sales on the social-offense theme — too insulting to be taken straight (p. 33).

**Headline.** "OFTEN A BRIDESMAID BUT NEVER A BRIDE" (p. 33) — Listerine.

**Body opener.** "Edna's case was really a pathetic one. Like every woman, her primary ambition was to marry... no one possessed more charm or grace or loveliness than she. And as her birthdays crept gradually toward that tragic thirty-mark, marriage seemed farther from her life than ever. She was often a bridesmaid but never a bride." (p. 33). Reveal: halitosis → Listerine. (p. 34)

## 7. The List Never Ends

Schwartz closes the fifth-stage section with two warnings about durability (pp. 34–35):

> "Every day new solutions, new patterns are being created. Wherever the direct appeal fails, or loses its power, you should begin to explore a fifth stage headline." (p. 34)
>
> "And, if it is an effective headline, and it works, then it too will become outdated as your market moves on to a new stage of awareness... You never step in the same river twice. No market ever stands still." (p. 35)

---

## 8. A Final Word on Style — Style Ages, Strategy Doesn't (pp. 35–36)

> "Markets change; desires change; fashions change. And so do the acceptable styles of advertisements change. Certain advertising styles — the form your advertising message takes — grow tired with time — then stale — then actually laughable." (p. 35)
>
> "At the turn of the century, effective ads were written in verse; twenty years later, no one would believe them. In the 1920s most of the great ads were narrative stories — either first-person confessions, or third person revelations, or comic strips dramatizations. Today everything but the comic strip is gone..." (p. 35)
>
> "When a new style is born, people believe it, and it reinforces the message it is carrying. When that same style grows trite, people cannot see the message for the advertisement." (pp. 35–36)
>
> "In effective advertising, though styles may change, strategy does not. If you will study the piano and bridesmaid ads in this chapter, you will notice this: That while the narrative style of both is now old-hat, you can still respond to their power. Both tap desires that still exist — though now perhaps directed toward different products and different problems. And both evoke those desires, and channel those desires, so effectively, that if they were rewritten in today's idiom, and applied to different products, they still might sell millions of dollars worth of goods today." (p. 36)

| Layer | Examples | Lifespan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Style** (the form) | Verse, first-person confession, third-person revelation, comic-strip dramatization | Ages — stale, then laughable (p. 35) |
| **Strategy** (the structure) | The five awareness states, the seven Stage-2 tasks, the three Stage-5 negative rules, the seven named fifth-stage tactics | Durable — re-skin in the current idiom (p. 36) |

The operational instruction: keep the strategy intact (the awareness diagnosis and the underlying tactic) and re-clothe it in the current idiom of the medium.

## 9. Quick-Reference Insights

- Awareness, not cleverness, sets the headline (p. 15).
- The headline does not sell — it buys the next sentence (pp. 14–15).
- A psychological wall separates each stage; a winner in one stage fails in the next (pp. 23–24).
- As awareness drops, creative wages rise (Stage 1 phrase-makers, p. 16; Stage 5 "domain of the idea man," p. 20).
- Stage 2 has exactly seven jobs (p. 17).
- Stage 3 = crystallize the amorphous desire in one statement (p. 20).
- Stage 5 forbids price, product name, and direct statement of function or desire (p. 25).
- The fifth-stage headline never sells the product — it sells the rest of the ad (p. 26).
- The prospect must identify with the headline before he can buy from it (p. 35).
- "You never step in the same river twice. No market ever stands still." (p. 35)
- Style ages, strategy doesn't (p. 36).
