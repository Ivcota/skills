# Intensification (Technique #1) — deep dive

> Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Ch. 7 (book pp. 77–106).

## Core principle

Intensification is Schwartz's First Mechanism of Persuasion: the act of taking a desire that already exists in the prospect's mind and making it sharper, more vivid, more concrete, and more emotionally weighted by presenting a continuous series of fresh fulfillment-images. Desire is the engine: "The force that creates sales, that powers our present economy, is desire" (p. 77). The problem is that desires don't show up to the ad already crystallized — they arrive as "indistinct... blurs — hazy, ambiguous, not yet crystallized into words or images... they have only a fraction of their true potential power" (pp. 77–78). The copywriter's first job is to fill out those vague desires with concrete images, and "to multiply their strength by the number of satisfactions that you can suggest to achieve them" (p. 78).

The role of the copywriter, in Schwartz's framing, is unmistakable: "You are literally the script writer for your prospect's dreams. You are the chronicler of his future. Your job is to show him in minute detail all the tomorrows that your product makes possible for him" (p. 78).

Crucially, Intensification is not repetition. The rule, stated as a hinge for the entire chapter: "Once you have presented your basic fulfillment in a certain way, then you must vary your viewpoint in your second description, or not present it again. Otherwise you will lose your reader in the middle of your ad" (p. 79). Or more compactly: **you cannot repeat — you can only reinforce**. Each new viewpoint must be a fresh perspective on the same dominant promise so that "with every additional new and different way that you can present that idea, it becomes sharper and more real in your prospect's mind, and it builds up more and more emotional weight" (p. 79).

The chapter's running thesis ties sharpness directly to price-elasticity: "The sharper you can draw your pictures — and the greater the number of them that you can legitimately present — the more your prospect will demand your product, and the less important will seem your price" (p. 78).

## The Thirteen Ways to Strengthen Desire

Schwartz lists thirteen devices on p. 77, then walks each in turn from p. 80 through p. 100.

### 1. Your First Presentation of Your Claims (p. 80)

**Mechanism.** Open with the product or its satisfaction directly and bluntly, via "a thorough, completely detailed description of its appearance or the results it gives" (p. 80). Pile on sensory specifics — counts, dimensions, colors, sounds — until the prospect can almost see and touch the result. The first presentation is the seed image; everything after only reinforces it.

**Verbatim example.** A rose-plant ad headlined *"MORE ROSES THAN YOU EVER SAW ON ANY ROSE BUSH..."* lists "500 blooms in June... 1523 more blooms in July... 1616 more blooms in August... 437 more blooms in September... 4,076 roses all from one single plant" (p. 80). Companion: a Pan American plastic model jet whose "tail section alone stands six full inches from the table" with a built-in "JET SOUND SIMULATOR" (p. 83).

**Copy pattern.** "[Product] gives [Avatar] more [Outcome] than you ever saw — [count 1] in [period 1]... [count 2] in [period 2]... [count 3] in [period 3] — [grand total] [Outcome] from one single [Mechanism]."

### 2. Put the Claims in Action (p. 83)

**Mechanism.** After the static description, move the product. "PUT THE PRODUCT IN ACTION for your reader. To show, not only how the product looks, and what benefits it gives the reader, but exactly how it does this" (p. 83). Action copy keeps adding verbs (lift, pour, cast, lower, swim, spring, buzz) so the product becomes a moving thing in the prospect's head.

**Verbatim example.** Self-propelled fish lure: the lure floats, then water reaches the fuel charge and "the lure will seem to shudder for a second — and then it will spring dramatically to 'life'... filled with the buzzing sound of a dying bee... like a maimed minnow, it will swim noisily downward — buzzing and humming — traveling about nine feet every fifteen seconds" (pp. 84–85). Companion: the Pan Am model jet — "Pick this magnificent plastic model up from the table, and hand it to your child... Before your astonished eyes, you will see one of the most thrilling sights of your entire life!" (p. 83).

**Copy pattern.** "[Avatar] [verb 1] the [Product]. Within [short interval], [Mechanism] activates — and [Product] will [vivid verb] — [sensory detail] — until [Outcome] in [measured distance/time]."

### 3. Bring In the Reader (p. 85)

**Mechanism.** Put the prospect inside the product-in-action story. "If your product lends itself to this kind of treatment, put your reader right smack in the middle of this product-in-action story, and give him a verbal demonstration of [what] will happen to him the first day he owns that product" (p. 85). Done in second person, future-perfect, anchored in a familiar moment.

**Verbatim example.** Power-booster ad: "Picture this to yourself! Next weekend you go down to your car... when you turn on the ignition, a modern miracle of engineering science comes to life under your hood!... pull up to another car at the stop light... slam your foot down on the gas pedal!... you will leave him a full block behind... you will look in your rear view mirror and see the startled look of amazement on the other driver's face!" (pp. 85–86).

**Copy pattern.** "Picture this to yourself! [Time anchor], [Avatar] [familiar action with familiar object]. You've done nothing but [single change with Product]. But now, when you [trigger], [Mechanism] — and you will [Outcome] while [witness] [reaction]."

### 4. Show Him How to Test Your Claims (p. 86)

**Mechanism.** Turn the demonstration into a test the reader can run. "Turn the demonstration into a test. Let your reader visualize himself proving the performance of your product — gaining its benefits immediately — in the most specific and dramatic way possible" (p. 86). The test must be specific (steps, instruments, numbers) so that the prospect mentally rehearses both the proof procedure and the reward.

**Verbatim example.** SA-FIRE INJECTORS walks the reader through measuring crawl-speed, RPM, and low-gear speed with motor idling before and after install, ending: "no matter what you drive, here is absolute proof that you can go further, faster and cheaper..." (p. 86). Memory-course companion: "Give Me One Evening and I'll Give You a Push-Button Memory" — read 8 pages, then sit down and write 20 facts you could never memorize before, then amaze friends and family the next morning (p. 87).

**Copy pattern.** "When [Avatar] gets [Product], here is all you do. First, note [baseline metric] of your current [thing]. Next, [install Mechanism]. Now, re-measure [metric]. No matter what you [own/do], here is absolute proof that [Outcome]."

### 5. Stretch Out Your Benefits in Time (p. 87)

**Mechanism.** Show the product working not for an hour or a day "but over a span of weeks and months. Here you extend your reader's vision further and further into time — showing him a continuous flow of benefits" (p. 88). Sequence in chronological gates ("the very first month"... "within one or two short weeks"... "and then, the most remarkable part of all!") so the reader experiences a moving forward narrative rather than a single snapshot.

**Verbatim example.** English plant-food pellets walk through three sequential stages: a "tremendous new burst of growth" the first month, a second wonder-working nutrient "within one or two short weeks," then a third ingredient that releases when plants reach full height, climaxing in "AND YOU WILL BE BLINDED BY THE EXPLOSION OF COLOR THAT GREETS YOU IN YOUR GARDEN!" and "A FEELING OF GARDEN ACCOMPLISHMENT AND PRIDE THAT YOU NEVER DREAMED OF BEFORE!" (p. 88).

**Copy pattern.** "First, [Avatar] sees [Stage-1 Outcome] in [Time 1]. But this is just the beginning! Within [Time 2] — without your even [effort] — [Mechanism] AUTOMATICALLY begins the second stage of its work, delivering [Stage-2 Outcome]. And then, the most remarkable part of all — [Stage-3 Outcome]."

### 6. Bring In an Audience (p. 89)

**Mechanism.** Add other actors. "Each one of them — each group of them — provides a fresh new perspective through which your reader can view the product. Seen through their eyes — experienced through their actions and reactions — the product performances become new, vivid and completely different again" (p. 89). Three sub-flavors: (1) celebrities via testimonial, (2) average men/women whom the reader identifies with via narration or case history, and (3) stacks of case histories used as social proof at scale.

**Verbatim example.** Reducing ad case-history stack: readers learn how others "lost as much as 17 and 25 and even 34 pounds... had turned back their weight clock 5, 10 and even 15 years" (p. 90). The canonical narration cue is "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano" (p. 89).

**Copy pattern.** "[Audience member name/type] [previous state]. Then [he/she] [tried Mechanism]. Today, [Outcome with measurable number]. And [Avatar] is just one of [N] who [Outcome]."

### 7. Show Experts Approving (p. 90)

**Mechanism.** "Experts in the field — professionals — the sophisticated, the discriminating, the blasé can be called on to register their reactions. There is nothing so astounding as the astonishment of experts. Here the elements of surprise, competition and discovery all combine to sharpen the image even more" (p. 90). The form: stage a contest in which experts gather expecting one outcome, then your product overturns it. Strong superlatives go into a third-party expert mouth instead of into the brand's own.

**Verbatim example.** Nearly Wild rose ad stages a college horticulturist test against "all the highly-praised queens of rosedom" with a famous rose expert exclaiming, "Why, it's like having a complete rose garden of 20 plants — all on a single bush!" and another saying, "If we ever released these flowering figures to the public, they'd never believe it" (pp. 90–91).

**Copy pattern.** "[Expert title] gathered at [credible venue] to test [Product] against [list of established competitors]. When [Mechanism] produced [Outcome], [Expert name] said, '[verbatim astonishment quote].'"

### 8. Compare, Contrast, Prove Superiority (p. 91)

**Mechanism.** "The competition can be carried into contrast. The disadvantages of the old product or service can be laid side by side with the advantages of the new — throwing these advantages into sharp relief" (p. 91). Two structural patterns: side-by-side price-anchors and parallel before-after lists.

**Verbatim example.** Dollar Book Club radio: "While your friends were paying $3.95 for their copies of Frank Yerby's run-away best seller, The Golden Hawk, Dollar Book Club members were getting the same exact book for only 99¢..." (p. 91). Fuel Reclaimer: "polluted oil, polluted engine, polluted air" vs. "young-car power, young-car economy, young-car resistance to repair bills" (p. 92).

**Copy pattern.** "While [comparison group] were paying [price A] / suffering [old state A] / [old state B] / [old state C], [Avatar] using [Mechanism] got [Outcome A] / [Outcome B] / [Outcome C] for only [price B]."

### 9. Picture the Black Side, Too (p. 92)

**Mechanism.** "There's no need to neglect the Heaven-Or-Hell approach. Here the negative aspect to every promise — the problem that you are liberating your prospect from forever — is painted in all its full black color. You irritate the wound, and then you apply the salve that heals it" (p. 92). "Thus you derive two currents of motivation — repulsion away from the former problem or inadequate product, and the attraction generated by your own product's contrasting solutions" (p. 92).

**Verbatim example.** Spark-plug ad: "lift one of the old-fashioned spark plugs out of your car!... what you'll see on the bottom of that plug is FILTH! The Firing Point... will be choked, strangled and fouled with BLACK, FILTHY CARBON! Carbon that robs your car of as much as 20 vital horsepower... CORRODED — PITTED — SCARRED — AND WORN!" — pivoting to "AT LAST! A PLUG SO SMART THAT IT THINKS!" (pp. 92–93).

**Copy pattern.** "Look at [Avatar]'s current [thing]. What you'll see is [BLACK, FILTHY, DRAMATIC NOUN] — [destructive consequences in caps]. Now look at the new [Product]: [breakthrough headline]. Here is a [Product] that not only [restores baseline] — but [delivers Outcome]."

### 10. Show How Easy It Is To Get These Benefits (p. 94)

**Mechanism.** Apply the Intensification frame to every contact point — "price, availability, ease of use, durability, portability, replacement and maintenance, even unwrapping the carton it comes in — it furnishes you with another fresh perspective in which to reiterate and reemphasize its benefits" (p. 94). Tactical move: repeat the same friction-reducing phrase at the head of each benefit paragraph so that ease itself becomes a refrain.

**Verbatim example.** Engine-additive ad: "lift up its hood... pour its contents into the oil filler pipe of your car!... that's all the work you will do! That's all the skill you need! And yet in that 30 seconds work, you will improve that car's performance in eight different ways!" — repeating "with just 30 seconds work" three times across mileage, lubrication, and engine-life claims (p. 94).

**Copy pattern.** "With just [trivial-time/effort], [Avatar] will [Outcome 1]... With just [trivial-time/effort], [Avatar] will [Outcome 2]... With just [trivial-time/effort], [Avatar] will [Outcome 3]."

### 11. Use Metaphor, Analogy, Imagination (p. 94)

**Mechanism.** "Nor do you have to be satisfied merely with the statement of [bare] fact. There are infinite opportunities for the use of imagination to present those facts in more dramatic form, outside of the rigidly realistic approach" (p. 94). The technique: take an inanimate product and give it a human role, or an abstract benefit and give it a vivid analog.

**Verbatim example.** Sherwin Cody English Course personifies its grammar manual: "Suppose he himself were standing forever at your elbow. Every time you mispronounced or misspelled a word... suppose you could hear him whisper, 'That is wrong, it should be thus and so'... 100% Self-Correcting Device" (pp. 94–95). Companion canon: "The Glory of the Upward Path" (Alexander Hamilton, p. 94) and Big Ben "First he whispers, then he shouts" (p. 94).

**Copy pattern.** "Suppose [Product] himself were standing forever at [Avatar]'s elbow. Every time [Avatar] [Pain action], suppose [he/she] could hear [Product] whisper, '[Correction line tied to Outcome].' That's [Mechanism] — [memorable nickname]."

### 12. Before You're Done, Summarize (p. 95)

**Mechanism.** "There is an infinite number of new approaches. No list of them can ever be complete... As long as each additional fresh perspective continues to build the dominant desire in your prospect's mind, use it. But if the additional perspective is not different or dramatic enough to renew your prospect's interest in your claims, then leave it out" (pp. 95–96). The summary devices come in two shapes:

- **Horizontal catalog** — expands desire across applications: "ALL IN ONE! Blow-Torch, Paint-Remover, Soldering Iron... Does 101 jobs around the house" (p. 96).
- **Vertical catalog** — deepens the dominant desire: the memory-book "over 50 MEMORY INTENSIFIERS" sequence (pp. 97–98).

The unifying metaphor: the body of the ad has been a "high-velocity rifle" hitting one dominant desire; the catalog at the end "shotguns" with every wide-angled appeal in case the rifle missed. "Your ad has taken a single dominant desire, and developed that single desire to the point of absolute maximum intensity. This willingness to gamble on being precisely right... gives your ad the impact of a high-velocity rifle. But it also demands the accuracy of an expert marksman. Now — through this multi-appeal catalog listing — you have a last-minute chance to hedge" (p. 97).

**Copy pattern.** "[Product] does [N] jobs for [Avatar]: [job 1], [job 2], [job 3]... [job N] — every one of them tied to [dominant Outcome via Mechanism]."

### 13. Put Your Guarantee to Work (p. 99)

**Mechanism.** "As you close the sale, as you ask the prospect for action, as you state the terms of your guarantee, you can turn that guarantee into the climax of your ad — the last brief summary of your product's performances — reinforced at every step by the positive reassertion of that guarantee" (p. 99). Two formats: numbered guarantees pairing each performance promise with a refund clause, and guarantee-driven application stacks where each USE prompt resolves into a money-back hook.

**Verbatim example.** Power Flash spark plugs: "Guaranteed For Two Full Years!... 1. These plugs must give you up to 9 miles more per gallon instantly — or your full purchase price back! 2. These plugs must give you up to 31 more horsepower instantly — or your full purchase price back! 3. As an extra added assurance — these plugs must continue to give you this power, performance and gas savings FOR TWO FULL YEARS — or we will send you a brand-new set ABSOLUTELY FREE!" (pp. 99–100). Magic English Pellets companion stacks "USE THEM TO CREATE SUPER-FLOWERS!... USE THEM TO CREATE SUPER-VEGETABLES!... YES! USE THEM IN YOUR ENTIRE GARDEN!" each tied to a money-back clause (p. 100).

**Copy pattern.** "Guaranteed For [Period]! 1. [Product] must give [Avatar] [specific metric 1] — or your full purchase price back! 2. [Product] must give [Avatar] [specific metric 2] — or your full purchase price back! 3. As an extra added assurance — [Product] must continue to deliver [Outcome] for [Period] — or we will send you a brand-new [unit] ABSOLUTELY FREE!"

## How to Apply Intensification to a Campaign (pp. 101–106)

Schwartz acknowledges that all the chapter's examples come from mail order because mail order "compresses such a campaign into a single ad. It says all it can, everything it can think of, in this one ad" (p. 101) — making the Intensification devices visible in concentrated form. National advertising changes the rules: products live longer than two years, ads run far more frequently, and "national advertising, by its frequent insertions, soon loses its immediate novelty. The creative problem in national advertising thus changes from finding the theme for a particular advertisement to finding the theme for a series of advertisements. And the problem of Intensification shifts from building desire throughout the advertisement to building desire throughout the series" (p. 102).

The two-fold creative problem (p. 102):

1. Compress the dominant image into a single statement or picture so powerful it sells the first time and so true to market that it keeps selling on repeat.
2. "Present a series of variations or perspectives of that central image — each emerging from your dominant idea, but each so different from the rest that they impel your prospect to read through them, and so fresh that they make that dominant idea seem new again" (p. 102).

Schwartz's headline rule: **"Thus you have your Central Campaign Idea, and its continuous restatement in a series of fresh disguises"** (p. 102).

### Four canonical campaigns

- **Colgate Gardol (late 1950s) — Rule and Example.** Central idea openly stated and ritualistically repeated: "Gardol — the Invisible Shield that Protects Your Teeth" (p. 103). Variation lives only in the opening 10-second TV demo (a baseball thrown at the announcer, bouncing off the shield), with the closing line reciting verbatim: "Just as this invisible shield... protects me against this baseball, so the invisible shield in Colgate toothpaste protects your teeth against decay" (p. 103). The verbal rule is precisely repeated; the illustrations rotate.

- **Toni / Clairol — fixed headline + rotating illustrations.** Toni: "WHICH TWIN HAS THE TONI — and which has the $15 permanent?" anchoring a continuously-changing stream of pretty-girl photos (p. 104). Clairol: "DOES SHE OR DOESN'T SHE? Hair-coloring so natural only her hairdresser knows for sure" (p. 104), re-invigorated by a series of glamorized everyday-life main illustrations.

- **Cadillac — quality theme carried by typography, photography, fashion.** The focal point is brand quality, "symbolized in every Cadillac advertisement in the identification background photography, in the typography, in the copy style, in the fashions and jewelry that are chosen to appear with the car... even the highly-stylized pen-and-ink drawings that are used when the campaign shifts to newspapers" (p. 104). The dominant image lives in the campaign as a whole, not in any single ad.

- **Volkswagen — product as focal point.** "The advertisements are reduced to severe simplicity. There is no background identification whatsoever. Different perspectives of the product are shown as close-up as possible (except in the 'Think Small' advertisement) against an absolutely bare background" (p. 105). Headlines: "THINK SMALL" (compactness as asset), "Lemon" (microscopic flaw disqualifying a unit), "Our new model" (engineering changes you can't see), "Why you should open the window before you close the door of a Volkswagen" (airtight cabin), "Our number one salesman" (the serviceman) (pp. 105–106). Each headline (a) hits the prospect's dominant desire (more value for car money), (b) expresses the campaign's common theme (this is an honest car), and (c) compels readership by setting up an apparent contradiction or unusual request that body copy must resolve (p. 106).

**The Creative Gamble (p. 106).** In a VW-style campaign the headline does not mention product, trademark, or performance — "the copywriter is taking the position that he can only sell adequately with the number of words allowed him by the body copy. Therefore he is willing to risk his headline in grasping the attention of his prospect and impelling him to read on, into the sales message itself, presented in convincing detail below. If he fails in his gamble, he has wasted his page. If he succeeds, he has doubled and tripled the effectiveness of his ad" (p. 106).

## Common pitfalls

- **Repeating instead of reinforcing.** "Once you have presented your basic fulfillment in a certain way, then you must vary your viewpoint in your second description, or not present it again. Otherwise you will lose your reader in the middle of your ad" (p. 79). Fix: change perspective every section, never restate the same image with different words.
- **Adding perspectives that aren't different enough.** "If the additional perspective is not different or dramatic enough to renew your prospect's interest in your claims, then leave it out" (p. 96). Fix: re-read the ad days later — "perhaps the best way to measure the balance between the two — that critical turning point where reinforcement becomes mere repetition — is to re-read your ad, some days after you have first written it" (p. 96).
- **Picking the wrong dominant desire.** The rifle metaphor: a single dominant desire developed to maximum intensity "demands the accuracy of an expert marksman" (p. 97). Mistake: assuming you picked correctly. Fix: hedge with a closing catalog (shotgun) so secondary appeals can still close the sale.
- **Treating the guarantee as a footnote.** Schwartz makes the guarantee carry a re-intensification of every key benefit at the close (p. 99). Fix: number each performance promise inside the guarantee so each clause re-states a benefit on the way out.
- **Letting the central image burn out in national ads.** "National advertising, by its frequent insertions, soon loses its immediate novelty" (p. 102). Fix: lock the dominant image; vary everything around it — illustrations, contradictions, tone — so the campaign achieves "novelty within continuity" (p. 106).
- **Refusing the Creative Gamble in long-copy campaigns.** Stuffing the headline with product, trademark, and performance "just to be safe" gives back the doubled/tripled effectiveness Schwartz attributes to a contradiction-driven headline (p. 106). Fix: only gamble when the body copy can carry the close.

## Ethical posture

Schwartz's only explicit guardrail in Chapter 7 is one word — but it is load-bearing: **"the greater the number of [pictures] that you can *legitimately* present"** (p. 78, emphasis added). Legitimacy of the picture is a precondition for intensifying it; sharpening a fictitious fulfillment is not on the menu.

The implicit guarantee contract reinforces this. Schwartz's guarantee structure pairs each performance promise with its own refundable clause ("9 miles more per gallon instantly — or your full purchase price back!" p. 99). Every metric named in the guarantee must be one the product can actually deliver, because each is independently refundable. The guarantee is the honesty enforcement mechanism: claim only what you will pay back if it fails to materialize.

Two further constraints follow from the chapter's own metaphors:

- The shotgun catalog "demands the accuracy of an expert marksman" (p. 97). Intensifying a desire the prospect doesn't actually hold wastes the rifle shot — the ad fails on its own terms.
- The Heaven-or-Hell pivot only works if the salve actually heals the wound (p. 92). Irritating a wound your product cannot close is, in Schwartz's frame, a structural failure as well as an ethical one.

Chapter 7 does not contain explicit warnings against fabricated expert testimony, fake case histories, or unsupported black-side claims; those guardrails sit elsewhere in the book. For Intensification, the rule is: **legitimate desire, legitimate picture, legitimate guarantee** — and let every fresh perspective sharpen something that's actually there.

## Copy patterns at a glance

A consolidated index of the thirteen patterns above, plus the campaign-level patterns from pp. 101–106. Each is parameterised in `[Avatar] [Outcome] [Mechanism]` form so it can be slotted into a working draft.

1. **Sensory-stack opener (p. 80)** — "[Product] gives [Avatar] more [Outcome] than you ever saw — [count 1] in [period 1]... [count 2] in [period 2]... [count 3] in [period 3] — [grand total] [Outcome] from one single [Mechanism]."
2. **Product-in-action verbs (p. 83)** — "[Avatar] [verb 1] the [Product]. Within [short interval], [Mechanism] activates — and [Product] will [vivid verb] — [sensory detail] — until [Outcome] in [measured distance/time]."
3. **Picture-to-yourself second person (p. 85)** — "Picture this to yourself! [Time anchor], [Avatar] [familiar action with familiar object]. You've done nothing but [single change with Product]. But now, when you [trigger], [Mechanism] — and you will [Outcome] while [witness] [reaction]."
4. **Test-the-claim stepwise script (p. 86)** — "When [Avatar] gets [Product], here is all you do. First, note [baseline metric] of your current [thing]. Next, [install Mechanism]. Now, re-measure [metric]. No matter what you [own/do], here is absolute proof that [Outcome]."
5. **Stretched-time stages (pp. 87–88)** — "First, [Avatar] sees [Stage-1 Outcome] in [Time 1]. But this is just the beginning! Within [Time 2] — without your even [effort] — [Mechanism] AUTOMATICALLY begins the second stage of its work, delivering [Stage-2 Outcome]. And then, the most remarkable part of all — [Stage-3 Outcome]."
6. **Audience case-history stack (pp. 89–90)** — "[Audience member name/type] [previous state]. Then [he/she] [tried Mechanism]. Today, [Outcome with measurable number]. And [Avatar] is just one of [N] who [Outcome]."
7. **Astonished-expert contest (pp. 90–91)** — "[Expert title] gathered at [credible venue] to test [Product] against [list of established competitors]. When [Mechanism] produced [Outcome], [Expert name] said, '[verbatim astonishment quote].'"
8. **Side-by-side contrast (pp. 91–92)** — "While [comparison group] were paying [price A] / suffering [old state A] / [old state B] / [old state C], [Avatar] using [Mechanism] got [Outcome A] / [Outcome B] / [Outcome C] for only [price B]."
9. **Black-side / heaven-or-hell pivot (pp. 92–93)** — "Look at [Avatar]'s current [thing]. What you'll see is [BLACK, FILTHY, DRAMATIC NOUN] — [destructive consequences in caps]. Now look at the new [Product]: [breakthrough headline]. Here is a [Product] that not only [restores baseline] — but [delivers Outcome]."
10. **Ease refrain (p. 94)** — "With just [trivial-time/effort], [Avatar] will [Outcome 1]... With just [trivial-time/effort], [Avatar] will [Outcome 2]... With just [trivial-time/effort], [Avatar] will [Outcome 3]."
11. **Personified-product metaphor (pp. 94–95)** — "Suppose [Product] himself were standing forever at [Avatar]'s elbow. Every time [Avatar] [Pain action], suppose [he/she] could hear [Product] whisper, '[Correction line tied to Outcome].' That's [Mechanism] — [memorable nickname]."
12. **Catalog summary (pp. 96–98)** — "[Product] does [N] jobs for [Avatar]: [job 1], [job 2], [job 3]... [job N] — every one of them tied to [dominant Outcome via Mechanism]."
13. **Numbered guarantee climax (pp. 99–100)** — "Guaranteed For [Period]! 1. [Product] must give [Avatar] [specific metric 1] — or your full purchase price back! 2. [Product] must give [Avatar] [specific metric 2] — or your full purchase price back! 3. As an extra added assurance — [Product] must continue to deliver [Outcome] for [Period] — or we will send you a brand-new [unit] ABSOLUTELY FREE!"
14. **Rule-and-Example campaign anchor (p. 103)** — Fixed verbal rule ("[Mechanism] — the [metaphor] that [Outcome] for [Avatar]") + rotating opening demonstrations every insertion.
15. **Creative-Gamble headline (pp. 105–106)** — Drop product, trademark, performance from the headline; lead with a contradiction or unusual request that forces the prospect into the body copy where the close lives.

## Key insights

A handful of Schwartz's lines from Chapter 7 are worth holding alongside the thirteen devices because they govern when and how to apply them.

- **Desire is raw material; intensification is the process.** "These desires, as they exist in the mind of your prospect today, are indistinct... blurs — hazy, ambiguous, not yet crystallized into words or images... they have only a fraction of their true potential power" (pp. 77–78). The copywriter's job is to crystallize them, not invent them.
- **You cannot repeat — you can only reinforce.** "Once you have presented your basic fulfillment in a certain way, then you must vary your viewpoint in your second description, or not present it again. Otherwise you will lose your reader in the middle of your ad" (p. 79). Reinforcement = same promise, fresh viewpoint.
- **Sharper images = price disappears.** "The sharper you can draw your pictures — and the greater the number of them that you can legitimately present — the more your prospect will demand your product, and the less important will seem your price" (p. 78). Intensification is, among other things, a price-elasticity tool.
- **The copywriter is the script writer for the prospect's dreams.** "You are literally the script writer for your prospect's dreams. You are the chronicler of his future. Your job is to show him in minute detail all the tomorrows that your product makes possible for him" (p. 78). Copy writes the prospect's future in the second person.
- **Heaven-or-Hell creates two currents of motivation.** "Repulsion away from the former problem or inadequate product, and the attraction generated by your own product's contrasting solutions" (p. 92). Use both currents — irritate the wound, then apply the salve.
- **The catalog is a hedge for the rifle shot.** A single dominant desire developed to maximum intensity is the rifle; the closing catalog is the shotgun that hedges in case you picked the wrong dominant appeal (p. 97).
- **The guarantee is the climactic summary, not a footnote.** Every guarantee clause should re-state a benefit; the structure pairs claim → "or your money back" so the guarantee re-intensifies desire on the way out (pp. 99–100).
- **Re-read your ad days later to spot the line where reinforcement becomes repetition.** "Perhaps the best way to measure the balance between the two — that critical turning point where reinforcement becomes mere repetition — is to re-read your ad, some days after you have first written it" (p. 96).
- **Mail order forces all 13 devices into one ad; campaigns spread them across a series.** "Mail order as a rule contains the greatest number of these Intensification devices in a single ad" (p. 101); national campaigns shift Intensification from "throughout the advertisement to throughout the series" (p. 102).
- **The Creative Gamble: skip product/trademark in the headline if you trust your body copy.** VW-class campaigns risk the page on a headline that grasps attention via contradiction, then close in body copy. Fail = wasted page. Succeed = "doubled and tripled the effectiveness of his ad" (p. 106).
