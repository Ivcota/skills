# Camouflage (Technique #7) — deep dive

> Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Ch. 13 (book pp. 185–194).

## Core principle

Camouflage is borrowing believability "from all the places in our society where it is stored up" (p. 185) — chiefly the publications, networks, and editorial channels prospects already trust. The mechanism is a conditioned reflex:

> "after a while [its] phraseology begins to carry an aura of truth all by itself no matter what material it embraces" (p. 186).

A reader trusts a publication because he has bought it repeatedly; that trust attaches to the publication's *form* — its layout, its typography, its idiom. When you mimic that form, the ad inherits the trust as "stored believability… a believability reflex" (p. 186).

Schwartz frames three borrowing strategies — Adopt Format, Adopt Phraseology, and Mood Adaptations (Understatement and Deadly Sincerity). The first two borrow from the host medium; the last two break out of "advertising language" into the cadence of report or confession.

## Strategy 1 — Adopt Format (pp. 186–190)

Two objectives govern format work (pp. 186–187):

1. Allow the reader to enter the ad with the least possible mental shifting of gears from "editorial" to "advertisement."
2. Carry along the greatest possible amount of believability through every sentence of the ad.

Schwartz's general anti-layout-fetishism caveat applies — but layout matters here:

> "I have not discussed layout in this book, because I do not believe layout is nearly as effective as copy in determining the results of your ad. Here, however, layout is important. A single change in format can add 50% to your readership, and your results" (p. 187).

The ideal version of the technique:

> "Your job here, once again, is to approximate as closely as possible the format of the medium in which you are advertising. This means, ideally, letting *them* set your ad… using their kind of headline-to-body-copy transition… using their illustrations, their sub heads, their break-up of space" (p. 187).

### Worked case — "How to Make Anybody Like You!" / Wall Street Journal adaptation (pp. 187–190)

A house-set magazine version was "mildly successful." The same ad re-set feature-for-feature in *Wall Street Journal* style ran nineteen times monthly with no drop-off in pull. The five adaptations Schwartz lists (pp. 187–190):

1. **Headline in journal type, upper-and-lower case** — indistinguishable from editorial headlines. Does NOT signal "this is an ad: beware!"
2. **Two stacked sub-headlines** — one directly after the other. Schwartz calls this "Very Nineteenth Century" — a convention abandoned by 99% of newspapers, which the *Journal* had kept.
3. **Two bars directly above and below the first sub-head** — "small touches that define individuality."
4. **Sub-heads placed at the extreme left of the column** — matching the *Journal*'s editorial column layout.
5. **Line drawing of the author** rather than a photograph — uglier on its own merits, but matches the *Journal*'s editorial convention.

> "What has been done by these adaptations, is that a corny, old-fashioned, rather ugly advertisement has been developed for this specific medium — which has about twice the believability, twice the pulling power, and twice the staying power of the same exact copy and illustration presented the mass-produced way" (p. 190).

The principle: pretty-on-the-newsstand and pulls-on-the-page are different jobs. The five adaptations work because each one is a feature *the reader has already learned to trust*.

## Strategy 2 — Adopt Phraseology (pp. 190–191)

> "The second — slightly less specific — is adopting phraseology. This method stems from the fact that certain media, or classes of media, use certain stereotyped phrases over and over again, which after a while take on a believability of their own" (p. 190).

### Newspapers — news-indicators (p. 191)

In newspapers there are news-indicators: an issue date, a city of origin, perhaps a by-line. Each one is picked up by the copywriter to add believability.

**Skin specialist demonstration ad (p. 191):**

> SKIN SPECIALIST DEMONSTRATES
> HOW TO RINSE AWAY YOUR BLACKHEADS
> By Claire Hoffman
> New York, N.Y. — A leading doctor today showed an audience of men, women and skin-troubled teenagers how to clean oily skin and shrink enlarged pores with a 10-minute home medical treatment he has perfected….

> "The news tone has been set by the opening phrases. The entire remainder of the copy has been given the atmosphere of a report rather than a sales story by these first few sentences" (p. 191).

### Cross-medium application (p. 191)

- **Direct mail** — mimic refund checks, government correspondence, dividend notices, confidential reports, high-priced newsletters. Each format carries its own pre-built trust profile.
- **Radio / TV** — adopt the news format and phraseology: "the clipped phrase of the newscaster, the insider tone of the analyst, the documentary feel of the news, hand-held camera."

> "Study the channels of communication that people believe in. Adopt their tone, their feel, their style, their sincerity. Make your ads blend in, so there is no jarring transition. Camouflage them" (p. 191).

## Mood Adaptations (pp. 192–194)

> "Advertising language, which is quite naturally biased language and emotionally-charged language, tends to produce a counter-reaction in its prospects by its very appearance. To overcome this instant and automatic skepticism — besides borrowing our host publication's idiom — we also have two other idioms which allow us to escape the 'hard-sell' stereotype" (p. 192).

### Understatement (pp. 192–193)

> "The first is Understatement. Simplicity. A lack of color words. Fewer adjectives than the reader would expect — to superlatives. Short sentences, that fall rather than rise in tone at their end" (p. 192).

> "For example, take any Volkswagen ad. Count the number of nouns per sentence, and compare it to the number of adjectives. See how the ad kids itself — never takes itself too seriously. Notice how it makes its point quietly, and then stops. Even how the extremely short, extremely simple sentence structure adds to the feeling of sincerity" (p. 192).

**Volkswagen example (pp. 192–193):**

> You never run out of air.
> You also won't have any worries about draining or flushing the radiator in spring. There is no radiator.
> Or hoses. Or water pump. Or rust.
> And so there are no worries about anti-freeze in the fall or cracked blocks in the winter.
> If you feel that you owe your Volkswagen engine a little something special every spring and fall, you can do this much.
> Just run it once around the block and let it air itself out.

> "This is great copy because of its simplicity and its image and its sell, all of which interact to give a very powerful argument to anyone looking for a new car" (p. 193).

The trick is the falling cadence. Each sentence lands shorter than expected; the absence of superlatives reads as confidence; the self-kidding ("if you feel that you owe your Volkswagen engine a little something special") inoculates against the prospect's reflexive resistance to advertising voice.

### Deadly Sincerity (pp. 193–194)

> "The second mood you may employ to break away from the 'advertising' stereotype is what I call Deadly Sincerity. This is the technique of leaning over backwards to point out the flaws in an offer, so that the benefits, when you bring them in, will be believed that much more deeply. Since this is not done in ordinary advertising — since ordinary copy does not knock its product as well as praise it — it carries great emotional impact, especially in highly-competitive fields" (p. 193).

**"Settle for Nothing Less Than the Presidency" book ad (pp. 193–194):**

> TO THE MAN WHO WILL SETTLE FOR NOTHING LESS THAN THE PRESIDENCY OF HIS FIRM
> And who is willing to make the incredible sacrifices necessary to get there in the shortest possible time.
> Here is the most realistic handbook ever written for you — and you alone.
> This is a private advertisement.
> It is not meant for ninety-nine men out of every hundred. These men do not have the drive — the impossible pride — the absolute compulsion to succeed that this advertisement demands…

> "The use of such 'non-advertising phraseology' as 'incredible sacrifice'… 'impossible pride'… 'absolute compulsion'… 'this advertisement demands' set the ad off from the others around it — give it an air of unpredictability that induces the reader to go on" (p. 194).

> "This is one more way to break out of the hard-sell stereotype, and gain almost the same type of believability that would be given to a factual report" (p. 194).

The phrases work because they belong to no other ad in the column. "This is a private advertisement," "this advertisement demands" — none of these are the language of selling. The reader's filter doesn't activate, and the offer slips past it.

### Damaging-admission variant (pp. 200–201)

A second variant of Deadly Sincerity is shown in Chapter 14. The John Horn book opener begins by *flagging the flaws*:

> "This is John Horn's first book. He is not an accomplished writer, and it has flaws…"

Then turns:

> "But there are paragraphs in this book — ideas in this book — whole chapters in this book… that may take a lifetime of waiting for you to walk through!"

Schwartz's diagnosis: "The startling frankness of the first paragraph… serves as an intensifying contrast to the positive claims of the second paragraph — making them far more believable…" (p. 201). The technique is the same as Deadly Sincerity proper — confess first, claim second — and the contrast does the persuasion work.

## Copy patterns

- Same-as-editorial headline type, upper-and-lower-case, no all-caps banners (p. 187).
- Stacked sub-heads in the medium's idiom, even if "Nineteenth Century" (p. 187).
- Line drawings instead of glamour photos when the host publication uses line drawings (p. 190).
- News-indicator opener: "[CITY], [STATE] — A leading [authority] today…" with byline (p. 191).
- Direct-mail mimicry: refund-check, dividend-notice, confidential-report formats (p. 191).
- Broadcast mimicry: clipped newscaster cadence, hand-held camera feel (p. 191).
- Understatement copy: short sentences, sentences that *fall* in tone, almost no adjectives (Volkswagen, p. 192).
- Deadly Sincerity opener: state who the offer is *not* for / list the product's flaws first (pp. 193–194, 201).
- "This is a private advertisement" / non-advertising phraseology (p. 194).

## Ethical posture

Schwartz frames Camouflage as borrowing trust the prospect *already has* — and is careful to note that the trust is finite. The reader trusts the publication because "he simply assumes that his publication wouldn't carry the ad if it weren't true" (p. 186). The implication, which Schwartz states without moralizing, is that this trust is *spent* by the ad: lose it, and "the effectiveness of its advertising just goes to pieces. If he no longer believes in the publication, he won't believe in the advertising it carries" (p. 186).

Deadly Sincerity is presented as the inverse, cleanest move: leaning over backwards to point out the flaws earns the believability that factual reporting earns (p. 193). It's the ethical counterweight to "advertising language" skepticism — the ad that admits its limits is the ad that gets believed on its strengths.

**Modern reader caveat.** What Schwartz does NOT discuss as an ethical question is the deliberate blurring of editorial and advertisement under Adopt Format and Adopt Phraseology (pp. 186–191). In 1966 he treats it purely as a craft optimization. Today this lands squarely in the FTC's native-advertising disclosure rules and similar regimes elsewhere — an ad designed to look like editorial generally requires a clear "Advertisement," "Sponsored," or equivalent label. The technique still works; the disclosure obligation is the difference between borrowing trust and stealing it.
