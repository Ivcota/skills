# Final Touches (Ch 14) — deep dive

> Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Ch. 14 (book pp. 195–226), Epilogue (p. 227).

After the seven big techniques (Intensification, Identification, Gradualization, Redefinition, Mechanization, Concentration, Camouflage) have been broken out and analyzed, the copywriter still has to *put the ad back together* as a single living document. Chapter 14 is the reassembly chapter — six craft skills (Verification, Reinforcement, Interweaving, Sensitivity, Momentum, Mood) that govern *placement, blending, pacing, and tone*.

---

## Why a final-touches chapter exists

Schwartz opens Ch. 14 with a confession: the analytic chapters made copy sound mechanical. The reader of Chapters 6–13 could be forgiven for thinking copywriting is a paint-by-numbers operation — "here you put in a promise; there you nail on a believability sentence; then cover both with an identification" (p. 202). That's the wrong picture.

Schwartz uses a **golf-pro analogy** (pp. 202–203). Watch a great pro swing — fluid, unbroken motion. Then read his book and you discover the swing is forty distinct movements, each individually awkward when practiced alone. That awkwardness is unavoidable; it is the **middle stage** between not-knowing and unconscious mastery. He calls the destination **Creativity #2** (p. 203):

> Writing from the full depth of emotion *and* mind, no longer conscious of the mechanisms — the way you're not conscious of your fingers on the typewriter keys. (paraphrased, p. 203)

Creativity #1 is the analytic stage — phrase-carpentry, mechanical, conscious of every part. Creativity #2 is the post-analytic stage where the same techniques fire intuitively at the exact moment they're needed. Ch. 14 is the bridge.

## Verification — placement of proof (pp. 196–200)

**Verification is the placement of proof — anywhere in the copy where it produces maximum effect.**

> "Now, of course, comes the most obvious kind of believability copy. Your proof: your statistics; your tests; your testimonials; your authorities; your trends; your documentation; your seals of approval; your awards won. Any fact at all that you can use, anywhere in the copy, to show that your product does what you say it does." (p. 196)

> "The key words here are *anywhere in your copy*. Because… the placement of your proof is as important to its overall effect as the content itself." (p. 196)

**Phrasing rules** (p. 197): "keep it as short as possible, as dramatic as possible, as specific as possible." Proof copy is still selling copy — it must make the prospect *hungrier* with every line, not bog him down.

**The core rule** (p. 197):

> "Proof — like claims — is most effective when the reader unconsciously demands it, and when he is ready to accept its content as necessary and logical."

The TV Repair Manual ad (referenced p. 199) introduces "manufacturers test rooms" exactly when the reader has been led to demand a solution — placement at peak readiness, not at a fixed slot.

**The three-part distinction** (p. 199):

| Term | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Documentation** | The proof itself — statistics, facts, tests, testimonials, awards. |
| **Mechanization** | The verbal/logical demonstration that the product works — proves on logic alone, no outside data needed (Ch. 11). |
| **Verification** | The *arrangement* of documentation within the copy so it lands at peak emotional readiness. |

**The anti-"Here's Proof box" prescription** (pp. 199–200):

> "Stop thinking of placing your documentation only in the headline of your ad, or in the sub-head, or in a separate box labelled, 'Here's Proof.' … start thinking of documentation as one more element… to be interwoven together, side by side."

## Reinforcement — 1 + 1 = 10 (pp. 200–201)

**Two emotions skillfully joined produce the impact of four — sometimes ten.**

> "In mathematics, one plus one always equals two — never more. In emotional writing, one plus one can often equal ten." (p. 200)

> "Two emotional images, joined together in the right way, can often have TEN TIMES the impact that either of these images has by itself." (p. 200)

**Canonical example — Avis** (p. 200):

- "We try harder." — good alone.
- "We're only second." — the *reason why*.
- Combined: "(1) We're second; (2) Therefore we try harder." — joint impact far greater than either claim alone.

> "We can even define one type of creativity as the ability to combine separate images into a new unity that is much more powerful than the mere logical sum of its parts." (pp. 200–201)

**Reinforcement via damaging admission — John Horn book** (p. 201). The first paragraph confesses the book's flaws (not an accomplished writer, has flaws, won't be read for style). That confession becomes "an intensifying contrast to the positive claims of the second paragraph — making them far more believable."

> "It is the *juxtaposition* of these two paragraphs — one following right on top of the other — that makes their combination so effective." (p. 201)

> "Copywriting, in many phases, is the search for such juxtapositions… underline the combination points — and junctures — where one claim blends into another, and either sharpens it, pours strength into it, or makes it more believable." (p. 201)

**Working rule** (p. 201): constantly build one claim on top of another — always seeking to make each stronger and more believable by the combination.

## Interweaving — every sentence, every element (pp. 202–205)

**The Walter S. Campbell rule** (p. 203, from Campbell's *Writing Non-Fiction*, The Writer Inc., 1961 — a book Schwartz says every copywriter can master in a weekend):

> "Even in the most factual reporting, no sentence can be effective if it contains only the facts alone. It must also contain emotion, evaluation, impact — if those facts are to be given meaning and importance to the reader." (paraphrased from Campbell, p. 203)

**The rule for copy** (p. 203):

> "That sentence should contain not only promise … not only image … not only logic — but as much of all three as possible."

> "Weave together your promise, your logic, your emotion, your image. Pack your sentences full of every one of them. Make them blend into each other, till it's almost impossible to pull out the individual threads of the rich pattern of conviction and desire you're weaving." (p. 204)

### Worked example — fire injector ad (p. 204)

**Disadvantage-packed sentence (six negative-images for ordinary plugs in one sentence):**

> "With ordinary spark plugs you are using, or should be using premium gas (disadvantage) which costs from 4 to 8 cents more than ordinary gas (disadvantage), and despite this (disadvantage) you're getting inefficient (disadvantage), wasteful (disadvantage) gas consumption (disadvantage)." (p. 204)

**Promise-packed contrast (five promises in one sentence):**

> "Will fire injectors regular gas (promise) will give you up to 8 miles more per gallon (promise), up to 31 more horsepower (promise), plus easier starting (promise) in all weather (promise)." (p. 204)

### Maximum-interweaving sentence (pp. 204–205)

Annotated breakdown of a single sentence carrying mood + authority + believability + core promise + ease + condensation:

> "And, most important, [a mood-builder, indicating the value to the reader of the promises which are about to follow] these experts [reference to authority to build believability] have discovered [more believability through use of the scientific idiom] that you do not have to be a handyman [the core-promise] or mechanic [restatement of the core-promise] in order to coax [promise of ease] this performance [promise, condensing all the claims of superior reception detailed in previous copy] out of your set." (pp. 204–205)

> "Now, is this done consciously? Not at all. But the copy writer knew the principle of interweaving … had experimented with it before … and had it stored away in his unconscious as one more tool to be used — intuitively — to gain the effect he wanted, at the exact moment he needed it." (p. 205)

> "See how many different emotions you can pack into a single sentence … paragraph … sequence. See how much richer and more powerful your copy becomes. How much more you can say in the same space. How you bring reinforcement-multiplied impact into more and more of your copy." (p. 205)

## Sensitivity — knowing when to stop (pp. 205–206)

The opposite problem of interweaving's piling-up: **knowing when to stop, when to switch.** Reader satiation is the killer.

> "Now; if you continue to pile promise upon promise, identification upon identification, documentation upon documentation, element upon element, where do you stop? Where does it become too much? Where does the reader become satiated or bored and want to go on to a new image or new promise, or even the close? At what point are you starting to oversell?" (p. 205)

**The skill is empathy as a copywriter** — be simultaneously the writer and the reader.

> "You must anticipate that point in the copy flow — as it is transformed into a series of impressions in your reader's mind — where he is going to say: 'I've read enough about this. Give me this instead.' And then you must shift the direction of the copy to meet his new direction of interest." (pp. 205–206)

**Architecture-level craft** — not sentence-level, not paragraph-level:

> "What we are talking about now is the over-all structure of your ad. Not a sentence, or a paragraph, or a sequence: but the architecture of the ad as a whole. Every ad has an architecture… This is the over-all pattern of the ad, including when and where and how many times you shift from one type of copy to another. It is the ability to recognize these shift points, and to change when you encounter them — from say promise copy to mechanism copy to documentation copy to promise copy again — that makes your ad stick together, or fall apart." (p. 206)

> "This is an almost impossible skill to communicate, since it relies so much on the feel of each individual situation." (p. 206)

The teaching device Schwartz reaches for next: four mail-order book ads, each annotated with alphabetized blocks (A, B, C…) and numbered shift-points (1, 2, 3…).

## The four sample-ad architectures (pp. 206–214)

### Ad #1 — "How to make anybody like you!" (p. 206)

Paul P. Parker / Executive Research Institute. Mail-order popularity / friend-winning self-help book.

**Pattern: Promise → Definition → Documentation.**

- (A) Headline: "How to make anybody like you!" — direct-promise, almost universal.
- (B) Sub-promise: "Can popularity be purchased? The answer is yes. And the cost is two to three hours of your time."
- (1) → first transition shifts from promise to *redefinition*: making-anybody-like-you becomes "popularity," and the unrecognized fact that popularity *can be taught*.
- (C) Documentation block — the corporate-name list:

> General Motors · DuPont · Sears Roebuck · A&P · Eastman Kodak · Ford · Borden · Coca-Cola · Goodyear · Standard Oil · General Electric…

These corporations paid the author heavily to teach their employees, so the documentation does double duty — proves expertise *and* anchors the redefined claim ("popularity can be taught") in lived corporate fact.

- (2) → detailed promise block (D)
- (E) echo of documentation + price comparison
- (F) transitional promise
- (G) restate over-all promise
- (H) third documentation restatement
- (I) price comparison + money-back guarantee
- (3) → classic transition into the catalog of detailed promises (now far stronger because definition + documentation laid the ground)

The negative promises (K) and full documentation (L) are placed *out of the main stream* — readable or skippable (p. 210).

### Ad #2 — "I'll Make You a Mental Wizard in One Evening!" (p. 210)

Harry Lorayne mind-power book.

**Pattern: the most subtle interweaving in the four-ad set.**

- (A) Headline: "I'll make you a mental wizard in one evening!"
- (B) Sub-promise intensifies: "Yes! Here at last is your chance to gain the THINKING-MACHINE MIND you've dreamed about… so easily and quickly that you'll be astounded… and do it without risking a penny!"
- (1) first transitional sentence → two paragraphs of negative redefinition (C) and positive redefinition (D).
- (2) single transition-word "Simply" → promise grows stronger and more detailed, gaining a believability-tone of definition.
- Definition-tone unchanged through (F), where promise is interwoven with fact.
- (3) tone is proof, content is straight promise (G).
- (4) proof connected to promise catalog (H) which fills the rest of the ad.

**Schwartz's verdict** (p. 210):

> "This ad — enormously successful — is one of the best examples of interweaving we have. There is so subtle a blending here of definition, documentation and promise, that every word of the copy soon takes on the aura of pure fact."

### Ad #3 — "You Are Twice as Smart as You Think!" (p. 210)

Schwartz's own ad (Executive Research Institute, "How to Double Your Power to Learn").

**Pattern: the straight-promise / momentum train. Rock-em sock-em.**

> "The straight-promise ad. This is the rock-em, sock-em type, dominated by promise and mechanism-promise, which gains its power from the sheer momentum and relentlessness of its claims." (p. 210)

- (A) Headline: "YOU ARE TWICE AS SMART AS YOU THINK!"
- (B) Sub-promise: "And these seven simple actions will prove it to you in a single weekend — by doubling your power to learn!"
- Over-all promise (A) → second elaborating promise (B) → fundamental definition (C) equating it in a single phrase "(then you could —)" → greatly detailed promise (D) → condensed transition (2) → restatement of fundamental redefinition (E) → out (3) to the remainder (F), sheer promise and promise-mechanism from then on.

**Schwartz's verdict** (p. 211):

> "Here, documentation is disregarded entirely. The full weight of the sale depends on the power and momentum of the promise-train."

### Ad #4 — "Look Years Younger in 10 Short Days!" (p. 211)

Dr. Robert Alan Franklyn / "The Art of Staying Young." Beauty book by a Hollywood plastic surgeon.

**Pattern: authority-first architecture. When prospect skepticism is ingrained, anticipate it, agree with it, then shift to authority for many paragraphs before returning to promise.**

- (A) Authority-reference: "Hollywood plastic surgeon's…" — placed *before* the promise-headline, two reasons: separates this from competing similar products the prospect has been disappointed by; brings in the key persuader (the author) immediately.
- (B) Main promise-headline.
- (C) Promise + momentum-builder. Different type-faces and sizes serve as transitional elements.
- (D) Concentration copy redefines what the woman can expect; outdates other approaches.
- (1) "Because…" → first promise-elaboration block.
- (F) Agree with the prospect's skepticism.
- (G) Shift the entire story onto the doctor for **twelve paragraphs** — by far the longest authority block in the four-ad set.
- (2) Back to promise (H), filling out the rest of the ad.

> "But there is no piling up of claim upon claim here. There is too much ingrained skepticism in this prospect — too much awareness of other, previous claims that have disappointed her in the past." (p. 211)

### See How the Structure Differs (pp. 214–215)

Same product type. Four radically different architectures. Why? Each prospect's mind state differs.

> "They range from the simplest promise-elaboration (example 3), to the most subtle blending of all the various elements of desire, image, proof, definition and what have you (example 2), to the most complicated promise-and-proof… promise-and-proof reinforcement (example 4). Each problem demands a different structure." (p. 214)

**The two analyses to do *after* writing** (pp. 214–215):

1. **What kind of ad have you written?** Straight promise? Too much documentation? Too little definition of the new world of possibilities? Does it still sound like everyone else's copy? **Could you put another brand-name on it, and not make any difference?**
2. **Have you left anything out?** Will this bare promise carry the sale? Could you increase its impact by paving its way with mechanism or believability copy? How about a proof section? A use photo? A fuller description of the way it works?

**The most actionable single rule of Ch. 14** (p. 215):

> "If you take the same content, and give it a new structure, you are going to create a new ad. Try it. See for yourself. If you're stuck sometime, throw away your headline, throw away your rough copy flow, and start off again 180 degrees away from where you started before."

> "I want to repeat this again: A new structure is a new ad. It brings out new ideas. Gives your promises new sharpness, new flavor, new believability and even begins to create whole new sections that you never dreamed were there in the product before." (p. 215)

> "If the copy story doesn't develop — if you gradually find that the headline isn't really that good after all — then perhaps the very elements that are begging to come out of your 6-point type should be at the top of your ad." (p. 215)

## Momentum (pp. 215–219)

Two devices that draw the reader deeper and deeper into the copy — keep him from quitting in the middle.

> "Copy's first job, of course, is to be read. If it's not read, it can't sell. And if you take some time to make your points — to intensify your desire, to build and rebuild your believability, to reinforce one claim with another — then you'd better make darn sure that you've put plenty of momentum-builders into your copy." (p. 218)

### Device 1 — Time-honored momentum-phrases

Inserted in transition-sentences to keep interest from flagging and signal what type of material is coming. Full list from p. 218:

1. "They paid up to $22.50 a person to learn priceless techniques like these:"
2. "You can learn them all, in your own home next weekend, without risking a penny. Here's how"
3. "Here is the information you will find in this book."
4. "Let me explain."
5. "All I ask from you is this."
6. "What you are going to do, in the very first hour that you receive the book, is this."
7. "And yet it's only the beginning."
8. "THEN put this simple trick to work for you — that VERY SAME HOUR"
9. "For example —"
10. "Read the thrilling answer below."
11. "… to start with…"
12. "Just wait till you try this."

### Device 2 — Incomplete-statement teasers

A statement that creates interest, raises a question, and implies an answer later in the copy — pulling the reader forward to complete it.

**The three-step mechanism** (p. 219):

1. Create interest in a specific point.
2. Raise a question in his mind about that point.
3. Imply an answer to that question later in the copy.

**Sample teasers** (p. 219):

- "And you will do it often using nothing more than ordinary tap water, your own ten fingers, and the contents of your garden and your refrigerator."
- "But now, when you turn on the ignition, a modern miracle of engineering science comes to life underneath your hood!"
- "Yes! Here at last is the Engineering Miracle you've been hearing rumors about for months. The fantastic Power-Booster that Continental millionaires use to soup up the performance of $12,000 cars!… The sixty-second attachment that HAD TO BE BANNED from the great European race-ways…"
- "How to Grow Thousands of Flowers — Without Dirtying Your Hands."
- "Here it is at last. Authoritative medical proof that men in their forties and fifties and sixties can enjoy a sex life in many ways as satisfying as when they were in their twenties — if they are only shown how to combat the hidden forces within their minds and bodies that literally drive them into impotency!"
- "Caught! 120 Fish in One Hour!"

> "The objective here is to keep the reader going — for one more sentence, one more paragraph, one more sequence. Notice, of course, that every one of these examples not only produces momentum, not only raises questions in the reader's mind that he virtually must read on to answer — but is also, at exactly the same time, *loaded with sell*. Again, interweaving — making each element in the ad accomplish two objectives. No waste words." (p. 222)

## Mood (pp. 222–225)

**Words and rhythm = line and color for the painter.**

> "Words and rhythms. They are to the copy writer what line and color are to the painter. They set the mood of your story — carry the emotion so subtly that your reader never really realizes where the excitement or the image or the conviction is actually coming from." (pp. 222–223)

### Two kinds of mood-words (p. 223)

- **Visual image-sharpeners** — sharpen the picture in the reader's mind. ("weighted down… solid, blazing rows… so thick you can't even see a leaf in between")
- **Emotion-definers** — tell the reader what to *feel* about the images. ("precious… that very morning… blinded… greets… you never imagined before")

> "These words are emotion-definers. They tell the reader what to feel about the images and ideas you are presenting to him. Their very essence is that they are so subtle that he does not see them… that they do not call attention to themselves, but simply flavor and evaluate the images your copy is forming in his mind." (p. 223)

### The visible-mood-word warning (pp. 223–224)

> "Certain classes of readers will notice these emotion-definers. When they do, your copy becomes either questionable or corny to them, and you have lost them. Therefore, you have to know always *whom you are writing for*. What their level of sophistication is — in tone as well as product-awareness. And you may have to change your tone when you take the same ad from one class of media to another — down as well as up."

> "Of course, when *everybody* sees your emotion words, then you've got a bad ad, and you'd better rewrite, fast." (p. 224)

**Audience-class warning:** must downshift as well as upshift. Same ad, wrong audience class — rewrite the tone, not the structure.

**Rhythm:** read the ad aloud. "As though it were close to blank-verse. This richness of rhythm parallels the richness of image — helps build the excitement and sensuousness of the mood." (p. 224)

### Pounding/staccato example — Lorayne (pp. 224–225)

> "This letter says that if you could only liberate the exact same talent and intelligence and ability that you have tied up inside you today — then you could:
> 'read anything you wish, twice as fast as you can read it today…'
> 'absorb facts like a sponge, and repeat them almost word for word years later…'
> 'flash through math, business, financial problems that have you stopped cold today…'
> 'hold people spellbound with the power of your speech and your written word…'
> 'out-think others when you have to, tower over them in judgement, outshine them completely in imagination…'"

Two emotion-definer types stacked here:

- **Power words** ("liberate… hold spellbound… out-think… tower over… outshine completely") — build a subconscious current of potency.
- **Frankness / man-to-man / let's-let-our-hair-down tone-builders** ("this letter says… tied up inside you… like a sponge… stopped cold") — say silently that there's no nonsense here.

> "And, of course, the machine-gun rhythm. Short, choppy sentences. Verb… verb… verb. Claim… claim… claim. Propelling the reader through the copy. Piling promise upon promise, image upon image in an attempt to overwhelm him." (pp. 224–225)

### Volkswagen contrast — sincerity by absence of adjectives (p. 225)

Volkswagen's emotional tone of *sincerity* is conveyed by the **absence** of adjectives in all the places you'd ordinarily expect them, plus whimsy and continuous self-kidding. First builds corporate affection, then trust.

### Fire-injector contrast — clumsy sentences as ingenuousness (p. 225)

> "Long, rather clumsy sentences give a feeling of ingenuousness and reason… Try to read them out loud. You can't. They don't scan; they have no definable rhythm: they don't look like a 'copy writer' wrote them at all. And, of course, there is the same straight-from-the-shoulder phraseology, to reinforce this feeling of honest appraisal." (p. 225)

> "Mood-building is deliberate on the part of the copy writer — unnoticed on the part of his reader. As with so much of the rest of the ad, it lies underneath the surface, to do its work imperceptibly." (p. 225)
