# Concentration (Technique #6) — deep dive

> Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Ch. 12 (book pp. 175–184).

## Core principle

Concentration is *direct attack on alternative ways your prospect could satisfy his desire*. It is the fifth competitive weapon, distinct from product superiority, promise superiority, product-role, and response/reaction (pp. 175–176).

> "Concentration differs completely from the other four methods… [they] act as though there is no other way possible of gaining the satisfaction your prospect desires" (p. 176).

Where the other four ignore the competition, Concentration names it — and dismantles it — so the desire that was previously channeled there can be re-routed to your product. It is "the process of pointing out weaknesses in the competition… emphasizing their disservice to your prospect… and then proving to him that your product gives him what he wants without them" (p. 177).

## When to use it (p. 177)

Three conditions point toward Concentration:

1. **Smaller budget than the competition.** You can't outspend; you have to out-attack.
2. **Most prospects are already customers of the competitor.** You can't ignore the relationship; you have to break it.
3. **There is an image to crack.** Loyalty has to be shaken before desire can be rechanneled.

## The cardinal rule (p. 177)

Attack alone is not Concentration. Concentration is attack-plus-solution, and the two halves are inseparable:

> "But this process of Concentration — this careful, logical, documented process of proving ineffectual other ways of satisfying your prospect's desire — is much more than mere attack. If you can only attack another product — without showing at the same time, by comparison, how your product provides what the other lacks — then say nothing at all! Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time" (p. 177).

> "Your prospect knows that your attack is biased. If, therefore, you are attacking another product only for your own good — in other words, to win the sale by disparaging your competitor — what you will probably evoke in his mind is skepticism and dislike, and very little else" (p. 177).

## Concentration combines other techniques (p. 178)

> "Concentration is therefore a complicated process, taking up sizable space to do its job properly, and combining almost every trick you have learned in this book" (p. 178).

The three load-bearing techniques inside a Concentration sequence (p. 178):

- **Intensification** — dramatize the penalties of staying with the old product.
- **Gradualization** — supply the logical cause of those weaknesses, step by step.
- **Mechanization** — prove that *your* product removes those weaknesses with a documented why.

A Concentration ad without Mechanization is a complaint. A Concentration ad without Gradualization is name-calling. The technique only earns its sales force when all three are operating at once.

## Structure 1 — Bad/Good Interleave (pp. 178–180)

### Worked example: Spark plug / Fire injector ad ("MECHANICS AND ENGINEERS READ THIS CAREFULLY")

The copy alternates one-for-one, claim against claim (pp. 178–180):

- "A spark plug jumps a spark of electricity across an air gap. This is the most wasteful and power consuming way…"
- "A fire injector fires on the surface of an electrical conductor. This is the most efficient way to get a big powerful spark…"
- "On ordinary spark plugs the air gap … is always getting bigger because the electrode is always burning away. This means you have misfiring…"
- "On fire injectors there is no air gap and no electrode to burn away. That means maximum gas explosion which means full power, full economy…"
- "A spark plug accumulates filth and carbon … needs regular cleaning, setting and expensive replacement!"
- "A fire injector never needs cleaning or setting. It actually 'breaks in' and becomes more efficient with use. It will actually outlast your car…"
- "A spark plug gives you a thin skimpy spark…"
- "A fire injector gives you a heavy powerful flame…"

**Underlying structure (p. 180):**

> Bad. / Good. / Bad. / Good. / Bad. / Good.

**Devices Schwartz identifies in the worked example (pp. 179–180):**

- **Word-image contrast pairs** — "spark" (weak) vs. "flame" (strong); "thin skimpy spark" vs. "heavy powerful flame." The metaphor does the work the adjectives can't.
- **Definition / redefinition baked into every claim** — "most wasteful and power consuming way" vs. "most efficient way." Each line redefines the category before stating the comparison.
- **"Breaks in"** — redefinition by analogy. The fire injector is reframed in the language of a baseball glove or a pair of boots: it improves with use.
- **Documentary "because…" tail** — almost every benefit carries its mechanism on its back ("because the electrode is burning away," "because of inefficient firing"). Mechanization rides inside Concentration.
- **Authority capstone** — "These are some of the reasons that the U.S. Air Force pays premium prices for surface supported injectors for their aircraft…" The attack sequence closes with an outside witness.

## Structure 2 — Time Sequence (p. 181)

When points can't be cleanly broken down one-for-one, switch to the time-sequence structure:

1. **What happens to you now**, with the product or products you are using presently.
2. **What will happen when you switch** to the new product.

### Worked example: Lecithin reducing pill (pp. 181–183)

**Negative copy phase** (definitions/redefinition; willpower failure cycle):

> "For years doctors have known that ordinary reducing plans … are completely passive! That they depend strictly on your own will power… HUNGER-APPEASING PRODUCTS — pills, powders and liquids that do nothing more than swell up in your stomach — that do nothing more than 'dull' your hunger a little. But not one of these products could do anything to ACTIVELY help you reduce your weight…" (pp. 181–182).

> "So what happened? If you were overweight, you struggled to do the job of reducing BY YOURSELF! You took your hunger-appeasing pills religiously. You pushed away the foods you love. You spent week after week of torture. And finally, if you were lucky, you carved off 5, 10, or even 12 precious pounds. And then your will power snapped! You broke your terrible diet… And the fat flowed back…" (p. 182).

**Schwartz's dissection on p. 182:**

- **¶1–2 — Definitions and Redefinition.** Ordinary reducing plans are *passive*; depend on your willpower; can do nothing to *actively* burn fat. The category itself is reframed before any attack lands.
- **¶3 — Equating ordinary pills with "doing the job yourself."** The pills are denied any active role; their purchase is rewritten as a form of self-effort.
- **¶3–4 — Logic, cause and effect** ("So what happened?"). The reader is walked through a sequence of events she has lived through. Each line is one she nods at. The sequence is the documentary mechanism.
- **¶4 climax** — "buried in a sentence with which no woman could disagree — is that it was the failure of the *pills* that caused the failure of the diet" (p. 182).

That last move is the load-bearing trick of the time-sequence: the failure that the prospect *blames herself for* gets reassigned to the alternative product.

**Hero-product phase** (case-history return to promise; p. 183):

> "These doctors had the answer in a tiny grey pill — and a common-sense plan… It was a miraculous compound called LECITHIN — brand-new — whose amazing fat-dissolving properties had been discovered by a Nobel prize winner — the co-discoverer of insulin… they were not given any starvation diets … they never experienced a single hungry moment … the ugly excess fat around their bodies melted away!"

> "Here are your contrast — your mechanization — your documentation — your reference to authority — and then your return to promise, in the form of case history, which now has many times its original power, based on both the elimination of alternate channels of fulfillment, and the strong supporting mechanism which documents its claims" (p. 183).

## One Final Word (p. 184)

Concentration does not require length. The same emotional move can be compressed into a single headline:

> "I have purposely chosen extreme examples to illustrate each mechanism. These copy blocks are longer than Concentration need logically be. The same effect may be boiled down into two or three sentences, or even a single phrase, as in this classic headline: 'SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY.' Here is contrast — implied weakness in other products — compensating promise in your own" (p. 184).

> "It is not the content, nor is it the length of copy used in a mechanism that makes it effective. It is simply and solely the problem it solves for you in the development of your copy — by the emotional reaction it produces on your reader when he encounters it" (p. 184).

The headline-form Concentration carries all three elements: contrast (surgery vs. shrinkage), implied weakness (the alternative is the knife), and compensating promise. Length is a packaging decision; the structure is the same at six paragraphs and at six words.

## Copy patterns

- Bad/Good interleave structure (p. 180).
- Word-image contrast pairs ("spark" vs. "flame"; p. 180).
- Documentary "because…" tail on every benefit (p. 180).
- Authority capstone after the attack sequence (U.S. Air Force; p. 179).
- Time-sequence structure: present misery (with current option) → switch moment → new outcome (p. 181).
- Universal-experience cause/effect ("So what happened?") that lets the prospect nod through every line (p. 182).
- Reassign self-blame to the alternative product (the failure of the pills caused the failure of the diet; p. 182).
- Short-form Concentration in a single headline (contrast + implied weakness + compensating promise; p. 184).

## Ethical posture

Two guardrails govern Concentration's use, both at p. 177.

**First — never attack without supplying the solution.** Naked disparagement evokes "skepticism and dislike, and very little else." The reader knows your attack is biased; the only way to redeem the bias is to make the attack *useful to him*, which means demonstrating the alternative in the same breath. "Say nothing at all" is the explicit fallback when you can't pair attack with solution.

**Second — don't attack at all when your story is fresh enough that you'd lend the competitor "the prestige of attack."** When your product, promise, or mechanism is genuinely new, naming the competition does him a favor. The space costs you a dollar; spending it on his name is a transfer payment. Concentration is the technique you reach for when you must — not when you can.
