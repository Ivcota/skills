# Breakthrough Advertising

A copywriter's operating system from Eugene M. Schwartz's 1966 book of the same name (Boardroom 2004 reissue). Schwartz wrote direct-response copy "for the best direct marketers in America" (Edelston foreword, p. iii). The book's one-line thesis, which he discovered only on rereading it eighteen years later: "There is a way to develop an entirely new market for a new or an old product. That way involves a certain number of clearly-defined steps. And in this book I show you every single one of those steps." (p. v)

## Core Principle

**Copy cannot create desire — it can only channel it.** "The power, the force, the overwhelming urge to own that makes advertising work, comes from the market itself, and not from the copy. Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product. This is the copy writer's task: not to create this mass desire — but to channel and direct it." (p. 3)

Because mass desires are built by social, economic, and technological forces "far greater than advertising itself can command" (p. 5), advertising that taps an already-existing desire commands an economic force "hundreds of times more powerful than the mere number of dollars the advertiser can spend on it" — Schwartz's **Amplification Effect** (p. 5). Advertising that tries to *create* a desire instead of channel it "is no longer advertising but education" and "can produce at best only one dollar in sales for every dollar spent" (p. 5). The Edsel — "a good car, backed by a deluge of fine advertising, that died trying to fight the overwhelming switch in demand" (p. 4) — is Schwartz's reminder that you cannot fight a mass desire.

**The foundation:** before a single word of the headline is written, three diagnostic questions must be answered (p. 14):
1. What is the **mass desire** that creates this market?
2. How much do these people know today about the way your product satisfies this desire — their **State of Awareness**?
3. How many other products have been presented to them before yours — their **State of Sophistication**?

The next three sections answer each question. Then seven techniques govern the body copy. Then six final-touch craft skills reassemble the ad as a living document.

## Part 1 — The Diagnostic Triad

### 1. Mass Desire — the engine

**Core concept.** A market exists "the moment a private desire is shared by a statistically significant number of people, large enough to profitably repay selling these people" (p. 5). The copywriter's job is to inventory those desires, pick the most powerful one *that the product can deliver on*, and channel it through the headline.

**Why it works.** "Every product appeals to two, three or four of these mass desires. But only one can predominate; only one can reach out through your headline to your customer. Only one is the key that unlocks the maximum economic power at the particular time your advertisement is published. Your choice among these alternate desires is the most important step you will take in writing your ad. If it is wrong, nothing else that you do in the ad will matter." (p. 8)

**Three dimensions of every mass desire** (pp. 7–8):
- **Urgency** — degree of demand to be satisfied. "Constant arthritic pains compared to a minor headache."
- **Staying power** — degree of repetition, inability to become satiated. "Raw hunger compared to a craving for gourmet foods."
- **Scope** — number of people who share it. "The number of men willing to pay $10 for an automotive accessory that saves gas — as compared to those willing to pay the same price for one that merely prevents future repair bills."

**Two source-types of desire** (p. 6):
- **Permanent forces** — Mass Instinct (virility, attractiveness, health) and mass technological problems (bad TV reception, slow-acting aspirin). "The instinct never fades — the desire never changes."
- **Forces of change** — beginning, fulfillment, reversal of a trend (the Fifties horsepower wave collapsing into gas-economy demand).

**Three components of the prospect's mind that the body copy must touch** (Ch. 6, pp. 71–75):
- **Desires** — physical / material / sensual wants (be thin, possess a car, drink a cold beer). The first task: "make your prospect want." (p. 73)
- **Identifications** — roles, status traits, self-image. "These longings for identification… complement and intensify the physical desires — add another dimension to them." (p. 73)
- **Beliefs** — opinions, attitudes, prejudices, "the world of emotionalized reason that he inhabits." Beliefs are a *filter*: "These beliefs form a filter through which your product-information must pass or be rejected." (p. 74)

**Physical product vs functional product** (pp. 9–12). "The physical product does not sell. People do not buy the steel in a car, the glass in a vase, the tobacco in a cigarette, or the paper in a book." (p. 9) "What they are really paying you for is what the product will do." (p. 9) "No physical part of your product can ever become a headline." (p. 9) Physical facts get used *later*, to document price, quality, durability, mental picture, and believability (pp. 9–10).

**Copy patterns:**
- "[Avatar] buys [product] for [functional benefit] AND for [identity it broadcasts]." (p. 73)
- "[Physical fact about manufacturing] means [continued functional benefit]." (Rolls-Royce: rubber-isolated chassis → "perfect riding silence", p. 10)
- Headline-entry rule by awareness: aware of product → start with product; aware of desire only → start with desire; aware only of a problem → start with problem and "crystallize it into a specific need." (p. 8)

**Ethical boundary.** Schwartz does not lay down explicit guardrails in this chapter — but he is firm that advertising cannot fight a mass desire, only ride it. "You cannot create it, and you cannot fight it. But you can — and must — direct it, channel it, focus it onto your particular product." (p. 4)

See: [references/mass-desire.md](references/mass-desire.md) for the full Amplification Effect treatment, the desire inventory, and worked auto-industry / Edsel cases.

### 2. State of Awareness — where the headline starts

**Core concept.** Every prospect sits on one of five rungs of an awareness scale; the headline must begin at exactly that rung. "It is at this precise point of awareness that your headline begins." (p. 15) Different rungs demand different headline mechanics: as awareness *drops*, mechanical phrasing gives way to creative work, and "the more aware your market, the easier the selling job, the less you need to say" (p. 16).

**Why it works.** "Each of these stages is separated from the others by a psychological wall. On one side of that wall is indifference; on the other, intense interest. A headline that will work wonders in the first stage… will fail completely when addressed to a third-stage market where your prospect doesn't even realize that soaps can be made with built-in deodorants." (pp. 23–24)

**The Headline's only job** (pp. 14–15): "Your headline does not need to sell at all. It does not have to mention your product. It does not even have to mention your main appeal… Your headline has only one job — to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence of your ad."

**The Five States** — using Schwartz's own section headings (popular labels like "Solution-aware" and "Problem-aware" are *not* in the book; preserve Schwartz's wording):

| # | Schwartz's term | Definition | Headline strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Most Aware** (p. 16) | Knows of your product, knows what it does, wants it, hasn't bought yet. | Product name + price. *Nothing more.* |
| 2 | **The Customer Knows of the Product But Doesn't Yet Want It** (p. 16) | Aware of product and brand, but isn't convinced of the performance, or hasn't been told what's new. | Display product + state superiority on one of seven dimensions (reinforce desire / sharpen image / extend image / new proof / new mechanism better / new mechanism eliminating limitation / reposition). (p. 17) |
| 3 | **How to Introduce New Products** (p. 19) | Knows what he wants but doesn't know your product exists. | Crystallize the amorphous desire in one statement. Three steps: name desire+solution → prove → show product is the mechanism. (p. 20) |
| 4 | **How to Introduce Products That Solve Needs** (p. 21) | Knows the need / problem but not the connection to your product. | Name the need / solution → dramatize so the prospect realizes how badly he needs the solution → present product as inevitable solution. (pp. 21–22) |
| 5 | **How to Open Up a Completely Unaware Market** (p. 23) | Doesn't know the desire / won't admit it / can't verbalize it. | **Identification headline.** No price, no product name, no direct statement of function. Echo an emotion or unvoiced grievance to "call your market together in the headline." (pp. 25–26) |

**Key insights:**
- The headline does not sell — it buys the next sentence. (pp. 14–15)
- As awareness drops, creative wages rise. Stage 1 copywriters are "phrase-makers" who deserve "the lowest possible scale of pay" (p. 16); Stage 5 is "the domain of the idea man" (p. 20).
- Stage 5 has *seven named tactics* — Giving Words to a Hidden Dream, Exploiting a Hidden Fear, Leading Into an Unacceptable Problem via a Universally Accepted Image, Projecting a Hidden Desire That Cannot Be Put Bluntly Into Words, Using a Common Resentment, Projecting an Ultimate Triumph, Projecting the Result of a Problem (pp. 26–34).
- Markets move; even a winning headline ages. "You never step in the same river twice." (p. 35)
- Style ages, strategy doesn't. (p. 36)

**Copy patterns** (representative — full library in references):
- Stage 1: `[Product] — Formerly [old price] — Now Only [new price].` (Revere Zomar, p. 16)
- Stage 2: `[Product] — [the {-est} {category} in the world].` (Joy, p. 17); `At [extreme condition], the [unexpected detail] is [mundane fact].` (Rolls-Royce, p. 18)
- Stage 3: `How to [achieve big desire].`; `Who else wants [outcome] — with no [expected sacrifice]?`; `Now! [Surprising elimination of a default step]!` (p. 21)
- Stage 4: `[Problem]?` (p. 22); `Stops [vivid symptom].`; `Shrinks [problem] without [feared method].` (p. 22); `Look, [witness]! No [previously-expected problem]!` (p. 23)
- Stage 5: `WHY [identity] [verb]…` ("WHY MEN CRACK…", Postum, p. 28); `WITHIN [intimate / poetic image]` ("WITHIN THE CURVE OF A WOMAN'S ARM", Odorono, p. 29); `THEY LAUGHED WHEN I [low-status setup]. BUT WHEN I [reveal of competence]!—` (U.S. School of Music, p. 32); `OFTEN [secondary social role] BUT NEVER [primary social role].` (Listerine, p. 33)

**Ethical boundary.** "Most copy writers use a fifth stage problem to write an empty headline, and are therefore simply wasting their client's money… Your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it. It must pick out the product's logical prospects — and reject as many people as it attracts." (p. 35) Cute, startling, or pretty-picture-only headlines fail Schwartz's test.

See: [references/awareness-states.md](references/awareness-states.md) for all 5 stages with worked examples, the 7 fifth-stage tactics in full, and the case studies (Postum, Listerine, U.S. School of Music, I.C.S., Odorono, Marlboro).

### 3. Sophistication of the Market — escalation

**Core concept.** Sophistication is "How many similar products have they been told about before?" (p. 37). It is the easiest of the three diagnostic questions: "A few hours research should give you samples of every competing ad in the field — if there are any." (p. 37) Markets progress through five stages roughly in order. The same claim that breaks through a virgin market becomes invisible to a market that has heard it ten times. **Diagnose the stage; pick the matching headline strategy.**

**Why it works.** Desire never fades — only believability does. "The desire never fades. The dissatisfaction builds up… these women are hoping to find a new product — a new headline — that promises them a new way to satisfy that age-old desire." (p. 41)

**The Five Stages**

| # | Schwartz's term | Strategy | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **First in the market** (p. 37) | "Be simple. Be direct… Name either the need or the claim in your headline — nothing more." (p. 39) | "NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!" (p. 39) |
| 2 | **Second in market, claim still working** (p. 39) | Copy the proven claim but **enlarge it** — bigger numbers, bolder promises, broader comparisons. (p. 39) | "LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS — OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!" (p. 39); "WHO EVER HEARD OF 17,000 BLOOMS FROM A SINGLE PLANT?" (p. 40) |
| 3 | **Introduce a new mechanism** (p. 41) | Headline = mechanism; body restates claim. "Here the emphasis shifts from what the product does to HOW it works." (p. 42) | "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!" (p. 42); "FIRST WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING!" (p. 42) |
| 4 | **Elaborate the mechanism** (p. 44) | Embellish the now-accepted mechanism — easier, quicker, surer; remove old limitations; add benefits. | "FIRST NO-DIET REDUCING WONDER DRUG!" (p. 44) |
| 5 | **How to Revive a Dead Product** (p. 45) | Identification — "from the promise and the mechanism… to identification with the prospect himself." (p. 45) Cross-references Awareness Stage 5. | Postum: "WHY MEN CRACK…" (p. 45); Marlboro Virile Men (p. 47); cigarette market for women: "BLOW SOME MY WAY" (p. 48) |

**Worked example.** The cigarette industry through all five stages (pp. 46–47):
- Stage 1 (taste): "I'D WALK A MILE FOR A CAMEL!", "CHESTERFIELD — THEY SATISFY!"
- Stage 2 (broader comparison): "LIGHT UP A LUCKY, AND YOU WON'T MISS THE SWEETS THAT MAKE YOU FAT!"
- Stage 3 (new mechanisms): "LUCKIES — THEY'RE TOASTED!"; "PALL MALL'S GREATER LENGTH FILTERS THE SMOKE FURTHER!"; "CAMELS — PROTECT YOUR T-ZONE!"
- Stage 4 (mechanism elaboration): "PHILIP MORRIS — ALL THE HARSHNESS BAKED OUT!"; "NINE OUT OF TEN DOCTORS PREFER LUCKIES!"
- Stage 5 (identification): Marlboro Virile Men, post-health-claim ban.

Filter cigarettes then re-traced the entire 5-stage cycle within a few decades — Kent → Viceroy → Parliament → Tareyton → Winston/L&M (p. 49). "And so it goes. In industry after industry. The same life cycle for each market." (p. 50)

**Key insights:**
- Sophistication is a state of the *market*, not the product (p. 41).
- Stages 1–2 promise *what*; Stage 3+ promise *how* (p. 42).
- Stage 5 is the awareness problem in disguise — "The strategy is the same." (p. 45)
- The cigarette industry is the canonical full-stage walkthrough (pp. 46–49).

**Ethical boundary.** Schwartz adds an explicit Personal Note (p. 50): "I have tried to write a scientific study of advertising, without troubling the reader with whatever personal ethics I myself may observe… examples such as those detailed above must be listed, in all objectivity, as a business strategy that has and will solve competitive problems in a competitive industry. I include them here — not as recommendations, but as possible strategies to be chosen or rejected."

See: [references/sophistication.md](references/sophistication.md) for the full cigarette walkthrough, the 38 Verbalization Ways from Ch. 4, and the Creative Planning meta-rules from Ch. 5 (Echo Ads, motivation research, splinter markets, prevention headlines, the "five-to-ten words = 90% of the value" rule).

## Part 2 — The Seven Techniques of Breakthrough Copy

These are the body-copy mechanisms. The first three (Intensification / Identification / Gradualization) sell the three components of the prospect's mind (Desires / Identifications / Beliefs). The next three (Redefinition / Mechanization / Concentration) handle objections and competition. The seventh (Camouflage) borrows believability from the medium itself.

### 4. Technique #1: Intensification — sharpen desire

**Core concept.** Take the dominant desire and make it sharper, more vivid, more concrete by piling on a continuous series of fresh fulfillment-images. Existing desires are "indistinct… blurs — hazy, ambiguous, not yet crystallized into words or images" (pp. 77–78). The copywriter is "literally the script writer for your prospect's dreams. You are the chronicler of his future. Your job is to show him in minute detail all the tomorrows that your product makes possible for him." (p. 78)

**Why it works.** "The sharper you can draw your pictures — and the greater the number of them that you can legitimately present — the more your prospect will demand your product, and the less important will seem your price." (p. 78)

**Cardinal rule:** "You cannot repeat, but you can reinforce." (p. 79) Each new section must be a *fresh viewpoint* on the same dominant promise — never a restatement.

**The Thirteen Ways to Strengthen Desire** (Ch. 7, p. 77):

| # | Way | Page |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First Presentation of Your Claims (sensory description) | 80 |
| 2 | Put the Claims in Action (motion verbs) | 83 |
| 3 | Bring In the Reader (second-person hypothetical) | 85 |
| 4 | Show Him How to Test Your Claims (stepwise script) | 86 |
| 5 | Stretch Out Your Benefits in Time (chronological gates) | 87 |
| 6 | Bring In an Audience (testimonial / case history / case-history stack) | 89 |
| 7 | Show Experts Approving (staged contest of authorities) | 90 |
| 8 | Compare, Contrast, Prove Superiority (price-anchor, before-after) | 91 |
| 9 | Picture the Black Side, Too (heaven-or-hell pivot) | 92 |
| 10 | Show How Easy It Is To Get These Benefits (ease-refrain) | 94 |
| 11 | Use Metaphor, Analogy, Imagination (personification) | 94 |
| 12 | Before You're Done, Summarize (horizontal vs vertical catalog; rifle vs shotgun) | 95 |
| 13 | Put Your Guarantee to Work (numbered, refundable, performance-bound) | 99 |

**Copy patterns:**
- **Picture-to-yourself second person:** "Picture this to yourself! [Time anchor] you [reader action with familiar object]. You've made only one simple change… But now, when you [trigger], a [outcome]." (p. 85)
- **Stretched-time stages:** "First, [Stage 1 outcome] [Time 1]. But this is just the beginning! Within [Time 2] — without your even [effort] — [Mechanism] AUTOMATICALLY begins…" (pp. 87–88)
- **Heaven-or-Hell pivot:** "[Look at the old/inadequate thing]. What you'll see is [BLACK, FILTHY DRAMATIC NOUN]… Now look at the new [Product] — [brilliant headline]." (pp. 92–93)
- **Ease-refrain:** "With just [trivial-time] work, you will [Benefit 1]. With just [trivial-time] work, you will [Benefit 2]…" (p. 94)
- **Numbered guarantee climax:** Pair each performance promise with its own money-back clause. (Power Flash spark plugs, pp. 99–100)

**Mail order vs national campaigns.** Mail-order ads compress all 13 devices into one piece (p. 101). National campaigns spread Intensification across a *series*: "Your Central Campaign Idea, and its continuous restatement in a series of fresh disguises." (p. 102) Canonical campaigns (pp. 103–106): Colgate Gardol "Rule and Example", Toni "Which Twin", Clairol "Does She or Doesn't She?", Cadillac (quality theme carried by typography, photography, fashion), Volkswagen ("Think Small", "Lemon", "Our number one salesman" — "product as focal point" with absolutely bare backgrounds).

**Ethical boundary.** Schwartz uses one word as a guardrail: "the greater the number of [pictures] that you can **legitimately** present" (p. 78, emphasis added). The numbered guarantee structure carries an implicit contract: every guaranteed metric must be one the product can actually deliver, since each is independently refundable.

See: [references/intensification.md](references/intensification.md) for all 13 ways with worked examples (rose plant, jet model, fish lure, plant-food pellets, spark plugs, memory course, Sherwin Cody English Course) plus the four canonical national campaigns.

### 5. Technique #2: Identification — sell who he becomes

**Core concept.** Build a "saleable personality" into the product so owning it lets the prospect express the character role he wishes he had, or display the achievement role he has won. Identification is "a special form of desire — the desire, not for satisfaction, but for recognition." (p. 115) When products do "the same job as [their] competitors, and [are] so priced that price is no longer a factor, then the prospect's choice will almost overwhelmingly depend on the difference in role that your product offers him." (p. 115)

**Why it works.** Character roles are believed *more readily* than performance claims because they are "almost never claimed openly but only hinted at, implied and prompted, therefore they can never truly be tested or measured… Your prospect is far more ready to believe in the character roles you assign to him, than he is to believe in either your product's performance claims, or the achievement roles it may offer him." (p. 112)

**Two types of roles:**

| Type | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Character roles** (pp. 110–112) | Self-assigned, ambiguous, subconscious; "subject to fantasy" — never tested. | Virility implied by "a test pilot smoking a cigarette" — the prospect "easily persuades himself" smoking the same cigarette gives him "at least some of the implied virility of the genuine possessor." (p. 112) |
| **Achievement roles** (p. 113) | Status / class / position roles created by society and earned — but immaterial, so they "must first be translated into physical symbols of success, for everyone around us to see." | Men: "Executive", "Home Owner", "$20,000-a-year-Man", "Five Handicap", "Block Chairman"; Women (1960s framing): "Wife", "Fashion Setter", "Career Woman", "Good Mother", "Patron of the Arts." (p. 113) |

"In America today we are known — not only by the company we keep — but by the products we own." (p. 113) The newly-wed accepts a wedding ring "which has absolutely no function other than to define her" (p. 114); the "Man-On-His-Way-Up… trades in his Ford for a Buick, and, when he becomes an 'Executive,' trades in his Buick for a Cadillac." (p. 114)

**The two-step build** (pp. 117–119):
1. **Identify the primary image** the product already has (cigarettes → virile; piston rings → precision-machined; appliances → time-saving).
2. **Use that primary image as raw material.** Either *intensify* it through repetition with variation (Marlboro: virile model + virile situation + tattoo — "a single dominant emotion, virility, symbolized three ways"; p. 118) OR use it as a *bridge* to richer images.

**The substitution rule.** "People just won't believe that a product is what it isn't. You cannot contradict accepted images or beliefs in advertising." (p. 119) Instead: "incorporate them in a larger, overall image — lower their emotional intensity — and use them as readily-accepted bridges to lead your prospect into far more compelling appeals."

**The believability bridge.** "If you demand that your prospect jump across a believability-chasm, your ad will fail. If, however, you build a bridge of ideas or images across that chasm — starting on his side — then he will let you lead him almost anywhere." (p. 125)

**Visual implication beats verbal naming.** "Stated in words, these images must take on a measure of disbelief, which we express by saying they're 'corny.' However, expressed in visual terms, where they can be symbolized, and therefore implied rather than named, they enter into our minds unnoticed, and we accept them without question." (p. 123)

**Copy patterns:**
- **Primary-image emphasis:** Take the strongest already-accepted association; deepen it through repetition with variation (one dominant emotion, three symbolizations). (p. 118)
- **Bridge construction:** Start on his side, build inward. (p. 125)
- **Package-as-symbol:** Borrow the physical form of an authority context. (Micrin mouthwash: doctor's-office glass + ice-blue liquid = "this is medicine. This works." p. 127)

**Ethical boundary.** Schwartz is candid that character roles work *because* they are "subject to fantasy" and never tested (p. 112). This is descriptive, not prescriptive. The closest practical guardrail: "people just won't believe that a product is what it isn't" (p. 119) — not as ethics but as ineffectiveness. The deodorant-soap campaign that flopped (p. 124) used society-elite imagery on a mass market; the disbelief spread to the performance claims and killed the sales.

See: [references/identification.md](references/identification.md) for character-role catalog, achievement-role list, the Marlboro and Chesterfield "Blow Some My Way" build, the hypothetical piston-ring ad walkthrough, and the limits-of-image rule.

### 6. Technique #3: Gradualization — build belief

**Core concept.** "Gradualization is the art of stating a claim in such a way that it will receive the greatest possible acceptance and/or believability from your prospect." (p. 144) The structural insight: every claim has *two* sources of strength — its content, and the preparation that precedes it (p. 133). Therefore "one fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten partially-believed promises." (p. 144)

**Why it works.** "Effective advertising, like effective literature, is built — not of words — but of reactions… We are creating a stream of acceptances, with a definite sequence and content and direction, and, if we are successful, with a definite goal — the absolute conviction in your prospect's mind that he must have your product." (pp. 133–134)

**The basic rule of belief** (p. 131): "If you violate your prospect's established beliefs in the slightest degree — either in content or direction — then nothing you promise him, no matter how appealing, can save your ad. But… if you can channel the tremendous force of his belief… behind only one claim, no matter how small, then that one fully-believed claim will sell more goods than all the half-questioned promises your competitors can write for all the rest of their days. This channeling of belief is so powerful that, if properly directed, it will even support otherwise-absurd claims… It is simply a question of whether you are going to paddle upstream or down."

**A new definition of awareness** (p. 134): **Readiness to accept.** "The effectiveness of your headline is as much determined by the willingness of your audience to believe what it says, as it is by the promises it makes." This is why power-claim headlines often lose money in mail-order tests — and why advertisers replace them with smaller-claim, more-believable heads, then "build a belief-bridge from this second headline, to the same exact claims they had featured in the first, but now anticipated by careful preparation every step along the way." (p. 135)

**Six belief-building tools** (pp. 145–151):

| Tool | What it does | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **Inclusion Question** | Direct yes-question stack ("Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947?") that tags the prospect into the Yes-group. | 145 |
| **Detailed Identification** | Catalogue the prospect's specific symptoms so he knows you're talking about him. | 145 |
| **Contradiction of Present (False) Beliefs** | "From this moment on, forget everything you have ever heard about…" — used with strong authority. | 146 |
| **The Language of Logic** | Phrase armory: "Therefore…", "Here's why…", "And, most important of all…", "They discovered — in case after case — that…" | 147 |
| **Syllogistic Thinking** | Major premise → minor premise → inevitable conclusion. (Spark-plug ad: bigger spark = more power; our plug = bigger spark; p. 149) | 149 |
| **Other belief forms** | Contingency structures ("If… then…"), Echoing repetition, Promise-Belief-Promise variation, Paragraph Parallelism. | 150 |

**Worked example — the 1951 TV Repair Manual ad** (pp. 135–143). The obvious power headline ("Save up to $100 a Year on Your TV Repairs!") tested and lost money — "Power alone could not move the product." (p. 136) The replacement: **"WHY HAVEN'T TV OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS?"** — no claim, only the implication of foul play, which echoed the prospect's existing suspicion. Then sentence 2 (the Inclusion Question): "Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947?" — 95% answer Yes. Then sentence 3 (the first definite promise) lands on already-accepted ground. Then a Yes-cascade of universal symptoms, then "90% Of These Breakdowns Are Unnecessary!", then graphic test descriptions, and finally the syllogistic close: **"It's as simple as that. You pay a repairman — not for his work — but for his knowledge. If you had that knowledge yourself — then you would not have to pay him at all."** (p. 143)

**Copy patterns:**
- **Inclusion-Question stack:** "Do you find it increasingly difficult to…? Is your … a source of …? Are you tormented by…?" (p. 145, *The Art of Selfishness* ad)
- **Yes-cascade by universal symptoms:** "How many times this week have you had to…? How many times have you had to…?" (pp. 138–139)
- **Authority-led contradiction:** "From this moment on, forget everything you have ever heard or read about…" (p. 146)
- **Syllogistic close:** State the major premise, the minor premise, drop the inevitable conclusion. ("It's as simple as that…", p. 143)
- **Smuggle-the-new-claim:** Paragraph parallelism + lead word "And" + "most important" + repeated identifying subhead — so the new claim rides on the accepted stream. (p. 142)

**Ethical boundary.** Schwartz is explicit and candid: "structure can support otherwise-absurd claims" (p. 131). His one practical guardrail is the **accuracy rule** for Detailed Identification: "your copy must be accurate. You must know enough about the reader's problems to make every word you write ring true. If you don't, you'll shatter your believability-net, and he'll simply turn the page." (p. 146) The implicit defense: structural belief-building only works while the underlying facts hold up; an absurd claim might survive the first read but won't survive the experience.

See: [references/gradualization.md](references/gradualization.md) for the full TV-Repair anatomy paragraph by paragraph, the language-of-logic phrase armory, the syllogistic-thinking structure, and the four other belief forms.

### 7. Technique #4: Redefinition — remove objections

**Core concept.** Where Gradualization works "by its arrangement of facts and phrases," Redefinition works "by its rearrangement of perspective" (p. 164). It is the tool you reach for when a product has built-in handicaps that "actually repel the prospect" (p. 153).

**Three drawback categories — three sub-techniques:**

| Drawback | Sub-technique | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Too complicated / too hard | **Simplification** (p. 155) | Redefine the loaded word *before* you use it. ("Repairs" → "adjustments"; "breakdowns" → "warning signals"; pp. 157–158) |
| Not important enough | **Escalation** (p. 160) | Broaden the role — make it the *means* to a more-wanted end, or pit the small price against a large prior expenditure. (Spark plugs: "$2,000 car / 99¢ part", p. 161) |
| Costs too much | **Price Reduction** (p. 162) | Switch the comparison standard upward (your $1.49 product compared to a $5 hand-made or $30–$40 set, p. 162); use "you can have" not "you can buy" (p. 164). |

**Classic example — Lifebuoy / B.O.** (p. 154): the soap had a "horrible medicinal odor" that couldn't be removed without killing its cleaning power. Redefinition flipped it: the prospect's *own* body odor became the enemy; the medicinal smell became proof of "odor-destroying power." "A simple concept-judo. A complete reversal. Turning a liability into an asset, with a single idea."

**Sherwin Cody — "Only 15 Minutes a Day"** (pp. 159–160): English-course prospects were "absolutely convinced that good English was too hard for them." The copy redefined the field with concrete, astonishing facts: *69 words make up more than half of all speech and letter writing*; *only one dozen fundamental principles of punctuation*; *25 typical errors constitute nine-tenths of all everyday grammar mistakes*. "Thus the complicated becomes simple — the hard becomes easy." (p. 160)

**Copy patterns:**
- **Liability-into-asset flip** ("concept-judo"). (Lifebuoy, p. 154)
- **Redefine the loaded word.** ("Repairs" → "adjustments"; p. 157)
- **Shrink the field with concrete numbers.** ("only 69 words…", p. 159)
- **Cost-to-stake ratio.** Pit small price against large prior spend. (p. 161)
- **Switch the comparison standard upward.** (p. 162)
- **"You can have" rather than "you can buy."** Gift framing. (p. 164)

**Ethical boundary.** "A new breakthrough is not merely accepted because its manufacturer says so. Its claims for ease and simplicity must be proved, in the ad, or the reader will simply shrug his shoulders and say 'it's just another copywriter gone wild.'" (p. 158) Redefinition without proof fails — pair it with Mechanization.

See: [references/redefinition.md](references/redefinition.md) for the SA Fire Injectors phrase-by-phrase price-reduction dissection and the TV Repair Manual full simplification analysis.

### 8. Technique #5: Mechanization — verbal proof

**Core concept.** Mechanization answers the prospect's third inevitable demand — *How does it work?* — alongside "Tell me more" and "Oh yeah? Who says so?" (p. 166). It is the heir to Claude Hopkins's "Reason Why" copy. The question is not whether to use it but **how much** — governed by the prospect's State of Awareness (p. 167).

**Three doses:**

| Dose | When | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Name the mechanism** (p. 167) | The mechanism is already familiar — competitors made it familiar for you. Name and move on. | "TAKE FOOLPROOF PHOTOS WITH THE XENOPHON 1750 — With Electronic Light Setter… Push-Button Zomar Lens… Magazine Load… Only $135." (p. 168) |
| **Describe the mechanism** (p. 168) | Prospect doesn't understand it, OR everyone has the same mechanism and you need a fresh way to compete. Promise → Reason Why one-two punch. | Rinso 1926 (p. 169): promise paragraphs ("Who else wants a whiter wash?") followed by mechanism paragraphs ("a mild, granulated soap that gives rich, lasting suds even in the hardest water"). |
| **Feature the mechanism** (p. 170) | Highly sophisticated market; mechanism *is* the headline. | "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY"; "SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY"; "RUN YOUR CAR WITHOUT SPARK PLUGS" (p. 170). The framing: **A NEW WAY** — "a new chance to satisfy your desire — even if everything else you've tried has failed you." (p. 171) |

**Mechanism for bargain.** "Many manufacturers are startled when they cut a price — advertise the reduction — and see no increase in sales. What happened? No one believed them. A price cut — like a product advantage — is only as good as your words, and your strategy, makes it." (p. 171) "Price cuts must be justified. There must be a reason for them. A mechanism behind them. Without such a mechanism — without such a reason-why you should give this bargain — you are going to get only a fraction of its real sales power." (p. 172)

Robert Collier's "Before the Price Goes Up!" Madras shirt letter (p. 172) is the canonical case: unseasonable weather → mill overstocked → S.O.S. calls → quarter-million yards offered "below any price we had ever paid." Schwartz calls it **"a mechanism within a mechanism"** — chained reason-whys building belief upon belief.

**Copy patterns:**
- **Name-only headline:** PROMISE WITH [MECHANISM 1] · [MECHANISM 2] · [MECHANISM 3] · [PRICE]. (p. 168)
- **Promise → Reason-Why two-step.** Stack quick promise paragraphs, then mechanism paragraphs that explain why the promise must be true. (Rinso, p. 169)
- **Mechanism-as-headline.** Lead with HOW, the *what* lives in body. ("Floats fat right out of your body!", p. 170)
- **"A NEW WAY" framing.** Position the mechanism as a fresh chance after every prior failure. (p. 171)
- **Chained reason-whys.** Mechanism within a mechanism — each layer makes the next more believable. (Collier, p. 172)

**Ethical boundary.** "You must never allow [mechanism copy] to become dull, or merely factual. You must load it with promise, load it with emotion. Every word in good copy — including mechanism copy — sells." (pp. 169–170) The implicit guardrail: the mechanism must be real enough that emotion-loaded copy doesn't outrun it.

See: [references/mechanization.md](references/mechanization.md) for the Xenophon 1750 / Rinso / Power Flash mechanism progressions and Collier's Madras-shirt mechanism-within-a-mechanism.

### 9. Technique #6: Concentration — destroy alternatives

**Core concept.** Concentration is *direct attack* on alternative ways your prospect could satisfy his desire. It is the fifth competitive weapon, distinct from product superiority, promise superiority, product-role, and response/reaction (pp. 175–176). Where the other four "ignore the competition," Concentration confronts it. **Use it when** you have a smaller budget than the dominant competitor, when most prospects are already his customers, or when you must crack his image and shatter loyalty before you can rechannel desire (p. 177).

**Core rule.** "If you can only attack another product — without showing at the same time, by comparison, how your product provides what the other lacks — then say nothing at all! Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time." (p. 177) Naked disparagement evokes "skepticism and dislike, and very little else." (p. 177)

**Two structures:**

| Structure | When | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Bad–Good interleave** (pp. 178–180) | Points break down cleanly, one-for-one. | "A spark plug jumps a spark of electricity across an air gap. This is the most wasteful and power consuming way…" (Bad) / "A fire injector fires on the surface of an electrical conductor. This is the most efficient way to get a big powerful spark…" (Good). Repeat. End with authority capstone (e.g., U.S. Air Force premium prices). |
| **Time-sequence** (p. 181) | Points don't decompose cleanly. Use chronological staging. | Phase 1 — *what happens to you now* with current alternatives (Lecithin reducing pill: "you struggled to do the job of reducing BY YOURSELF! You took your hunger-appeasing pills religiously…"). Phase 2 — *what happens when you switch* (return to promise + mechanism + case history). |

**Devices:**
- **Word-image contrast pairs.** "Spark" (weak) vs "flame" (strong); "thin skimpy spark" vs "heavy, powerful flame." (p. 180)
- **Definition / redefinition baked into every claim.** ("Most wasteful and power consuming way" vs "most efficient way"; p. 180)
- **Documentary "because…" tail on every benefit.** ("Because the electrode is burning away…")
- **Universal-experience cause/effect.** "So what happened?" lets the prospect nod through every line. (Lecithin ad, p. 182)
- **Authority capstone after the attack sequence.** (U.S. Air Force, p. 179)

**Short-form Concentration.** Sometimes a single headline does it. "SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY" — "Here is contrast — implied weakness in other products — compensating promise in your own." (p. 184)

**Ethical boundary.** Schwartz draws his clearest competitive guardrail here: never attack without supplying the solution. Naked disparagement — attacking only for your own benefit — produces skepticism and dislike (p. 177). And: do not use Concentration when "your story is so powerful, so different, or so fresh that the competition has nothing to match it… it's better not to give him the prestige of attack." (p. 177)

See: [references/concentration.md](references/concentration.md) for the full fire-injector Bad/Good sequence, the Lecithin time-sequence dissection, and the case for and against attack copy.

### 10. Technique #7: Camouflage — borrow believability

**Core concept.** Borrow believability "from all the places in our society where it is stored up" (p. 185) — chiefly the publications, networks, and editorial channels prospects already trust. The mechanism is a **conditioned reflex**: a reader trusts a publication because he has bought it repeatedly; "after a while [its] phraseology begins to carry an aura of truth all by itself no matter what material it embraces." (p. 186) When you mimic the host medium's format and idiom, your ad inherits that "stored believability… a believability reflex." (p. 186)

**Three borrowing strategies + two mood adaptations:**

| # | Strategy | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Adopt format** (pp. 186–190) | Let the publication set your ad in its own type, sub-heads, illustrations. "A single change in format can add 50% to your readership." (p. 187) | "How to Make Anybody Like You!" — same copy re-set in *Wall Street Journal* style, ran 19× monthly with no drop in pull. (pp. 187–190) |
| 2 | **Adopt phraseology** (pp. 190–191) | Pick up the medium's stereotyped phrases. Newspapers: issue date, city of origin, by-line. | "SKIN SPECIALIST DEMONSTRATES… By Claire Hoffman. New York, N.Y. — A leading doctor today showed an audience of men, women and skin-troubled teenagers…" (p. 191) |
| 3 | **Mood — Understatement** (pp. 192–193) | Few adjectives. Short sentences. Sentences that *fall* in tone. Self-kidding. | Volkswagen: "You never run out of air. You also won't have any worries about draining or flushing the radiator in spring. There is no radiator. Or hoses. Or water pump. Or rust." (pp. 192–193) |
| 4 | **Mood — Deadly Sincerity** (p. 193) | Lean over backward to point out the flaws first; positive claims then carry factual-report believability. | "TO THE MAN WHO WILL SETTLE FOR NOTHING LESS THAN THE PRESIDENCY OF HIS FIRM… This is a private advertisement. It is not meant for ninety-nine men out of every hundred." (pp. 193–194) |

**Cross-medium application** (p. 191):
- **Direct mail** — mimic refund checks, government correspondence, dividend notices, confidential reports.
- **Radio / TV** — clipped newscaster cadence, hand-held camera feel, insider-analyst tone.

**Ethical boundary.** Schwartz's framing: trust is *spent* by the ad. "If [the reader] no longer believes in the publication, he won't believe in the advertising it carries." (p. 186) And on attack copy in Concentration: the attack must be *for the prospect's good*, not "to win the sale by disparaging your competitor." (p. 177) Modern reader caveat: the "adopt format / adopt phraseology" strategies in particular blur the editorial / advertisement distinction and now sit squarely in FTC native-advertising disclosure rules; in 1966 Schwartz treats this purely as craft optimization.

See: [references/camouflage.md](references/camouflage.md) for the full WSJ "How to Make Anybody Like You" line-by-line adaptation, the Volkswagen Understatement analysis, and the Deadly Sincerity opener templates.

## Final Touches — Reassembling the Living Ad

Chapter 14 is the seventh-and-a-half technique: six craft skills that turn the analyzed ad back into a single fluid document. **The goal is intuition, not phrase-carpentry.** Schwartz calls the awkward phase "Creativity #1" (the golf-pro stage where you're conscious of every move) and the destination "Creativity #2" — writing from the full depth of emotion *and* mind, no longer conscious of the mechanisms (pp. 202–203).

| Touch | What it does | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **Verification** | Place documentation *anywhere in the copy where the reader is begging for an answer* — not in a labeled "Here's Proof" box. | 196 |
| **Reinforcement** | Two emotions joined the right way produce the impact of ten. Avis: "We try harder" + "We're only second" → "(1) We're second; (2) Therefore we try harder." | 200 |
| **Interweaving** | Every sentence carries promise + image + logic + emotion. (Walter S. Campbell rule: even pure factual reporting fails if the sentence has only the facts; p. 203) | 202 |
| **Sensitivity** | Architecture-level empathy — anticipate the reader's silent "I've read enough about this. Give me this instead." Then *shift direction*. | 205 |
| **Momentum** | Two devices: time-honored momentum-phrases ("Here's how", "Let me explain", "And yet it's only the beginning") and incomplete-statement teasers that raise questions you'll answer later. | 215 |
| **Mood** | Words and rhythm. Two kinds of mood-words: image-sharpeners (visual) and emotion-definers (evaluative). Both must stay invisible to the reader; if they're visible, you've lost him. | 222 |

**The most actionable single rule from Chapter 14:** **"A new structure is a new ad."** (p. 215) "If you take the same content, and give it a new structure, you are going to create a new ad. Try it. See for yourself. If you're stuck sometime, throw away your headline, throw away your rough copy flow, and start off again 180 degrees away from where you started before." (p. 215)

**Diagnostic test:** "Could you put another brand-name on it, and not make any difference?" (p. 215) — if yes, rewrite.

See: [references/final-touches.md](references/final-touches.md) for the four sample-ad architecture analyses (Parker popularity book, Lorayne mind-power, Schwartz's own "Twice as Smart" ad, Franklyn beauty book), the momentum-phrase library, and the Volkswagen Understatement scan.

## The Schwartz Process (end-to-end)

Apply in order. The first five chapters of the book describe a process that "may take days, or weeks, or months to complete" (p. 66) — most of the work happens *before* you write a word of copy.

1. **Inventory the mass desires.** List every desire the product touches. (Ch. 1, p. 11)
2. **Score each on three dimensions.** Urgency × Staying power × Scope. Pick the one that has the most power *right now*. "If it is wrong, nothing else that you do in the ad will matter." (p. 8)
3. **Diagnose the State of Awareness.** Place the prospect on the 5-state ladder. (Ch. 2)
4. **Diagnose the State of Sophistication.** Audit competing ads. Place the market on the 5-stage ladder. (Ch. 3)
5. **Pick the headline strategy** at the intersection of awareness × sophistication. (Schwartz's "point of entry", p. 66)
6. **Verbalize the headline.** Use the 38 Ways (Ch. 4) only as guideposts — not formulas. Five to ten words = 90% of the value. (p. 67)
7. **Body copy: Intensification.** Pile fresh fulfillment-images of the dominant desire. Apply the 13 Ways. (Ch. 7)
8. **Body copy: Identification.** Build the saleable personality. Match a character/achievement role to the prospect; bridge from his side. (Ch. 8)
9. **Body copy: Gradualization.** Build belief through Inclusion-Question / Yes-cascade / Logic phrasing / Syllogistic close. Smuggle new claims into the accepted stream. (Ch. 9)
10. **Handle objections via Redefinition.** Simplify, escalate, or reduce price by reframing. (Ch. 10)
11. **Add Mechanization** at the dose appropriate to awareness — name, describe, or feature the mechanism. Justify any price cut. (Ch. 11)
12. **If competition is dominant: Concentration.** Bad/Good or time-sequence; never attack without providing the solution. (Ch. 12)
13. **Camouflage.** Adopt the medium's format and phraseology; consider Understatement or Deadly Sincerity to escape the "advertising language" reflex. (Ch. 13)
14. **Apply Final Touches.** Place proof where the reader demands it. Reinforce. Interweave. Test for satiation. Add momentum. Tune mood. (Ch. 14)
15. **Diagnose the architecture.** Could another brand-name swap in? Is documentation buried in a "Here's Proof" box? If stuck, throw away the headline and start 180° from where you began.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Fighting a mass desire instead of riding it.** | "He can only exploit it. And he dies when he tries to run against it." (p. 3) | Inventory the desires already moving in the market; pick the one with most urgency × staying power × scope; channel it. (pp. 7–8) |
| **Headlining the physical product instead of the functional benefit.** | "No physical part of your product can ever become a headline. No one will buy the size of your client's plant…" (p. 9) | Lead with what the product *does*; use physical facts later to justify price, document quality, sharpen the picture. (pp. 9–10) |
| **Asking the headline to make the sale.** | The selling burden gets crammed into "10% to 20% of the total physical space." (p. 14) | "Your headline has only one job — to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence." (p. 14) |
| **Using a Stage-1 price/name headline on a Stage-3 market.** | The market doesn't know the category exists yet. "'Dial Soap — 9¢ a cake' will fail completely…" (p. 24) | Diagnose awareness first; write at the prospect's actual point of awareness. |
| **Stage-5 headline that's just a startler / cute / pretty picture.** | "Most copy writers use a fifth stage problem to write an empty headline, and are therefore simply wasting their client's money." (p. 35) | Identification headline. "Your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it." (p. 35) |
| **Echo Ads (word-substitute headlines).** | "They pay no attention to the unique product-market-timing relationship that exists at the moment." (p. 60) | Probe analytically; the unique solution lives only inside the unique product-market-timing relationship. (p. 67) |
| **Repeating instead of reinforcing.** | "You will lose your reader in the middle of your ad." (p. 79) | Each new section = same promise, *fresh* viewpoint. Re-read days later; cut anything that reads as repetition. (p. 96) |
| **Forcing an unrealistic identification.** | The mass deodorant-soap campaign with society imagery — "the disbelief spread to the performance claims themselves, and killed the sales." (p. 124) | Build a believability bridge from the prospect's side; never demand the leap. (p. 125) |
| **Leading with the most powerful claim regardless of readiness.** | The TV-Repair power headline lost money. "Power alone could not move the product." (p. 136) | Headline at the prospect's *readiness to accept* (p. 134); build the belief-bridge to the bigger claim. |
| **Piling promise on promise.** | "Most copy writers try to strengthen ads by piling promise upon promise. What they usually get for their troubles is greater sales resistance." (p. 144) | "One fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten partially-believed promises." (p. 144) |
| **Cutting price without a reason-why.** | "No one believed them." (p. 171) | Justify every discount with a mechanism. (p. 172) |
| **Attack-only Concentration.** | "If you are attacking another product only for your own good… what you will probably evoke in his mind is skepticism and dislike." (p. 177) | Pair every attack with the demonstration that *your* product solves the attacked weakness. (p. 177) |
| **Visible mood-words.** | When the audience consciously notices "blinded… explosion… spellbound," the copy becomes corny and you've lost them. | Match tone to audience class; downshift as well as upshift. If *everybody* sees your emotion-words, rewrite. (pp. 223–224) |
| **Generic copy that any brand could run.** | Wasted page. "Could you put another brand-name on it, and not make any difference?" (p. 215) | New structure = new ad. Throw away the headline, start 180° from where you began. (p. 215) |

## Quick Diagnostic

Audit any ad / sales letter / landing page against these questions, in this order:

| Question | If No | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Have you named the dominant mass desire (urgency × staying × scope)? | Headline has no engine. | Inventory the desires; pick one. (Ch. 1) |
| Does the headline start at the prospect's actual State of Awareness? | Wrong rung — psychological wall. | Diagnose 1–5; rewrite the headline at that exact rung. (Ch. 2) |
| Is the headline strategy matched to Sophistication stage? | Stage-1 headline in a Stage-3 market = invisible. | Audit competing ads; promote *what* (Stage 1–2) or *how* (Stage 3+) accordingly. (Ch. 3) |
| Does each body section reinforce the *same* dominant promise from a fresh viewpoint? | Repetition fatigue; reader quits. | Apply the 13 Ways of Intensification; cut any section that reads as restatement. (Ch. 7) |
| Does the product carry a saleable identity the prospect would *display*? | Parity product loses on price. | Build a primary image; bridge from his side. (Ch. 8) |
| Is the prospect *ready to accept* every claim by the time it's stated? | Power-claim falls on cold ear. | Inclusion Question → Yes-cascade → smuggled new claim → syllogistic close. (Ch. 9) |
| Is every objection reframed before the prospect names it? | Resistance hardens. | Simplification / Escalation / Price Reduction. (Ch. 10) |
| Is there a mechanism for every claim and every price cut? | Discount under-converts. | Name / describe / feature the mechanism. (Ch. 11) |
| If the competitor dominates, do you attack — and supply the solution? | Naked attack = skepticism. | Bad/Good interleave or time-sequence. Never attack without solving. (Ch. 12) |
| Does the ad mimic the host medium's format and phraseology? | "Advertising language" reflex kills trust. | Adopt format + phraseology + Understatement / Deadly Sincerity. (Ch. 13) |
| Is documentation placed where the reader is *demanding* it (not in a "Here's Proof" box)? | Proof under-pulls. | Verification — interweave proof at peak demand. (Ch. 14) |
| Could another brand-name swap into this copy with no change? | Generic. Wasted page. | New structure = new ad. Start 180° from where you began. (p. 215) |

## About the Author

**Eugene M. Schwartz (1927–1995)** was a direct-response copywriter who, per the Boardroom foreword by Martin Edelston, "wrote advertising copy for the best direct marketers in America" (p. iii). He published *How to Double Your Child's Grades in School* (1964) and *How to Double Your Power to Learn* before *Breakthrough Advertising* in 1966; suffered a stroke in 1978 and continued working — eventually becoming a Boardroom consultant who "helped very much in the creation of the Bottom Line/Personal concept" (p. iv). With his wife Barbara, he assembled a major art collection (Hans Hofmann, Frank Stella, Donald Judd, Milton Avery), portions of which now reside at the Art Institute of Chicago (p. iv). The book itself was, per Schwartz's own preface, used "by men who were not in the business of advertising at all, to make more money than most of us ever dream of accumulating." (p. v)
