# State of Awareness — extraction notes (Ch 2, book pp. 13–36)

## Core concept

Before a single word of the headline is written, the copywriter must locate the prospect on a five-rung "awareness scale" — i.e. how much the prospect already knows about the desire, the problem, the solution-class, the product, and the product's specific superiority. The headline begins exactly at that point of awareness, and the copy walks the prospect from that point inward to the product. "The more aware your market, the easier the selling job, the less you need to say" (p. 16). As awareness drops, the headline's mechanics give way to creative execution, and the copywriter's verbal craft (naming, crystallizing, identification) carries more and more of the sale.

The chapter is built on three diagnostic questions (p. 14):
1. "What is the mass desire that creates this market?"
2. "How much do these people know today about the way your product satisfies this desire? (Their State of Awareness.)"
3. "How many other products have been presented to them before yours? (Their State of Sophistication.)"

## The Headline's Real Job (p. 14)

> "Your headline does not need to sell at all. It does not have to mention your product. It does not even have to mention your main appeal." (p. 14)

> "Your headline has only one job — to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence of your ad. In exactly the same way, your second sentence has only one job — to force him to read the third sentence of your ad. And the third sentence — and every additional sentence in your ad — has exactly the same job." (pp. 14–15)

> "It is simply common sense that the more of your story you can force your prospect to read, the more thoroughly you can sell him." (p. 15)

Exception: "Only to prospects actively seeking the client's specific brand-name product, and in a case where you can offer them a special price reduction, can your headline do the full selling job." (p. 15)

The headline begins at the prospect's "precise point of awareness" (p. 15): "It is at this precise point of awareness that your headline begins."

## The Five States of Awareness

Schwartz introduces them as awareness "stages," walked from most aware (mechanical) to least aware (creative): "We'll start at the Most Aware — the most mechanical selling job — and proceed to more and more difficult problems, requiring more and more creative solutions." (p. 16)

---

### 1. The Most Aware (p. 16)

**Schwartz's exact term.** "1. The Most Aware" (p. 16).

**Definition.** "The customer knows of your product — knows what it does — knows he wants it. At this point, he just hasn't gotten around to buying it yet." (p. 16)

**Headline strategy.** "Your headline — in fact, your entire ad — need state little more except the name of your product and a bargain price." (p. 16) "The remainder of the advertisement can summarize quickly the most desirable selling points. Then add the name of a store, or a coupon, and close." (p. 16)

**Example.** "Revere Zomar Lens, Electric Eye Camera — Formerly $149.50 — Now Only $119.95." (p. 16)

**Copy pattern.** `[Product name] — [former price] — Now Only [new price].` Plus brief recap of selling points + store/coupon + close (p. 16).

**Note on craft.** "Here the copy writer is nothing more than the merchandise manager's phrase-maker. The price is the most important part of his headline. There is nothing creative about his job, and he should receive the lowest possible scale of pay." (p. 16) — This is "the typical department store, discount store, mail-order-bargain-catalog type of advertising." (p. 16)

### 2. The Customer Knows of the Product But Doesn't Yet Want It (pp. 16–19)

**Schwartz's exact term.** "2. The Customer Knows of the Product But Doesn't Yet Want It" (p. 16). This is the bulk of all advertising: "Here you are dealing with a product which is known — which has established a brand name — which has already-linked itself with an acknowledged public desire, and has proven that it satisfied that desire." (p. 17)

**Definition.** "Here, your prospect isn't completely aware of all your product does, or isn't convinced of how well it does it, or hasn't yet been told how much better it does it now." (p. 16)

**Headline strategy — the seven tasks (p. 17).** "Here your headline is faced with one of seven tasks":
- (a) "To reinforce your prospect's desire for your product;"
- (b) "To sharpen his image of the way your product satisfies that desire;"
- (c) "To extend his image of where and when your product satisfies that desire;"
- (d) "To introduce new proof, details, documentation of how well your product satisfies that desire;"
- (e) "To announce a new mechanism in that product to enable it to satisfy that desire ev[en] better;"
- (f) "To announce a new mechanism in your product that eliminates former limitations;"
- (g) "Or to completely change the image or the mechanism of that product, in order to remove it from the competition of other products claiming to satisfy the same desire." (p. 17)

**The structural rule.** "In all seven cases, the approach is the same. You display the name of the product — either in the headline or in an equally large logo — and use the remainder of the headline to point out its superiority. The body of the ad is then an elaboration of that superiority — including visualization, documentation, mechanization. When you have finished weaving in every strand of your product's superiority, your ad is done." (p. 17)

**Examples by task.**
- (a) Reinforce desire — ASSOCIATION: "Steinway — The Instrument of the Immortals." / "Joy — The Costliest Perfume in the World." (p. 17). EXAMPLE: "Which Twin Has the Toni?" / "Hair Coloring So Natural Only Her Hairdresser Knows For Sure — Miss Clairol." (p. 18). SENSORY SHARPENING: "Tastes like you just picked it — Dole." / "The skin YOU love to touch — Woodbury" (p. 18). ILLUSTRATION: Life Saver's "Please don't lick this page." (p. 18)
- (b) Sharpen image of the way it satisfies: "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in a Rolls Royce is the electric clock." / "The amazing story of a Zippo that worked after being taken from the belly of a fish." (p. 18)
- (c) Extend image of where/when: "Anywhere you go, Hertz is always nearby" / "Thirst knows no season — Coca Cola." (p. 18)
- (d) Introduce new proof/documentation: "9 out of 10 screen stars use Lux Toilet Soap for their priceless smooth skins." / "Jake La Motta, 160-lb fighter, fails to flatten Mono paper cup." / "In Boston, the #1 tea-drinking city, the #1 tea is Salada." (pp. 18–19)
- (e) New mechanism to satisfy desire better: "Hoovers new invention washes floors and vacuums up the scrub water." / "Worlds only dog food that makes its own gravy — Gaines Gravy Train [sic? — OCR shows 'Graw']." (p. 19)
- (f) New mechanism that eliminates former limitations: "You breathe no dusty odors when YOU do it with Lewyt." / "A new Zenith hearing aid — inconspicuous beyond belief." (p. 19)
- (g) Completely change image/mechanism — "All of Chapter 3 will be devoted to some of the approaches to its solution." (p. 19)

**Copy pattern.** `[PRODUCT NAME] — [statement of superiority on dimension a–g].` Body = elaboration of that superiority via visualization + documentation + mechanization (p. 17).

### 3. How to Introduce New Products (Solution-aware / Desire-aware but doesn't know your product) (pp. 19–21)

**Schwartz's exact term.** "3. How to Introduce New Products" (p. 19).

**Definition.** "The prospect either knows, or recognizes immediately, that he wants what the product does; but he doesn't yet know that there is a product — your product — that will do it for him." (p. 19)

**Headline strategy.** Two-fold problem: "First, to pinpoint the ill-defined, as-yet-uncrystallized desire that is slowly spreading through great masses of people all over America. And second, to crystallize that desire, and its solution, so sharply and so dramatically that each and every prospect will recognize it at a glance." (p. 20)

Three-step process: "Name the desire and/or its solution in your headline. Prove that that solution can be accomplished. And show that the mechanism of that accomplishment is contained in your product." (p. 20)

**Required attributes of the copywriter (p. 20).** "First, analysis... Second, intuition... And third, verbal creativity... The ability to give a name to the still-undefined. To capture a feeling, a hope, a desire, a fear in words. To create a catchword or a slogan. To focus emotion, and give it a goal." (p. 20)

**Examples — amorphous desire crystallized in a single headline (p. 21):**
- "Light a Lucky, and you'll never miss sweets that make you fat."
- "Who else wants a whiter wash — with no hard work?"
- "How to win friends and influence people."
- "To men who want to quit work some day."
- "When doctors feel rotten — this is what they do."
- "Now! Run your car without spark plugs."
- "Who ever heard of 17,000 blooms [sic? — OCR garbled] from a single plant?"

**Pattern.** "Here, amorphous desire has been crystallized in the headline. Then sharpened and expanded in the first few paragraphs; satisfied and documented in the body of the ad: and focussed inevitably on the product throughout." (p. 21)

**Copy patterns (p. 21).**
- Naked statement of the desire: `How to [achieve desire].` ("How to win friends and influence people")
- Reinforced by fresh proof: `When [credible authority] feel [problem] — this is what they do.`
- By mystery: `Now! [Surprising claim/elimination of expected step]!`
- By wonderment: `Who ever heard of [astonishing result]?`
- "Who else wants [desired outcome] — with no [expected sacrifice]?"
- "To [identity] who want to [aspiration]."

### 4. How to Introduce Products That Solve Needs (Problem-aware) (pp. 21–23)

**Schwartz's exact term.** "4. How to Introduce Products That Solve Needs" (p. 21).

**Definition.** "The prospect has — not a desire — but a need. He recognizes the need immediately. But he doesn't yet realize the connection between the fulfillment of that need and your product." (p. 21)

**Headline strategy.** "This is the problem-solving ad... Here you start by naming the need and/or its solution in your headline. Then dramatize the need so vividly that the prospect realizes just how badly he needs the solution. And then present your product as the inevitable solution." (pp. 21–22)

**Examples (pp. 22–23).**
- Need alone, simplest possible form: "Corns?" (p. 22)
- Problem and solution stated together: "Stops maddening itch." (p. 22)
- Removal of previously unconquerable limitations (esp. catalog selling): "Lets portable transistor radios play on ordinary household current." (p. 22)
- Problem + solution + removal of expected limitation: "Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery." (p. 22)
- Substitute for unpleasant/expensive task: "Now! A ring and piston job in a tube!" (p. 22)
- Prevent a future problem: "Look, Mom! No cavities!" (p. 23)
- Emotional sharpening of an already-identified image (when the problem is vague — e.g. embarrassment over poor English): "Do YOU make these mistakes in English?" (p. 23)
- Verbal twist when the direct claim has lost freshness: "How a bald-headed barber helped save my hair." (p. 23)

**Copy patterns.**
- `[Problem]?` (single-word need statement, p. 22)
- `Stops [vivid symptom].` (p. 22)
- `Shrinks [problem] without [expected unpleasant method].` (p. 22)
- `Now! [Substitute] in a [convenient form]!` (p. 22)
- `Do YOU make these [mistakes/errors] in [domain]?` (p. 23)
- `How a [unlikely-source] helped [achieve solution].` (p. 23)

### 5. How to Open Up a Completely Unaware Market (pp. 23–35)

**Schwartz's exact term.** "5. How to Open Up a Completely Unaware Market" (p. 23). Schwartz also calls this "the fifth stage" (pp. 25, 34).

**Definition.** "The prospect is either not aware of his desire or his need — or he won't honestly admit it to himself without being lead [sic — OCR] into it by your ad — or the need is so general and amorphous that it resists being summed up in a single headline — or it's a secret that just can't be verbalized." (p. 23)

> "This is the outer reach of the awareness scale. These are the people who are still the logical prospects for your product; and yet, in their own minds, they are hundreds of miles away from accepting that product. It is your job to bridge that gap." (p. 23)

**The psychological-wall principle (p. 23–24).** "Each of these stages is separated from the others by a psychological wall. On one side of that wall is indifference; on the other, intense interest. A headline that will work wonders in the first stage — for example, 'Dial Soap — 9¢ a cake' — will fail completely when addressed to a third-stage market where your prospect doesn't even realize that soaps can be made with built-in deodorants. And a third-stage headline — for example 'Who else wants a whiter wash with no hard work?' — will be old-hat, no-news to today's housewife, who has been barraged by whiter-than-white advertising for twenty years." (p. 24)

> "To sum up, then: a headline which will work to a market in one stage of awareness will not work to a market in another stage of awareness. Nor will it work, even to a market in which it has been successful, once that market passes on to a new stage of awareness." (p. 24)

**When you face a fifth-stage problem (p. 24).** "When a product begins to slip... when volume falls off, even though advertising budgets are increased... when the name of the product no longer sells as much... when a direct statement of the products function no longer sells as much... when a direct statement of the desire or the need that the product fulfills no longer sells as much — then that product needs to be reborn, and its problem is the problem of opening up an unaware market."

**Headline strategy — process of elimination (p. 24).**
> "Planning a headline for a completely unaware or resistant market, then, is first of all a process of elimination. Here are the first paths:"

Three negative rules (p. 25):
1. **Eliminate price.** "Price means nothing to a person who does not know your product, or want your product. Therefore, eliminate all mention of price, or price reduction, in your headline or prime display type." (p. 25)
2. **Eliminate the product name.** "The name of your product means nothing to a person who has never seen it before, and may actually damage your ad if you have had a bad model the year before, or if it is now associated with the antiquated, the unfashionable, or the unpleasant. Therefore, keep your product out of the headline, and be extremely wary about breaking the mood or disguise of your ad with a prominent logo." (p. 25)
3. **Eliminate direct statement of function/desire.** "At this stage of your market, a direct statement of what your product does, what desire it satisfies, or what problem it sokes [solves — OCR], simply will not work... You are not faced here with a problem of sophistication, but one of complete indifference, or unacceptability. Therefore, the performance of your product, and the desire it satisfies, can only be brought in later. You cannot mention them in your headline." (p. 25)

**What's left — the identification headline (p. 25).** "So you cannot mention price, product, function or desire. What do you have left? Your market, of course!"

> "What you are doing essentially in this fifth stage is calling your market together in the headline of your ad. You are writing an identification headline. You are selling nothing, promising nothing, satisfying nothing. Instead, you are echoing an emotion, an attitude, a [dis]satisfaction that picks people out from the crowd and binds them together in a single statement." (pp. 25–26)

> "In this type of headline, you are telling them what they are. You are defining them for themselves. You are giving them the information they need and want, about a problem still so vague that you are the first to put it into words." (p. 26)

**The headline's only job at this stage (p. 26).** "Here, above all, is the type of headline that never attempts to sell a product or a performance, but simply tries to sell the remainder of the ad itself — the information that follows on the page. The only function of this headline is to get the prospect to read the next paragraph. And this second paragraph pulls him into the third; and the third into the fourth: and right on down the page, paragraph after paragraph."

> "Meanwhile these paragraphs are building a steady progression of logical images, from the first identification with the headline, to a growing awareness of the problem or the desire to the realization that a solution is at hand, and to the inevitable focussing of that desire and that solution onto your particular product." (p. 26)

**Cardinal rule (p. 35).** "Your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it. It must be his headline, his problem, his state of mind at that particular moment. It must pick out the product's logical prospects — and reject as many people as it attracts."

**Warning (p. 35).** "Most copy writers use a fifth stage problem to write an empty headline, and are therefore simply wasting their client's money... It cannot simply be a startler, or an attention-getter, or humorous, or cute. Nor can it mask the fact that it has no headline behind a pretty picture."

## Hidden-dream / hidden-fear tactics (pp. 26–35)

These are seven named fifth-stage tactics, each formatted as Problem → Solution → headline → key body paragraph.

### Giving Words to a Hidden Dream (pp. 26–27)
**What it does.** Names a hope the prospect has not yet articulated, then casts the product as the response to that named hope.
**Problem.** "to expand the market for home correspondence courses beyond that obtained by 'Earn more money' and 'Gain more skill' headlines." (p. 26)
**Headline.** "THE UNIVERSITY OF THE NIGHT" (p. 27).
**Body opener.** "The young Lincoln, poring over borrowed school-books far into the night... here is an example which has inspired the man who strives against the odds of circumstances to make his place in the world." (p. 27)

### Exploiting a Hidden Fear (pp. 27–28)
**What it does.** Surfaces a dread the prospect has not let himself name, then anchors the product as escape.
**Problem.** Re-vitalize a coffee substitute (Postum) after health/pep/taste headlines failed; brand name slipping. (p. 27)
**Headline.** "WHY MEN CRACK..." (p. 28)
**Body openers.** "An authority of international standing recently wrote: 'You have overeaten and plugged your organs with moderate stimulants...'" / "You know them. Strong men, vigorous men, robust men — men who have never had a sick day in their lives. They drive. They drive themselves to the limit. They lash themselves over the limit with stimulants. They crack. Often, they crash." / "Borrowed Energy Must Be Repaid!" (p. 28)

### Leading Into an Unacceptable Problem by Starting With a Universally Accepted Image (pp. 28–29)
**What it does.** Takes a problem the prospect (or publisher) would reject if stated directly, then opens with an image both find acceptable, then walks the reader into the problem.
**Problem.** "To gain both publisher and prospect acceptance for a woman's deodorant. A direct statement of the performance or product would not only offend, but would never be published." (p. 28)
**Headline.** "WITHIN THE CURVE OF A WOMAN'S ARM" (p. 29) — Odorono.
**Body opener.** "A frank discussion of a subject too often avoided. A woman's arm! Poets have sung of its grace; artists have painted its beauty. It should be the daintiest, sweetest thing in the world. And yet, unfortunately, it isn't, always." (p. 29)

### To Project a Hidden Desire Which Cannot Be Put Bluntly Into Words (pp. 29–30)
**What it does.** When verbal expression of the desire would be "absurd and offensive," project it through imagery.
**Problem.** "To capitalize on research findings that smoking cigarettes gives men a feeling of virility, importance, sexual strength. Any verbal expression of these themes, however, would be instantly rejected as absurd and offensive." (p. 29)
**Solution.** "The MARLBORO TATTOO AD: With its virile men (cowboys, racing car drivers, sky divers, etc.) whose appearance alone projected more of an image of raw virility than any number of words could ever convey." (pp. 29–30)

### Using a Common Resentment or Unvoiced Protest to Capture a Far Greater Market Than the Direct Statement of the Solution of That Resentment Would Produce (pp. 30–31)
**What it does.** Broadens the market by tapping a shared grievance instead of asking a narrower DIY-capable group to act.
**Problem.** Sell a do-it-yourself TV-repair book; the direct headline "Save up to $100 a year on your TV repairs!" only catches handymen. Broaden by exploiting "the existing resentment against TV service contracts." (p. 30)
**Headline.** "WHY HAVEN'T TV OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS" (p. 30)
**Body openers.** "Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947? Then here is the full, uncensored story of how you can avoid those $15-$20 repair bills... How many times this week have you had to get up to fix a jumpy TV picture?... 90% of These Breakdowns Are Unnecessary!" (p. 30) — Then a "Suppose you had a TV expert at your elbow 24 hours a day" sequence (p. 31).

### Projecting an Ultimate Triumph That the Prospect Will Identify With (pp. 32–33)
**What it does.** Skips the direct benefit and stages a cinematic triumph the prospect projects himself into.
**Problem.** Sell music lessons by correspondence to a wider audience than would respond to "Play Real Tunes on the Piano in Five Days." (p. 32)
**Headline.** "THEY LAUGHED WHEN I SAT DOWN AT THE PIANO. BUT WHEN I STARTED TO PLAY!—" (p. 32)
**Body.** "Arthur had just played 'The Rosary.' The room rang with applause... 'Can he really play?' I heard a girl whisper to Arthur. 'Heavens, no!' Arthur exclaimed... Then I Started to Play. Instantly a tense silence fell on the guests. The laughter died on their lips as if by magic..." (pp. 32–33). Reveal: U.S. School of Music correspondence.

### Projecting the Result of a Problem in Such a Way That It Will Be Identified With by People Who Would Reject a Direct Statement of the Problem Itself (pp. 33–34)
**What it does.** Dramatizes the social consequence of an unflattering problem so the reader self-identifies via the result, not the cause.
**Problem.** Increase mouthwash sales on the social-offense theme (bad breath) — too insulting to be taken straight. (p. 33)
**Headline.** "OFTEN A BRIDESMAID BUT NEVER A BRIDE" (p. 33) — Listerine.
**Body opener.** "Edna's case was really a pathetic one. Like every woman, her primary ambition was to marry... no one possessed more charm or grace or loveliness than she. And as her birthdays crept gradually toward that tragic thirty-mark, marriage seemed farther from her life than ever. She was often a bridesmaid but never a bride." (p. 33). Reveal: halitosis → Listerine. (p. 34)

### Projecting the Result of an Accomplishment to Attract People Who Would Be Frightened Away by the Work Implied to Achieve It (p. 34)
**What it does.** Direct the prospect's mind "away from effort, to reward."
**Problem.** Broaden home correspondence courses beyond a direct learning/skill claim. (p. 34)
**Headline + body.** "HERE'S AN EXTRA $50, GRACE — 'I'm making real money now!' 'Yes, I've been keeping it a secret until pay day came. I've been promoted with an increase of $50 a month... Just a little reward for urging me to study at home... We're starting up easy street, Grace, thanks to you and the I.C.S....'" (p. 34)

### The List Never Ends (pp. 34–35)
> "Every day new solutions, new patterns are being created. Wherever the direct appeal fails, or loses its power, you should begin to explore a fifth stage headline." (p. 34)

> "And, if it is an effective headline, and it works, then it too will become outdated as your market moves on to a new stage of awareness... You never step in the same river twice. No market ever stands still." (p. 35)

## A Final Word on Style in Advertising Copy (pp. 35–36)

> "Markets change; desires change; fashions change. And so do the acceptable styles of advertisements change. Certain advertising styles — the form your advertising message takes — grow tired with time — then stale — then actually laughable." (p. 35)

> "At the turn of the century, effective ads were written in verse; twenty years later, no one would believe them. In the 1920s most of the great ads were narrative stories — either first-person confessions, or third person revelations, or comic strips dramatizations. Today everything but the comic strip is gone..." (p. 35)

> "When a new style is born, people believe it, and it reinforces the message it is carrying. When that same style grows trite, people cannot see the message for the advertisement." (pp. 35–36)

> "In effective advertising, though styles may change, strategy does not. If you will study the piano and bridesmaid ads in this chapter, you will notice this: That while the narrative style of both is now old-hat, you can still respond to their power. Both tap desires that still exist — though now perhaps directed toward different products and different problems. And both evoke those desires, and channel those desires, so effectively, that if they were rewritten in today's idiom, and applied to different products, they still might sell millions of dollars worth of goods today." (p. 36)

Paraphrase: style ages, strategy doesn't. The five awareness states are strategy; verse, narrative, comic strip are style. Re-clothe the strategy in the current idiom.

## Key insights

1. **Awareness, not cleverness, sets the headline.** "It is at this precise point of awareness that your headline begins." (p. 15)
2. **The headline does not sell — it buys the next sentence.** (pp. 14–15)
3. **A psychological wall separates each stage; a headline that wins in one stage fails in the next.** "On one side of that wall is indifference; on the other, intense interest." (pp. 23–24)
4. **As awareness drops, creative wages rise.** Most-aware copywriters are "phrase-makers" who deserve "the lowest possible scale of pay" (p. 16); fifth-stage copywriting "is the domain of the idea man" (p. 20).
5. **Stage 2 has exactly seven jobs.** Reinforce, sharpen, extend, prove, add new mechanism (better), add new mechanism (eliminates limitation), reposition. (p. 17)
6. **Stage 3 = crystallize the amorphous desire in one statement.** Three steps: name desire+solution → prove → show product is the mechanism. (p. 20)
7. **Stage 5 forbids price, product name, and direct statement of function/desire.** What remains is the market itself — write an "identification headline." (pp. 25–26)
8. **The fifth-stage headline never sells the product — it sells the rest of the ad.** (p. 26)
9. **The prospect must identify with the headline before he can buy from it.** "It must pick out the product's logical prospects — and reject as many people as it attracts." (p. 35)
10. **Markets move; even a winning headline ages.** "You never step in the same river twice. No market ever stands still." (p. 35)
11. **Style ages, strategy doesn't.** (p. 36)

## Copy patterns / sentence templates

Stage 1
- `[Product name], [model details] — Formerly [old price] — Now Only [new price].` (p. 16)

Stage 2
- `[Product name] — [the {category} of the {superlative}].` ("Steinway — The Instrument of the Immortals", p. 17)
- `[Product name] — [the {-est} {category} in the world].` (p. 17)
- `[Sensory verb that re-experiences the product] — [Brand].` ("Tastes like you just picked it — Dole", p. 18)
- `At [extreme condition], the [unexpected detail] is [mundane fact].` (Rolls Royce, p. 18)
- `Anywhere you go, [Brand] is always [adjective].` (Hertz, p. 18)

## Common mistakes / warnings

- **Mistake: Demanding the headline make the sale.** Why fails: it crams the entire selling burden into "approximately 10% to 20% of the total physical space of your ad" (p. 14). Fix: "Your headline has only one job — to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence" (p. 14).
- **Mistake: Using a fifth-stage problem to write a startler/cute/pretty-picture headline.** Why fails: "It cannot simply be a startler, or an attention-getter, or humorous, or cute. Nor can it mask the fact that it has no headline behind a pretty picture. Most copy writers use a fifth stage problem to write an empty headline, and are therefore simply wasting their client's money." (p. 35) Fix: "Your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it. It must be his headline, his problem, his state of mind at that particular moment." (p. 35)
- **Mistake: Using a Stage-1 price/name headline on a Stage-3 market that doesn't yet know the category.** Why fails: "'Dial Soap — 9¢ a cake' will fail completely when addressed to a third-stage market where your prospect doesn't even realize that soaps can be made with built-in deodorants." (p. 24) Fix: write at the prospect's actual point of awareness.
- **Mistake: Re-running last year's winning headline indefinitely.** Why fails: "Nor will it work, even to a market in which it has been successful, once that market passes on to a new stage of awareness." (p. 24) Fix: when the direct appeal loses force, drop to the next stage of awareness (p. 34).
- **Mistake: Mentioning price, product name, function, or desire in a fifth-stage headline.** Why fails: "You are not faced here with a problem of sophistication, but one of complete indifference, or unacceptability." (p. 25) Fix: write an identification headline; "selling nothing, promising nothing, satisfying nothing... echoing an emotion, an attitude, a [dis]satisfaction." (pp. 25–26)
- **Mistake: Confusing style with strategy.** Why fails: styles "grow tired with time — then stale — then actually laughable" (p. 35). Fix: keep the strategy (the awareness-state diagnosis) and re-skin in the current idiom (p. 36).
