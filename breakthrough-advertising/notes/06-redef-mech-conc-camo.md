## CHAPTER 10 — Redefinition (book pp. 153–164)

### Core concept
Redefinition is "the process of giving a new definition to your product. It says that the product is *this* rather than *that*. Its objective is to remove a roadblock to your sale — if possible, before the prospect even knows it exists" (p. 154). Where Gradualization works "by its arrangement of facts and phrases," Redefinition works "by its rearrangement of perspective" (p. 164). It is the tool you reach for when a product has built-in handicaps that "actually repel the prospect" (p. 153) — handicaps that fall into three categories: (1) too complicated/too hard to use, (2) not important enough, (3) costs too much (pp. 153–154). One mechanism — redefinition — handles all three through three sub-techniques: Simplification, Escalation, and Price Reduction.

### How to Remove Objections to Your Product (p. 153)
> "Time and time again, you are going to have to sell a product that has built-in handicaps. That — along with its promises and its functions — also has certain aspects to it that actually repel the prospect" (p. 153).

> "No product, of course, is perfect. If only for the reason that he must pay money for what you have to sell, your prospect starts with a basic minimum of resistance against buying your product" (p. 153).

The three categories of drawback (pp. 153–154):
1. The product that is (or sounds) too complicated — too hard to use.
2. The product that is not important enough — appeal not statistically broad enough.
3. The product that just costs too much.

**Classic example — Lifebuoy / B.O. campaign (p. 154):** Lifebuoy had a "horrible medicinal odor" that couldn't be removed without killing its cleaning power. Redefinition flipped the liability into an asset: the prospect's *own* body odor became the enemy, and the medicinal smell became the proof of "odor-destroying power." "This is the simplest, and often the most effective kind of redefinition. A simple concept-judo. A complete reversal. Turning a liability into an asset, with a single idea" (p. 154).

### Simplification (p. 155)
**Worked example — Television Repair Book ad (pp. 155–158):** The original ad failed because its headline "Do your own TV repairs" was considered too difficult by the average set owner. The breakthrough ad never says "repairs" until the ground has been prepared. Instead it executes a three-step redefinition of the word "repairs" itself (pp. 157–158):

1. Compare the TV set to the human body, equating minor maladjustments with the running-nose warning signs of a cold — making the technical commonplace and familiar (p. 157).
2. Continuously describe these maladjustments as "warning signals" and "symptoms" rather than "breakdowns" or "repairs," so they sound easily corrected (p. 157).
3. State outright that they can be corrected by "making a few minor adjustments, on the outside controls of your set" (p. 157).

> "Therefore, 'repairs' are redefined as 'adjustments.' Troubles on the TV screen are redefined as 'warning signals' or 'symptoms.' And 'repair calls' or 'breakdowns' are carefully segregated into the least-likely-to-occur 5% of all possible TV troubles" (pp. 157–158).

> "Innovation without acceptance is valueless. The more people know that something is difficult, and the more revolutionary (and therefore different) your product is — the more resistance you will meet from them in accepting it. You must, therefore, lay a base for acceptance by redefining the entire field for them, before you bring in your product" (p. 158).

**Sherwin Cody ad — "Only 15 Minutes a Day" (pp. 159–160):** Prospects were "absolutely convinced that good English was too hard for them." The copy redefined the field with simple, astonishing facts: 69 words make up more than half of all speech and letter writing; only one dozen fundamental principles of punctuation; twenty-five typical errors constitute nine-tenths of all everyday grammar mistakes (pp. 159–160). "Thus the complicated becomes simple — the hard becomes easy" (p. 160).

When a product is hard, the answer is "twofold: 1. Redefine (as this chapter shows you). 2. Mechanize the new simplicity (as you'll see in the next chapter)" (p. 158).

### Escalation (p. 160)
> "Here you are dealing with a product which works, and which is acknowledged to be easy enough to use — but which simply does not have an appeal broad enough to assure it of a mass market. Your job here is to escalate your product. To give it more importance in your prospect's eyes" (p. 160).

> "You do this again by Redefinition. You broaden the horizon of benefits of the product. You redefine the role that the product plays in the prospect's life. You widen the area of reward that your product yields to the prospect — showing him that it enters into dozens of vital situations every day, paying off for him where he might least expect it" (p. 160).

**Worked example — "Word Power Machine" (pp. 160–161):** A second-generation English-course ad. "Good English must cease to be an end in itself. It must be redefined, to become instead a means to a more important end — one which is desired by far more people" (p. 160). The dominant benefit shifts to vocabulary as a *persuasion tool to win over other people*: "the blazing new self-confidence you need to make anybody like you — to win people over irresistibly to your point of view…" (p. 161).

**Three uses of escalation** (pp. 161–162):
1. Increase benefit appeal (shift to a more desirable benefit area).
2. Increase importance of the product — "showing that something the prospect wants very much hinges directly upon the performance of your product" (p. 161). E.g., spark plugs: "You pay $2,000 … $3,000 … $4,000 for your car. And a single 99¢ part robs you of the real power and enjoyment that car should give you" (p. 161).
3. Show the product is needed *constantly*, not occasionally. E.g., handling-people course: "Your Entire Life is Spent Trying to Get Others to Do What You Want — Without Friction" (p. 162).

### Price Reduction (p. 162)
> "Here you have the product which, quite simply, costs too much. Your job is to make that price seem less. You do it by a very simple act of redefinition" (p. 162).

> "Why does the product cost too much? Because it's being compared with other products in the same field. And how do you whittle away, psychologically, at this price? By switching the comparison, and relating it to some other, more expensive standard" (p. 162).

**Worked example — SA Fire Injectors (pp. 162–164):** $1.49 spark plug ad — 1.5x the field standard, 2x discount price. The copywriter made them feel cheap in two paragraphs of psychological redefinition. The phrase-by-phrase dissection (pp. 163–164):

- "practically made by hand…"
- "would have to sell for as high as $5 each…"
- "30 or 40 dollars…" (price repeated twice — first per plug, then for the entire set, so the prospect "winces at the $40 figure")
- "get the price down so low…" ("low" now legitimized by the hand-made set-up that preceded it)
- "these injectors would pay for themselves 12 times in one year of driving…" ("Not only value, but reward. Not only low-priced, but gas-saving.")
- "astonishing proposition…" (the price now sounds so low the mere statement may cause surprise)
- "If you will check your car's performance … and tell your friends and neighbors about them…" (a condition introduced — you must perform an action to get the now-low price; favor framing)
- "prepared to do…" (favor, allowance, discount connotation)
- "You can have…" (not "you can buy" — "He is letting you have the plugs at the low price. You are getting a bargain.")
- "only $1.49 each…" (the classic modifier — the tenth bargain-phrase in two paragraphs)

> "Most of it — perhaps all of it — is never consciously noticed by the prospect. He simply realizes that he feels that a bargain is being offered to him" (p. 163).

### Copy patterns + examples
- Liability-into-asset flip ("concept-judo") — Lifebuoy's medicinal odor → odor-destroying power (p. 154).
- Redefine the loaded word before you use it ("repairs" → "adjustments"; "breakdowns" → "warning signals"; p. 157).
- Shrink the field with concrete numbers ("only 69 words…", "only 12 principles…", "25 typical errors…"; p. 159).
- "Only 15 Minutes a Day" headline pattern — quantify the smallness of effort (p. 159).
- Escalate by redefining the *end* ("good English ceases to be an end in itself; becomes a tool to win people over"; p. 160).
- Cost-to-stake ratio — pit a small price against a large prior expenditure ("$2,000 car / 99¢ part"; p. 161).
- Universalize the use case ("Your Entire Life is Spent…"; p. 162).
- Switch the comparison standard upward (compare your $1.49 product to a $5 hand-made or $30–$40 set; p. 162).
- "You can have" rather than "you can buy" — gift framing (p. 164).
- Stack favor connotations: "we decided to get the price down," "prepared to do," "only…" (pp. 163–164).

## CHAPTER 11 — Mechanization (book pp. 165–174)

### Core concept
Mechanization is verbal proof — the answer to the prospect's third inevitable demand: "How does it work?" (p. 166). Schwartz lays out three demands every reader makes of copy: "Tell me more," "Oh yeah? Who says so?", and "How does it work?" — and Mechanization answers the third (p. 166). It is the heir to Claude Hopkins's "Reason Why" copy: "the basic question you must ask yourself, about this device, when you sit down to write a piece of copy, is not, 'Should I use it?' Or 'Should I build a mechanism into this copy?' But simply: 'How much?'" (p. 167). The amount needed depends on the State of Awareness of the prospect (p. 167). Mechanism matters not just for proving claims but also for *bargain* perception — a price cut without a mechanism (a reason-why) loses most of its sales power (pp. 171–172).

### Verbal Proof — Three Stages (pp. 167–170)

#### 1. Name the Mechanism (p. 167)
When the mechanism is already familiar to the prospect (other advertisers spent the money making it familiar), you simply name it and move on to compete on price or other features.

> "You may now take advantage of their investment by simply naming the mechanism, and going on to beat them with your price or other features" (p. 167).

**Example (p. 168):**
> TAKE FOOLPROOF PHOTOS WITH THE XENOPHON 1750
> With Electronic Light Setter… Push-Button Zomar Lens… Magazine Load… Only $135.

> "Here, the three mechanisms which insure the perfect pictures are simply named, and not described at all. The prospect is already familiar with the way they work from the other ads he has seen, and any further detailing of their nuts and bolts would simply bore him. Therefore, you name them in as bold type as possible, and go on to compete with your price" (p. 168).

Catalog and retail copy typically operates at this stage (p. 168).

#### 2. Describe the Mechanism (p. 168)
Two reasons you must describe rather than merely name (p. 168):
1. The prospect doesn't understand the mechanism.
2. Everybody else has the same mechanism, same promise, same price; the market is tired and you need a new way to compete.

> "Here your mechanism is not so well known, or not known at all, and you can't simply name it. You have to go into more detail; you have to describe it. So you have the classic situation of Promise—Reason Why. You build a strong, quick promise — and then you follow up with the reason why you can deliver that promise" (p. 168).

**Example — Rinso, 1926 (p. 169):**
Promise paragraphs ("Who else wants a whiter wash — with no hard work? … Now they just soak — rinse — and hang out to dry!") followed by mechanism paragraphs:
> "Dirt floats off — stains go. The secret is simply Rinso — a mild, granulated soap that gives rich, lasting suds even in the hardest water. Just soak the clothes in the creamy Rinso suds — and the dirt and stains float off…" (p. 169).

> "The first rule of mechanism copy is that it is not scientific discourse. You must never allow it to become dull, or merely factual. You must load it with promise, load it with emotion. Every word in good copy — including mechanism copy — sells" (pp. 169–170).

> "Today, of course, in our much more sophisticated and exploited market, she would no longer do so. Today you would need far more mechanism. You would have to explain more, promise deeper, perhaps even invent a miracle ingredient to do the work for you" (p. 170).

#### 3. Feature the Mechanism (p. 170)
When the market is highly sophisticated, promises sound alike, and price competition becomes suicidal — the mechanism itself becomes the headline.

> "Here we discovered that mechanism — strong mechanism — saleable mechanism — is not only a way to build belief, but may actually become so important to the success of your product that you must put it into the headline" (p. 170).

**Mechanism headlines (p. 170):**
- "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY."
- "FIRST WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING."
- "RUN YOUR CAR WITHOUT SPARK PLUGS."
- "SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY."
- "TOMMY ARMOUR SAYS SMACK HELL OUT OF THE BALL WITH YOUR RIGHT HAND."

> "Every one of them offers you a new way to get what you've been wanting. A NEW WAY: a new mechanism; a new chance to satisfy your desire — even if everything else you've tried has failed you" (p. 171).

**Decision rule (p. 171):**
- If people assume they know how it works, or your claim is so new they don't care — sum mechanism up in a word or phrase.
- If people aren't quite sure how it works — describe the mechanism in selling language until they have enough reason-why.
- If you have an exceptionally strong/dramatic mechanism, or want definite superiority — sell hell out of that mechanism (feature it in the headline).

### Mechanism for Bargain (p. 171)
> "One of the sad truths of our time — and profession — is that our readers do not always believe the truth when we tell it to them… many manufacturers, and their agencies, are startled when they cut a price — advertise the reduction — and see no increase in sales. What happened? No one believed them. A price cut — like a product advantage — is only as good as your words, and your strategy, makes it" (p. 171).

> "Price cuts must be justified. There must be a reason for them. A mechanism behind them. Without such a mechanism — without such a reason-why you should give this bargain — you are going to get only a fraction of its real sales power" (p. 172).

**Example — Robert Collier "Before the Price Goes Up!" Madras shirt letter (p. 172):** Mill overstocked because of unseasonable weather → "S.O.S. calls" → mill needed money → offered surplus quarter-million yards at "below any price we had ever paid for shirtings."

> "Here, the copy writer not only emphasizes bargain over and over again, but brings in *quality* as a counter-desire time after time. He thus uses a mechanism within a mechanism: (1) the unseasonable weather causing (2) the factory to become overstocked resulting in the primary markdown — to build belief upon belief" (p. 172).

> "Here the copy starts with the mechanism, and only goes into the bargain claims six paragraphs later. Again, he has taken the Creative Gamble: in this case, that he could hold the reader's interest for those six paragraphs. And, because of this gamble, he reaps ten times the believability for every word he said about the bargain-value of his offer from that point on" (p. 173).

### Copy patterns + examples
- Decision rule for mechanism dose: name → describe → feature, governed by State of Awareness (p. 167).
- Mechanism-named-only headline format: PROMISE WITH [MECHANISM 1] · [MECHANISM 2] · [MECHANISM 3] · [PRICE] (p. 168).
- Promise → Reason-Why two-step (Rinso, p. 169).
- Mechanism-as-headline ("Floats fat right out of your body," "Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery," p. 170).
- "A NEW WAY" framing — fresh mechanism as a fresh chance after every prior failure (p. 171).
- "Mechanism within a mechanism" — chained reason-whys (Collier weather → overstock → markdown, p. 172).
- Price-cut justification mandatory — a discount without a documented why is wasted (p. 172).

## CHAPTER 12 — Concentration (book pp. 175–184)

### Core concept
Concentration is *direct attack on alternative ways your prospect could satisfy his desire*. It is the fifth competitive weapon, distinct from product superiority, promise superiority, product-role, and response/reaction (pp. 175–176). Where the other four "ignore the competition," Concentration "differs completely from the other four methods… [they] act as though there is no other way possible of gaining the satisfaction your prospect desires" (p. 176). Concentration is for situations where you are smaller-budget than the competitor, where most prospects are already his customers, or where you must crack his image and shatter loyalty before you can rechannel desire (p. 177). Crucially: it is *more than mere attack* — you must always pair the attack on the competitor's weakness with the demonstration that *your* product solves that exact weakness (p. 177).

### What Concentration Is (p. 177)
> "But this process of Concentration — this careful, logical, documented process of proving ineffectual other ways of satisfying your prospect's desire — is much more than mere attack. If you can only attack another product — without showing at the same time, by comparison, how your product provides what the other lacks — then say nothing at all! Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time" (p. 177).

> "Your prospect knows that your attack is biased. If, therefore, you are attacking another product only for your own good — in other words, to win the sale by disparaging your competitor — what you will probably evoke in his mind is skepticism and dislike, and very little else" (p. 177).

> "Concentration, therefore, is the process of pointing out weaknesses in the competition… emphasizing their disservice to your prospect… and then proving to him that your product gives him what he wants without them" (p. 177).

> "Concentration is therefore a complicated process, taking up sizable space to do its job properly, and combining almost every trick you have learned in this book" (p. 178). It uses Intensification (penalties of the old), Gradualization (logical cause of weaknesses), and Mechanization (proof your product removes them) (p. 178).

### Worked example (p. 179) — Spark plug / Fire injector ad ("MECHANICS AND ENGINEERS READ THIS CAREFULLY")
The copy interweaves bad-good, bad-good in a relentless one-for-one contrast (pp. 178–180):

- "A spark plug jumps a spark of electricity across an air gap. This is the most wasteful and power consuming way…"
- "A fire injector fires on the surface of an electrical conductor. This is the most efficient way to get a big powerful spark…"
- "On ordinary spark plugs the air gap … is always getting bigger because the electrode is always burning away. This means you have misfiring … On fire injectors there is no air gap and no electrode to burn away. That means maximum gas explosion which means full power, full economy…"
- "A spark plug accumulates filth and carbon … needs regular cleaning, setting and expensive replacement!" vs. "A fire injector never needs cleaning or setting. It actually 'breaks in' and becomes more efficient with use. It will actually outlast your car…"
- "A spark plug gives you a thin skimpy spark…" vs. "A fire injector gives you a heavy powerful flame…"

**Underlying structure (p. 180):**
> Bad. / Good. / Bad. / Good. / Bad. / Good.

**Devices Schwartz identifies (pp. 179–180):**
- Interweaving contrast — "Bad — good; bad — good; bad — good: this is the underlying structure of this sequence."
- Parallelism repeating words to contrast inherent weakness vs. inherent strength ("A spark plug jumps a spark" vs. "A fire injector fires on…").
- Word-image contrast — "spark" (weak) vs. "flame" (strong); "thin skimpy spark" vs. "heavy, powerful flame."
- Definition/redefinition baked into every claim ("most wasteful and power consuming way" vs. "most efficient way").
- "Breaks in" — redefinition by analogy.
- Documentary mechanism for almost every benefit ("because the electrode is burning away," "because of inefficient firing").
- Authority capstone: "These are some of the reasons that the U.S. Air Force pays premium prices for surface supported injectors for their aircraft…"

### Second Strategy (p. 181)
When points can't be cleanly broken down one-by-one, switch to the *time-sequence* structure:
1. **What happens to you now**, with the product or products you are using presently.
2. **What will happen when you switch** to the new product.

**Worked example — Lecithin reducing pill (pp. 181–183):**

*Negative copy phase* (definitions/redefinition; pp. 181–182):
> "For years doctors have known that ordinary reducing plans … are completely passive! That they depend strictly on your own will power… HUNGER-APPEASING PRODUCTS — pills, powders and liquids that do nothing more than swell up in your stomach — that do nothing more than 'dull' your hunger a little. But not one of these products could do anything to ACTIVELY help you reduce your weight. To take the strain off that starvation diet. To actually help you BURN UP that ugly fat… OXIDIZE that fat… MELT IT AWAY — FOREVER!"

> "So what happened? If you were overweight, you struggled to do the job of reducing BY YOURSELF! You took your hunger-appeasing pills religiously. You pushed away the foods you love. You spent week after week of torture. And finally, if you were lucky, you carved off 5, 10, or even 12 precious pounds. And then your will power snapped! You broke your terrible diet… And the fat flowed back — heavier and uglier and more deadly than ever before!"

**Schwartz's dissection (p. 182):**
- ¶1–2: Definitions and Redefinition — ordinary reducing plans are *passive*; depend on your willpower; can do nothing to *actively* burn fat.
- ¶3: Equating taking ordinary pills with "doing the job of reducing yourself."
- ¶3–4: Logic, cause and effect ("So what happened?") — the woman recognizes each symptom and nods agreement.
- Climax of ¶4: implication "buried in a sentence with which no woman could disagree — is that it was the failure of the *pills* that caused the failure of the diet" (p. 182).

*Hero-product phase* (p. 183) — case-history return to promise:
> "These doctors had the answer in a tiny grey pill — and a common-sense plan… It was a miraculous compound called LECITHIN — brand-new — whose amazing fat-dissolving properties had been discovered by a Nobel prize winner — the co-discoverer of insulin… they were not given any starvation diets … they never experienced a single hungry moment … the ugly excess fat around their bodies melted away! While they were eating three delicious meals a day, they were shedding as much as 5 pounds a week. While they were feasting on mouth-watering steaks…."

> "Here are your contrast — your mechanization — your documentation — your reference to authority — and then your return to promise, in the form of case history, which now has many times its original power, based on both the elimination of alternate channels of fulfillment, and the strong supporting mechanism which documents its claims" (p. 183).

### One Final Word (p. 184)
> "I have purposely chosen extreme examples to illustrate each mechanism. These copy blocks are longer than Concentration need logically be. The same effect may be boiled down into two or three sentences, or even a single phrase, as in this classic headline: 'SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY.' Here is contrast — implied weakness in other products — compensating promise in your own" (p. 184).

> "It is not the content, nor is it the length of copy used in a mechanism that makes it effective. It is simply and solely the problem it solves for you in the development of your copy — by the emotional reaction it produces on your reader when he encounters it. If, in this case, you have caused him to question a habit … shift a loyalty … take a chance on your product — you have done your job, no matter how few or how many words you have used to do it" (p. 184).

### Copy patterns + examples
- Bad/Good interleave structure (p. 180).
- Word-image contrast pairs ("spark" vs. "flame"; p. 180).
- Documentary "because…" tail on every benefit (p. 180).
- Authority capstone after attack sequence (U.S. Air Force; pp. 179, 198).
- Time-sequence structure: present misery (with current option) → switch moment → new outcome (p. 181).
- Universal-experience cause/effect ("So what happened?") that lets the prospect nod through every line (p. 182).
- Short-form Concentration in a single headline ("Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery"; p. 184).
- Rule: never attack without supplying the solution to what you attacked, or "say nothing at all" (p. 177).

## CHAPTER 13 — Camouflage (book pp. 185–194)

### Core concept
Camouflage is borrowing believability "from all the places in our society where it is stored up" (p. 185) — chiefly the publications, networks, and editorial channels prospects already trust. The mechanism is a conditioned reflex: a reader trusts a publication because he has bought it repeatedly; "after a while [its] phraseology begins to carry an aura of truth all by itself no matter what material it embraces" (p. 186). When you mimic the host medium's format and idiom, your ad inherits that "stored believability… a believability reflex" (p. 186). Schwartz frames three borrowing strategies plus two mood adaptations that "rightly belong" (p. 192) in the same discussion.

### Three Believability-Borrowing Strategies

#### 1. Adopting Format (pp. 186–190)
> "First, of course, and most obvious, is Format. Each publication has its own look. You have your copy. Your job is to merge both of them into a combination that will: (1) Allow the reader to enter into your ad with the least possible mental shifting of gears from 'editorial' to 'advertisement.' (2) Carry along the greatest possible amount of believability through every sentence of the ad" (pp. 186–187).

> "I have not discussed layout in this book, because I do not believe layout is nearly as effective as copy in determining the results of your ad. Here, however, layout is important. A single change in format can add 50% to your readership, and your results" (p. 187).

> "Your job here, once again, is to approximate as closely as possible the format of the medium in which you are advertising. This means, ideally, letting *them* set your ad… using their kind of headline-to-body-copy transition… using their illustrations, their sub heads, their break-up of space" (p. 187).

**Worked case — "How to Make Anybody Like You!" book ad (pp. 187–190):** A house-set magazine version was "mildly successful." The same ad re-set feature-for-feature in *Wall Street Journal* style ran nineteen times monthly with no drop-off in pull. Adaptations Schwartz lists (pp. 187–190):

1. Headline set in journal type, upper-and-lower case, indistinguishable from editorial headlines — does NOT signal "this is an ad: beware!"
2. Two sub-headlines, one directly after the other ("Very Nineteenth Century" — abandoned by 99% of newspapers, but the *Journal* kept it).
3. Two bars directly above and below the first sub-head — "small touches that define individuality."
4. Sub-heads placed at the extreme left of the column.
5. Line drawing of the author rather than a photograph — uglier but matches *Journal*'s editorial convention.

> "What has been done by these adaptations, is that a corny, old-fashioned, rather ugly advertisement has been developed for this specific medium — which has about twice the believability, twice the pulling power, and twice the staying power of the same exact copy and illustration presented the mass-produced way" (p. 190).

> "Is it worth the extra cost? Yes. Should it be done for each important medium in turn? Yes. Why? Because in so doing, you are tapping the ingrained trust that the reader has for each medium in turn, and channeling at least part of it, unconsciously, onto your product" (p. 190).

#### 2. Adopting Phraseology (pp. 190–191)
> "The second — slightly less specific — is adopting phraseology. This method stems from the fact that certain media, or classes of media, use certain stereotyped phrases over and over again, which after a while take on a believability of their own" (p. 190).

In newspapers there are news-indicators — "an issue date, a city of origin, and perhaps a by-line" — each picked up by the copywriter to add believability (p. 191).

**Example (p. 191):**
> SKIN SPECIALIST DEMONSTRATES
> HOW TO RINSE AWAY YOUR BLACKHEADS
> By Claire Hoffman
> New York, N.Y. — A leading doctor today showed an audience of men, women and skin-troubled teenagers how to clean oily skin and shrink enlarged pores with a 10-minute home medical treatment he has perfected….

> "The news tone has been set by the opening phrases. The entire remainder of the copy has been given the atmosphere of a report rather than a sales story by these first few sentences" (p. 191).

**Cross-medium application (p. 191):**
- Direct mail — mimic refund checks, government correspondence, dividend notices, confidential reports, high-priced newsletters.
- Radio/TV — adopt the news format and phraseology; "the clipped phrase of the newscaster, the insider tone of the analyst, the documentary feel of the news, hand-held camera."

> "Study the channels of communication that people believe in. Adopt their tone, their feel, their style, their sincerity. Make your ads blend in, so there is no jarring transition. Camouflage them" (p. 191).

#### 3. Mood Adaptations — Understatement & Deadly Sincerity (pp. 192–194)
> "We've seen that you borrow your publication's format and your publication's phraseology or style. These two methods of adding confidence to your copy depend directly on the particular medium you are using at any particular moment. You pick up that publication's appearance and idiom, as it were, because people have come to contrast that editorial manner of presentation with 'advertising language' in general — and because they automatically trust the first, and automatically approach the second with an ingrained skepticism" (p. 192).

> "Advertising language, which is quite naturally biased language and emotionally-charged language, tends to produce a counter-reaction in its prospects by its very appearance. To overcome this instant and automatic skepticism — besides borrowing our host publication's idiom — we also have two other idioms which allow us to escape the 'hard-sell' stereotype" (p. 192).

**Mood A — Understatement (pp. 192–193):**
> "The first is Understatement. Simplicity. A lack of color words. Fewer adjectives than the reader would expect — to superlatives. Short sentences, that fall rather than rise in tone at their end" (p. 192).

> "For example, take any Volkswagen ad. Count the number of nouns per sentence, and compare it to the number of adjectives. See how the ad kids itself — never takes itself too seriously. Notice how it makes its point quietly, and them stops. Even how the extremely short, extremely simple sentence structure adds to the feeling of sincerity" (p. 192).

**Volkswagen example (pp. 192–193):**
> You never run out of air.
> You also won't have any worries about draining or flushing the radiator in spring. There is no radiator.
> Or hoses. Or water pump. Or rust.
> And so there are no worries about anti-freeze in the fall or cracked blocks in the winter.
> If you feel that you owe your Volkswagen engine a little something special every spring and fall, you can do this much.
> Just run it once around the block and let it air itself out.

> "This is great copy because of its simplicity and its image and its sell, all of which interact to give a very powerful argument to anyone looking for a new car" (p. 193).

**Mood B — Deadly Sincerity (p. 193):**
> "The second mood you may employ to break away from the 'advertising' stereotype is what I call Deadly Sincerity. This is the technique of leaning over backwards to point out the flaws in an offer, so that the benefits, when you bring them in, will be believed that much more deeply. Since this is not done in ordinary advertising — since ordinary copy does not knock its product as well as praise it — it carries great emotional impact, especially in highly-competitive fields" (p. 193).

**Example — Settle-for-nothing-less-than-the-presidency book ad (pp. 193–194):**
> TO THE MAN WHO WILL SETTLE FOR NOTHING LESS THAN THE PRESIDENCY OF HIS FIRM
> And who is willing to make the incredible sacrifices necessary to get there in the shortest possible time.
> Here is the most realistic handbook ever written for you — and you alone.
> This is a private advertisement.
> It is not meant for ninety-nine men out of every hundred. These men do not have the drive — the impossible pride — the absolute compulsion to succeed that this advertisement demands…

> "The use of such 'non-advertising phraseology' as 'incredible sacrifice'… 'impassible pride'… 'absolute compulsion'… 'this advertisement demands' set the ad off from the others around it — give it an air of unpredictability that induces the reader to go on" (p. 194).

> "This is one more way to break out of the hard-sell stereotype, and gain almost the same type of believability that would be given to a factual report" (p. 194).

A second variant of Deadly Sincerity is shown in Chapter 14 (pp. 200–201) — a sequence that starts by *flagging the flaws* of the book ("This is John Horn's first book. He is not an accomplished writer, and it has flaws…") and only then states the positive ("But there are paragraphs in this book — ideas in this book — whole chapters in this book… that may take a lifetime of waiting for you to walk through!"). "The startling frankness of the first paragraph… serves as an intensifying contrast to the positive claims of the second paragraph — making them far more believable…" (p. 201).

### Copy patterns + examples
- Same-as-editorial headline type, upper-and-lower-case, no all-caps banners (p. 187).
- Stacked sub-heads in the medium's idiom, even if "Nineteenth Century" (p. 187).
- Line drawings instead of glamour photos when the host publication uses line drawings (p. 190).
- News-indicator opener: "[CITY], [STATE] — A leading [authority] today…" with byline (p. 191).
- Direct-mail mimicry: refund-check, dividend-notice, confidential-report formats (p. 191).
- Broadcast mimicry: clipped newscaster cadence, hand-held camera feel (p. 191).
- Understatement copy: short sentences, sentences that *fall* in tone, almost no adjectives (Volkswagen, p. 192).
- Deadly Sincerity opener: state who the offer is *not* for / list the product's flaws first (pp. 193–194, 201).
- "This is a private advertisement" / non-advertising phraseology (p. 194).

## Common mistakes / warnings (across these four chapters)

- **Redefinition without proof.** "A new breakthrough is not merely accepted because its manufacturer says so. Its claims for ease and simplicity must be proved, in the ad, or the reader will simply shrug his shoulders and say 'it's just another copywriter gone wild'" (p. 158). Redefinition must be paired with Mechanization.
- **Mechanism copy that drifts into scientific discourse.** "You must never allow it to become dull, or merely factual. You must load it with promise, load it with emotion. Every word in good copy — including mechanism copy — sells" (pp. 169–170).
- **Cutting price without a reason-why.** "A price cut — like a product advantage — is only as good as your words, and your strategy, makes it" (p. 171). Without a mechanism behind the discount, you get only a fraction of its real sales power (p. 172).
- **Attack-only Concentration.** "If you can only attack another product — without showing at the same time, by comparison, how your product provides what the other lacks — then say nothing at all! Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time" (p. 177). Naked disparagement evokes "skepticism and dislike, and very little else."
- **Concentration when you shouldn't.** "When your story is so powerful, so different, or so fresh that the competition has nothing to match it… it's better not to give him the prestige of attack, not to mention his claims or his product, even invidiously, in the space which costs you such a dear dollar" (p. 177).
- **House-set ads ignoring the medium.** Treating every publication identically wastes the believability reflex. "A single change in format can add 50% to your readership" (p. 187); the WSJ-adapted version of the same copy ran 19x with no drop-off (p. 187).

## Ethical / honesty notes

Schwartz frames Camouflage explicitly as borrowing trust the prospect *already has* — and is careful to note where the line is. Borrowing the publication's format and phraseology works because the prospect has chosen the publication based on belief in its truth: "he simply assumes that his publication wouldn't carry the ad if it weren't true" (p. 186). The implication — though Schwartz doesn't moralize about it — is that this trust is *spent* by the ad: lose it, and "the effectiveness of its advertising just goes to pieces. If he no longer believes in the publication, he won't believe in the advertising it carries" (p. 186).

Where he comes closest to an ethical line is in the Concentration chapter: "Your prospect knows that your attack is biased. If, therefore, you are attacking another product only for your own good — in other words, to win the sale by disparaging your competitor — what you will probably evoke in his mind is skepticism and dislike" (p. 177). The honest version requires that the attack be *for his own good* — and that you must provide the solution at the same time you point out the weakness.

Deadly Sincerity is presented as the inverse move and the cleanest one: leaning over backwards to point out the flaws in an offer earns the believability factual reporting earns (p. 193). Schwartz treats this as the legitimate counterweight to "advertising language" skepticism — the ad that admits its limits is the ad that gets believed on its strengths.

What Schwartz does NOT discuss as an ethical question (modern reader caveat): mimicking news format and editorial phraseology to deliberately blur the editorial/advertisement distinction (pp. 186–191). Today this would land squarely in FTC native-advertising disclosure rules; in 1966 he treats it purely as a craft optimization.
