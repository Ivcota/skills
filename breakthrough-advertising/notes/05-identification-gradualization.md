## CHAPTER 8 — Identification (book pp. 107–128)

### Core concept
Identification is the second mechanism of persuasion: building a "saleable personality" into the product so that owning it lets the prospect express the character role he wishes he had, or display the achievement role he has won (or wants to win). It works because identification is "a special form of desire — the desire, not for satisfaction, but for recognition" (p. 115). Where Desire (Ch. 7) sells what the product *does*, Identification sells who the prospect *becomes* by owning it. When products do "the same job as [their] competitors, and [are] so priced that price is no longer a factor, then the prospect's choice will almost overwhelmingly depend on the difference in role that your product offers him" (p. 115).

### Roles the Prospect Desires

**Character Roles** — covered earlier in the chapter (pp. 110–113). These are *self-assigned, ambiguous, subconscious* identities the prospect projects onto himself; "they are not created by society but by the prospect himself, and since they are almost never claimed openly but only hinted at, implied and prompted, therefore they can never truly be tested or measured, and they are ambiguous. They are subject to error. And, more important, they are subject to fantasy" (p. 112). "Great areas of these character roles literally exist in the subconscious. They are never given definite words. They are not stated or discussed, but subtly expressed in symbols and images" (p. 112). Because they're never tested, "your prospect is far more ready to believe in the character roles you assign to him, than he is to believe in either your product's performance claims, or the achievement roles it may offer him" (p. 112). Example: virility implied by "a test pilot smoking a cigarette" — "the prospect can easily persuade himself that the same action, smoking, performed on the same product, the cigarette, conveys to him at least some of the implied virility of the genuine possessor" (p. 112). The character role's superpower is "ease-of-acceptance, this consolation-without-cost feature" (p. 112) — no verbal claim, no test, no proof required.

**Achievement Roles (p. 113)** — "These are the status roles . . . class roles . . . position roles that are created by every society on earth, and offered to the men and women who can earn them" (p. 113). Hundreds of them, "usually expressed by nouns, with the nouns serving as titles."
- For men: "Executive" . . . "Home Owner" . . . "$20,000-a-year-Man" . . . "Five Handicap" . . . "Man-On-His-Way-Up" . . . "Block Chairman" (p. 113).
- For women (mid-1960s framing): "Wife," then "Fashion Setter" . . . "Career Woman" . . . "Good Mother" . . . "Civic Leader" . . . "Power-Behind-the-Throne" . . . "Patron of the Arts" (p. 113).
- Each role is "an achievement to be won, and held, and — most of all — displayed" (p. 113). Because the achievements themselves are "immaterial and invisible — mere titles, roles, bundles of privilege," they "must first be translated into physical symbols of success, for everyone around us to see" (p. 113). "And the easiest and most universally acknowledged symbols of success in America today are the products we can buy . . . In America today we are known — not only by the company we keep — but by the products we own" (p. 113).
- Worked illustration: the newly-wed receives a wedding ring "which has absolutely no function other than to define her" (p. 114); she discards a closet of clothes "that still fitted her physically, but not socially — in order to replace them with a new material personality, her trousseau" (p. 114). "Man-On-His-Way-Up . . . trades in his Ford for a Buick, and, when he becomes an 'Executive,' trades in his Buick for a Cadillac" (p. 114). Together these possessions construct a "material personality, that we carry with us wherever we go, and whose function it is to define us, instantly, to whomever we meet" (p. 114).

### How to Put Identification to Work (p. 114)
"Thus products become more than products. In addition to their physical functions, they take on new immaterial functions — as status definers. They announce our achievements, define our role in life, document our success" (p. 114). All products can benefit, but the technique is decisive when "you have a product that does the same job as its competitors, and is so priced that price is no longer a factor" (pp. 114–115); then role differences carry the sale. "The performance of this job — the process of building these character and achievement roles into your product, to be used by your prospect — is the process of Identification, our second mechanism of persuasion" (p. 115).

The method follows the same discovery-and-magnification pattern used for desire (p. 115):
1. Discover which character/achievement roles your prospect is *ready* to identify with via your product, which he'll reject, and which is most compelling (p. 115).
2. Present those chosen roles "so vividly and so intensely — that the role you are projecting will become virtually irresistible" (p. 115).

The most generally compelling American roles Schwartz lists: "the wish to be virile or feminine — the wish to be exciting, unique, fun-filled and adventurous — the wish to be friendly and well-liked — the wish to be important, influential and correct — the wish to be modern and up-to-date — and, above all, the wish to be successful, to make something of our lives" (p. 117).

**Limitation rule:** "you cannot force your market to accept an unrealistic identification. People assign certain characteristics to certain products. These characteristics may arise out of the product itself . . . or they may stem from the role or roles that it plays in their lives today . . . These characteristics may be true or false, superficial or profound, flattering or libelous. But as far as your product is concerned, they are as hard as rock . . . They cannot be changed. If you try to break through them and establish a contradictory image, you will learn that they form the walls of a prison" (pp. 115–116). The right move: "build on to them — to use them as a foundation for sharper, coordinated images" (p. 116).

### Primary Image vs Building New Images (pp. 117–124)

**The Primary Image (p. 117)**: "the product you are given has its own personality at the moment you are given it. It has its own characteristics. And these characteristics may contradict what most Americans want — or simply not include them — or simply not include as many of them as you want" (p. 117). Examples (p. 117): a cigarette is not, by itself, a symbol of success; a piston ring is not, by itself, a symbol of virility; household appliances are not, at first glance, symbols of femininity.

**Two-step process (pp. 117–118)**:
1. Identify the primary image the product already has in the prospect's mind (cigarettes = virile; piston rings = precision-machined, mechanical beauty; appliances = time-saving).
2. Use that already-accepted image as raw material to construct "new, double, triple and quadruple images, that draw in more of these most-wanted roles into your product-personality, and multiply its identification appeal" (p. 118).

**Two ways to do step 2 (p. 118)**:
1. **Change the intensity of the primary image** — emphasize and dramatize it if it's already favorable, tone it down if it's negative or neutral.
2. **Use the primary image as a logical link to bring in other, more favorable images** (p. 119).

**Marlboro example (p. 118)**: the male virility already associated with smoking — "the sheer physical act of smoking — of 'playing with fire' — of 'breathing fire' — has been for centuries an assertion of manhood and of daring." Marlboro intensified it three ways: (1) presented men who were themselves virile, (2) in situations/occupations that demand virility, (3) the "Creative Gamble" of putting a tattoo — "one of the most primitive symbols of virility known to history" — on those men's hands. "A single dominant emotion — virility — symbolized three ways. The impact of repetition reinforced through variation" (p. 118).

**Critical rule about substitution (p. 119)**: "people just won't believe that a product is what it isn't. You cannot contradict accepted images or beliefs in advertising. This is not advertising's role. Nor is it really necessary." Instead, "incorporate them in a larger, overall image — lower their emotional intensity — and use them as readily-accepted bridges to lead your prospect into far more compelling appeals."

**Chesterfield "Blow Some My Way" (1926) example (pp. 119–120)**: unfavorable primary image — cigarettes were "a man's product" — had to be retained, not erased. Solution: keep the man, but subdue him. "His figure is darkened, almost blended into invisibility with the background . . . the arrangement of his hands and face as the light plays over them — all direct the attention of the viewer past the man himself and into the focal point of the picture, the woman sitting beside him" (p. 120). He becomes "a mere suggestion of man, leading the viewer into a far more appealing overall image — that of a handsome young couple, alone together on a moonlit beach, heightened emotionally with the carefully-blended-in suggestions of escape, intimacy, and a sense of shared daring" (p. 120). The primary image (man-smoking) makes the scene acceptable; once the woman accepts the scene, she accepts "Blow some my way" as a natural action she might take.

**The Limit (p. 121)**: "There is only one limit to the number, or the range of favorable images that you can add to that product personality. Simply that you must always include the primary image as their base. They must emerge from that primary image, and they must be logically consistent with its broadest definition." Marlboro could then weave in: tuxedo for success, boats/planes/horseback for adventure, a young girl for romance, a dark background or briefcase for affairs of importance, a rare book or antique telescope for culture (p. 121).

**Hypothetical worked build-out — piston rings (pp. 121–123)**: starting inventory is bleak: "mechanical, precision-made, unseen. Nothing exciting here. It also has a primary situation — replacement, and always by a mechanic, and usually only when there's trouble" (p. 122). Schwartz dramatizes those constraints upward: car becomes a Mercedes SL-300 sports car; garage becomes a "sports-car shop . . . Neat, clean, precise. With high-power equipment . . . gleaming chrome and steel" (p. 122); mechanic is "not young, not old, simply mature. Rugged, knowledgeable, precise" (pp. 122–123); owner is "young, wiry, virile, but he too has been around" (p. 123) — a non-professional driver who races for sport (roll-over bar tells us so) and carries "his success with a complete casualness (the absence of any special driving outfit — just slacks and a sport shirt)." Relationship between the two men: "Knowledge complementing skill . . . Comfort, understanding, teamwork — leading to mutual achievement" (p. 123). And what are they doing? Replacing rings — but not because of trouble. "Because this mechanic is installing these new rings in this car as high-performance equipment, exactly as he would install a supercharger to increase its horsepower" (p. 123). "It is a picture, a situation and a mood that invites participation . . . He will wish to share in this world. And he will buy the product that gives him this world — that offers it as a bonus to all its functional and physical satisfactions" (p. 124).

**Verbal vs. visual caveat (p. 123)**: stated in words, these images "must take on a measure of disbelief, which we express by saying they're 'corny.' However, expressed in visual terms, where they can be symbolized, and therefore implied rather than named, they enter into our minds unnoticed, and we accept them without question."

### Limits to Identification (p. 124)
"Many campaigns have collapsed because they have asked their market to identify themselves with an unbelievable image. For example, the deodorant-soap campaign of several years ago that used an elite, society image to sell the mass market. The men and women who composed the market could not make the jump between themselves and the characters pictured in the ad. And they not only refused to believe the suggested identification, but the disbelief spread to the performance claims themselves, and killed the sales" (p. 124).

Two diagnostic questions for image-building (p. 124):
1. "What do people already believe about the personality of your product? Do they believe, today, that it has the character traits that you say it has? And — if they do — can they identify themselves, their lives, their present position in society, or their next step up in society with these traits?" If yes/yes, "you can use the image exactly as you've conceived it" (pp. 124–125).
2. "What other primary image do I have to use as a believability-bridge to connect what my prospect already believes with what I want him to believe when he finishes my ad?" (p. 125). If the prospect doesn't believe the product has those traits, start with what he already believes and build up. If he believes the traits exist but not that they relate to his life, "insert a first-seen image that he can immediately identify with, and use that acceptable identification as a hook to hang my target-image on to" (p. 125). Marlboro example: men who'd never identify with a yachting costume or opera hat accepted those once introduced via the acceptable virility concept of the male model smoking the cigarette (p. 125).

Rule: "If you demand that your prospect jump across a believability-chasm, your ad will fail. If, however, you build a bridge of ideas or images across that chasm — starting on his side — then he will let you lead him almost anywhere" (p. 125).

### Identifications from the Physical Product (p. 125)
Every product is two products: "the functional product — what the product does for the consumer. And there is the physical product — what the consumer actually gets" (p. 125). The physical product has three image-source areas (p. 126):
1. Its appearance.
2. Its components and structure.
3. The technical background from which it emerged.

In each you can find primary images that already exist, or graft on related images. Example for cars (p. 126): "borrow from the glamour cars — the severe streamlining and stabilizing fin of the racing car — the hub caps and wheel wells of the sports car — the exhaust ports and roof line of the custom car. And you blend their feeling of excitement and power and distinction into the family sedan."

For chemical/non-mechanical products, "their physical appearance, to the manufacturer, is completely arbitrary . . . their physical package can take any shape you wish" (p. 127). Borrow images from background, components, or societal values. **Micrin (Johnson & Johnson) example (p. 127)**: a mouthwash whose container was shaped like the glass containers "found only in doctors' offices and operating rooms . . . Use this shape to house an ice-blue liquid, that in itself suggests medical cleanliness, and you have a product that says instantly, 'This is doctor's-liquid . . . This is medicine. This works.'" "The container itself becomes the finest image-illustration that the agency could use in creating their ad" (p. 127).

For products with electronic components: give them "an electronic flavor to both [their] appearance and [their] advertising. Show the radar installations, guided missiles, jet aircraft that use the same parts. Life for the average man is dull, completely lacking in adventure. Offer to let him participate in the frontier explorations of our world, and you have welded a tremendously potent appeal onto your product" (pp. 127–128).

### Key insights
- Identification is desire-for-recognition, not desire-for-satisfaction (p. 115).
- Character roles are believed *more readily* than performance claims because they are never tested (p. 112).
- Achievement roles must be *displayed* via possessions to count socially (p. 113).
- The primary image is immutable; you build *onto* it, never against it (pp. 115–116, 119).
- Images delivered visually slip past disbelief that words ("corny") would trigger (p. 123).
- The primary image must remain the base of every additional appeal (p. 121).
- Use a bridge-image when the prospect can't or won't make the leap (p. 125).

### Examples
- Marlboro: virile model + virile situation + tattoo (p. 118); later layered with tuxedo, boat, plane, horseback, rare book, antique telescope (p. 121).
- Chesterfield "Blow Some My Way" 1926 — subdued man + romantic moonlit beach scene (pp. 119–120).
- Micrin mouthwash — surgical-glass container + ice-blue liquid (p. 127).
- Hypothetical piston-ring ad — Mercedes SL-300 + sports-car shop + virile owner + master mechanic, treating ring replacement as supercharger upgrade (pp. 122–123).
- Deodorant-soap that flopped: society-elite imagery for a mass market (p. 124).
- Newly-wed and wedding ring; "Man-On-His-Way-Up" trading Ford → Buick → Cadillac (p. 114).

### Copy patterns
- Primary-image emphasis: take the strongest already-accepted association and deepen it via repetition through variation (single dominant emotion, three symbolizations) (p. 118).
- Bridge construction: "starting on his side . . . then he will let you lead him almost anywhere" (p. 125).
- Visual implication over verbal naming when the role would sound "corny" stated outright (p. 123).
- Package-as-symbol: borrow the form of an authority context (doctor's office) to communicate the claim without making it (p. 127).

## CHAPTER 9 — Gradualization (book pp. 129–152)

### Core concept
Gradualization is the third mechanism of persuasion: building belief into a claim *before* the claim is stated, by structuring the ad as a step-by-step chain of acceptances that ends in absolute conviction. "Gradualization is the art of stating a claim in such a way that it will receive the greatest possible acceptance and/or believability from your prospect" (p. 144). The copywriter wants the fusion of desire and belief — "this conviction — this certainty — this feeling in the prospect of being right in his choice — of being assured of what he has been promised" — which "the copy writer seeks as his ultimate goal" (p. 130). Power claims fail not because they're untrue but because the prospect hasn't been prepared to accept them; you delay the goal-claim until the chain of yeses can carry it (pp. 134–135).

### What Belief Is (p. 130)
"It is perhaps the most complex fusion of thought and emotion in the human mind. It is, first of all, your prospect's mental picture of the world he lives in — what facts make it up, how it works, in what direction its truths and values lie" (p. 130). Beyond the facts, belief gives "the wonderful feeling of comfort and reassurance of living in a world that has meaning, where there are answers to be had, where somehow the facts all fit. A world that he can understand and depend on, that he can predict, that will stay together and not blow up in his face tomorrow morning or the morning after that" (pp. 130–131).

Schwartz's basic rule of belief (p. 131): "If you violate your prospect's established beliefs in the slightest degree — either in content or direction — then nothing you promise him, no matter how appealing, can save your ad. But, on the other hand . . . If you can channel the tremendous force of his belief — either in content or direction — behind only one claim, no matter how small, then that one fully-believed claim will sell more goods than all the half-questioned promises your competitors can write for all the rest of their days. This channeling of belief is so powerful that, if properly directed, it will even support otherwise-absurd claims" (p. 131). "It is simply a question of whether you are going to paddle upstream or down" (p. 131). "As far as advertising is concerned, then, belief is immutable" (p. 131).

### The Architecture of Belief (p. 133)
The theory rests on this fact (p. 133): "every claim, every image, every proof in your ad has two separate sources of strength —
1. The content of that statement itself; and
2. The preparation you have made for that statement — either by recognizing that preparation as already existing in your prospect's mind, or by deliberately laying the groundwork for that statement in the preceding portion of the ad itself."

Therefore each statement can be strengthened two ways (p. 133):
1. By increasing the intensity of its content — bigger promises, more dramatic images, more compelling proof.
2. "By changing the place or position or sequence in which that statement occurs in the ad — by strengthening the groundwork for belief in that statement by the material which precedes it — and therefore increasing the intensity of belief given to it — the immediate acceptance of its content, without question, when the prospect encounters it in your ad."

"Effective advertising, like effective literature, is built — not of words — but of reactions. We put down on paper an architecture of words. If these words are effective, they evoke, in turn, an architecture of reactions in our prospect's mind. We are creating a stream of acceptances, with a definite sequence and content and direction, and, if we are successful, with a definite goal — the absolute conviction in your prospect's mind that he must have your product" (pp. 133–134).

### A New Definition of Awareness (p. 134)
"Gradualization is the art of starting your ad with a statement that will be immediately and entirely accepted, and then building a chain of subsequent acceptances upon this first statement. The purpose of this chain of acceptances is to lead your reader to a goal conclusion, which he will then accept, but which he would not as readily or as thoroughly have accepted without the preliminary statements" (p. 134).

The headline must be capable not only of awakening interest and desire, "but of being accepted at the very first glance as being true" (p. 134). State of Awareness can therefore be redefined as **"readiness to accept"**: "the effectiveness of your headline is as much determined by the willingness of your audience to believe what it says, as it is by the promises it makes" (p. 134). "This is the reason that you cannot always use the most powerful claim in your headline. Or even the very problem that your product solves. Because without supporting evidence already existing in the mind of your prospect to prepare him for that headline claim, he just won't believe it" (p. 134). Mail-order advertisers prove this routinely: when a power-claim headline fails, "they immediately split it against a second head, with far fewer claims in it, but far more likely to be believed. Then they build a belief-bridge from this second headline, to the same exact claims they had featured in the first, but now anticipated by careful preparation every step along the way" (p. 135).

### Detailed worked example — Television Repair Manual (1951) (pp. 135–141)
Two facts had to be believed before the prospect would buy: (1) he could save money by making his own TV repairs, and (2) he was capable of making them (p. 135). Both were factually true (≈80% of repairs were simple), but the average prospect thought of himself as a non-handyman and the TV set as "a complicated mechanical monster, far beyond their poor powers to understand" (p. 136). The obvious power-claim headline — "Save up to $100 a Year on Your TV Repairs!" — was tested and lost money; "Power alone could not move the product" (p. 136).

**The new headline** (p. 136): **"WHY HAVEN'T TV OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS?"** "No claim. No promise — except that of disclosure. But here is an implication of foul play, which echoed the existing suspicion in the TV owner himself . . . Facts have been withheld. This is something he can agree with!" (p. 137).

**Sentence 2 (Inclusion-Question)**: "Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947?" — "95% of television owners would answer Yes" (p. 137). The form looks like it's limiting the market; the content actually grants the desired Yes from nearly everyone.

**Sentence 3 (first definite promise)**: "Then here is the full, uncensored story of how you can avoid those $15-$20 repair bills — avoid those $30-$60 a year service fees — and still get the perfect, movie-clear pictures you've dreamed about!" (p. 137).

**How belief was built into the opening (pp. 137–138)**:
1. Grammatical bridges. "Was your . . ." (sentence 2) and "Then . . ." (sentence 3) "by [their] very form generate belief." "Then" lends "credence to whatever statement follows it"; remove the word and "it loses half its force — force added to the content of the statement by the grammatical-bridge tying it in to the first two acceptances" (pp. 137–138).
2. Descriptive richness of the promise — not just reward (money saved) but a "catalog of almost-universal symptoms (repair bills and service fees)," each of which evokes "Yes — I have them" reactions that get carried over to the money-saving claims that follow (p. 138).
3. Cures left ambiguous. The set owner's problems are described specifically; how the cures will work is "deliberately left out . . . because the reader has not yet been prepared for it. If he were to learn, at this moment in the ad, that he had to make repairs on his set to save this money, the average reader would turn the page" (p. 138).

The next paragraph adds Yes-reinforcement via universal symptoms: "How many times this week have you had to get up to fix a jumpy TV picture? . . . How many times have you had to put up with ghosts? . . ." (pp. 138–139). "Yes . . . Yes . . . Yes" — "A habit of acceptance is being built inside him. Trust is being formed, layer by layer — as each question poses a test, and each yes answer proves to the reader that the ad is talking about him" (p. 139). Then the next promise: **"90% Of These Breakdowns Are Unnecessary!"** plus the dramatic reframe that "the same exact set that you now have in your front room . . . has been playing in manufacturer's test rooms for months — and playing perfectly!" (p. 139).

### Goal Conclusions vs Ultimate Objective (pp. 139–144)

**Goal Conclusions (p. 139)**: the ad has two intermediate goal-conclusions to reach before the close — (1) "TV sets are not fragile — they have amazing endurance if they are properly cared for"; (2) "whatever minor breakdowns do occur can be easily handled by the owner himself" (p. 139). Each goal-conclusion is reached only after its own foundation is laid.

To establish dependability, the ad uses graphic test descriptions (p. 139): sets "subjected to 'Breakdown Tests' that would seem incredible to the average owner . . . turned on for 24 hours a day — 7 days a week — 4 weeks a month. Some of these sets have been running without a moment's pause for as much as 17 months" (pp. 139–140); tested against viewing hazards, 120 miles from station, against entire warehouses of electrical interference, in steel-ribbed buildings (p. 140). Then it pivots to expert authority and logical construction: "What TV Experts Have Learned About Your Set!" "If your set were properly cared for — as these sets were cared for in these tests — it need break down only once during the entire year!" etc. (p. 140). The ad "returns to the claims made in the third sentence — repeats them almost word for word — and then gives logical proof, in logical form, for each of them" (p. 140). "Thus a chain of proof upon proof is constructed — each new statement repeating the heart of the proof before it" (p. 141). Conclusion 1 is now proved by intensification — same theme, seven different ways (p. 141).

**The Ultimate Objective (p. 141)**: now prove conclusion 2 — that the owner can correct minor breakdowns himself. The transition uses a paragraph whose novelty is "deliberately concealed; it is presented as though it were simply another rephrasing of the by-now already accepted dependability conclusion" (pp. 141–142). The new claim ("you do not have to be a handyman or a mechanic in order to coax this performance out of your set!") is wrapped into the already-accepted stream by four constructions (p. 142):
1. Paragraph parallelism — formatted identically to the accepted paragraphs.
2. Lead word "And" — tying it to what came before.
3. "most important" — implying continuation of the same series.
4. Repetition of "these experts have discovered" — picking up the accepted subhead.

These let the reader "make what otherwise might be a jarring transition from already-established proof to an entirely new promise with a minimum of effort" (p. 142). The ad then redefines "repairs" by analogy to the human body: TV gives "warning signals before it has a major breakdown" (pp. 142–143), and "if you had the knowledge to quickly make a few minor adjustments, on the outside controls of your set, then you could correct those symptoms . . . If you do not have this knowledge . . . your set will weaken, you will get a consistently bad picture, and you will have to call a repairman" (p. 143).

The final close has "all the inevitable logical force of a syllogism" (p. 143):
- Set is dependable enough to avoid major breakdowns.
- Owner with the proper knowledge can correct minor breakdowns himself.
- Therefore: get the knowledge, save the service contracts and repair bills.

Closer: **"It's as simple as that. You pay a repairman — not for his work — but for his knowledge. If you had that knowledge yourself — then you would not have to pay him at all."** (p. 143).

### A Restatement of Our Basic Theory (p. 144)
"Gradualization is the art of stating a claim in such a way that it will receive the greatest possible acceptance and/or believability from your prospect.
Belief ultimately depends upon structure. Just as desire depends upon promise, so belief in that promise depends upon the amount of preparation that promise has been given before your reader is asked to accept it.
One fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten partially-believed promises. Most copy writers try to strengthen ads by piling promise upon promise. What they usually get for their troubles is greater sales resistance from their prospects . . . They could far better invest the same time in strengthening the believability-structure of the original justifiable promise" (p. 144).

### The Inclusion Question (p. 145)
"Designed to permit immediate identification with your story. To show the prospect that you're talking about him — not about someone else who would answer No to the question. Therefore, once he's identified with the questions — once he's made his first agreements with you and placed himself in the Yes-answer group — then your recommendations will have special meaning for him. This is perhaps the most direct way of building agreement at the beginning of the ad" (p. 145). Example from *The Art of Selfishness* ad (p. 145):
> ASK YOURSELF THESE NINE QUESTIONS
> 1. Do you find it increasingly difficult to cope with the world around you. . . .
> 2. Is your business or career a source of annoyance and frustration. . . .
> 3. Are you tormented by inadequacies, fear and embarrassments in your sex life. . . .
> And so on.

### Detailed Identification (p. 145)
"Another device used at the beginning of the ad, to establish immediate, and deep, agreement between the reader and the copy. Here, instead of asking questions to set up your Yes-train, you detail symptoms or problems that are your prospect's reasons for desiring your product. Thus, again, your reader knows that you are talking about him — that you 'have been there yourself' — and therefore that your recommendations will help answer these problems, his problems, that you have catalogued so well" (p. 145). Example for a child-grades course (p. 146): "Let me explain. I don't care whether your child is six years old or twenty — boy or girl — in grade school high school or college. It makes no difference how badly that child is doing in school today — how difficult it is for him to concentrate . . . how poor his memory may be . . . how much a prisoner he is of crippling mental habits . . ."

Caveat: "your copy must be accurate. You must know enough about the reader's problems to make every word you write ring true. If you don't, you'll shatter your believability-net, and he'll simply turn the page. So — before you write — research. Learn to know your customer" (p. 146).

### Contradiction of Present (False) Beliefs (p. 146)
"Used at the beginning of the ad . . . to prepare a foundation for strong claim-statements that the reader might never accept raw. Here, you come bluntly out and say, 'I know you think this is true; but I'm going to show YOU it's false.' Best used, of course, in conjunction with strong authority — strong enough to contradict present (unpleasant) beliefs, and get away with it" (p. 146). Example — cosmetics by a famous plastic surgeon (p. 146):
> From this moment on, forget everything you have ever heard or read about what age "must do" to your appearance. Forget anything you have ever believed about how "old" you must look at thirty . . . forty . . . fifty . . . or even sixty. . . .
"Here you are not looking for agreement as much as you are for a loosening of previous beliefs. You are saying that the old limitations are passing, and your next paragraph should be your first introduction of your positive claims" (p. 147), e.g.: "Because — starting with this moment — you are about to enter into a new world of beauty! A world where ordinary fruits are transformed into anti-wrinkle cosmetics . . ." (p. 147).

### The Language of Logic (p. 147)
"You interlace each new promise with language-signals that show that it logically follows from everything that has been proved before. And that it therefore can be believed without hesitation. What are these language-signals? They are, of course, the vocabulary of logic. They are the words we use when we reason: when we argue; when we prove our point in any discussion, and force others to agree with us that we are right" (p. 147). After centuries of conditioning in court, politics, science, and detective fiction, "the words themselves — regardless of the content of the statements to which they are attached — now carry conviction. Therefore, they should be woven throughout your ad, wherever they logically apply" (p. 148).

Sample lines (p. 148):
- Sherwin Cody ad: "Why do so many find themselves at a loss for words to express their meaning adequately? *The reason for this deficiency is clear*. . . . Most persons do not write or speak good English *simply because* they have never formed the habit of doing so."
- Difficult-people book: "*Take, as an example,* the man who habitually refuses to follow your instructions. *There is a basic, underlying reason for this.* Mr. Given shows you how to find that reason and then explains the means of correcting it. The whole solution can be surprisingly easy *once you have realized the underlying causes*. . . ."
- Learning-how-to-learn book: "but simply by putting your LOCKED-UP LEARNING POWERS to work — today — *as easily and logically as this*. . . ."

A standing armory of logic-phrases (pp. 148–149):
- "This has been proved by thousands. . . ."
- "Sound impossible? Not at all. It's actually as simple. . . ."
- "Here's why. . . ."
- "And, most important of all, is the fact that. . . ."
- "Therefore. . . ."
- "This was, without a doubt, the most thorough. . . ."
- "They discovered — in case after case — that. . . ."

"This, again, is the language of logic. It is a language equally as filled with emotion as the language of desire. Interwoven into your promises, so subtly that the reader never even notices that it is there, it gives your claims the invaluable air of conviction" (p. 149).

### Syllogistic Thinking (p. 149)
"Now we go from the language of logic to the mechanisms of logic. This is the role that reason plays in your ad. This is the moment when you prove that your product works, through the mechanism of logical reasoning" (p. 149). Spark-plug example (p. 149): the plug cost twice as much because it delivered a larger spark. The ad chains:
> Your car runs because gasoline is fed into the cylinders where a spark causes it to fire. This action causes the gas to explode . . . this explosion pushes down the piston. Now here is the important thing to you. The larger the spark is, the more powerful the explosion. The more powerful the explosion, the more power you get from your gasoline.
> Poor explosion means wasted gas — loss of power, poor getaway, bad starting, a sluggish car.
> Good explosion means more miles per gallon — more horsepower; a more exciting car to drive!

"One statement inevitably leads into another. Every word is logical. There is a constant process of equation: spark to power, power to performance. Size equals power — and his plug delivers the largest size" (p. 150). "Such structure — and the copy based on such a structure — develops the feeling of inevitability. The reader feels that the product must work. He has not only been told it works; he has been shown proof that it works" (p. 150). The structure is "a physical entity. You can seek it out of hundreds of ads, if you look beneath the words themselves. You can trace it, codify it, and then repeat it. Once learned, it becomes a powerful tool in selling hundreds of products" (p. 150).

### Other Belief Forms (p. 150)
"Once you grasp the fundamental idea that form — structure — determines believability, then all sorts of opportunities open up to you. You realize that simply by the arrangement of your claims, you can add to their believability" (p. 150).
- **Contingency Structures** — "If . . . then . . ." or "Was your . . . then . . ." (p. 150).
- **Repetition of Proof: Echoing** — "These experts found . . . . These experts found . . . ." (p. 150).
- **Promise–Belief–Promise Variation** — every sentence of promise followed (ideally) by another of proof, verification, or documentation, "so that the reader never has the breathing space to question" (p. 150).
- **Paragraph Parallelism** — "Where the same word structure used in an accepted statement is then picked up exactly, and used to borrow acceptance for a fresh claim" (p. 150).
"All have the same objective. To gain continued acceptance. To prevent rejection. To build conviction. Belief is the goal" (p. 151).

### Key insights
- Belief is structural, not merely content-driven; sequence and preparation can multiply the believability of any single sentence (p. 133).
- Awareness = readiness to accept (p. 134). The headline is bound by what the prospect will *believe* on first glance, not just by promise size.
- One fully-believed promise outsells ten partially-believed ones tenfold (p. 144).
- Rule: never violate established belief; always paddle downstream (p. 131).
- Build a Yes-train early (Inclusion-Question, Detailed Identification, universal symptoms) so that later, harder claims ride on accepted momentum (pp. 137–139, 145).
- New claims should be smuggled in as if they were continuations of accepted ones — paragraph parallelism, "And . . ." lead-ins, repeated identifying phrases (p. 142).
- The closing claim should land as a syllogism whose premises the reader has already accepted (p. 143).

### Examples
- TV Repair Manual 1951 — "Why Haven't TV Owners Been Told These Facts?" full ad anatomy (pp. 135–143).
- *The Art of Selfishness* — nine-question Inclusion-Question opener (p. 145).
- Child-grades course — Detailed Identification opener (p. 146).
- Plastic-surgeon cosmetics — Contradiction of Present Beliefs opener (p. 146).
- Sherwin Cody ad and difficult-people book — Language of Logic in single lines (p. 148).
- Spark-plug ad — three-paragraph syllogistic chain (p. 149).

### Copy patterns
- Inclusion-Question stack: "Do you find it increasingly difficult to . . . / Is your . . . a source of . . . / Are you tormented by . . ." (p. 145).
- Yes-cascade by universal symptoms: "How many times this week have you had to . . . / How many times have you had to . . ." (pp. 138–139).
- Contingency contract: "Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947?" → "Then here is the full, uncensored story . . ." (p. 137).
- Authority-led contradiction: "From this moment on, forget everything you have ever heard or read about . . ." (p. 146).
- Logical bridges: "The reason for this deficiency is clear. . . . simply because . . ." (p. 148); "Take, as an example . . . There is a basic, underlying reason for this . . . once you have realized the underlying causes . . ." (p. 148).
- Phrase armory: "This has been proved by thousands . . . / Here's why . . . / And, most important of all, is the fact that . . . / Therefore . . . / They discovered — in case after case — that . . ." (pp. 148–149).
- Syllogistic close: state the major premise, the minor premise, drop the inevitable conclusion ("It's as simple as that . . .") (p. 143).
- Smuggle-the-new-claim package: paragraph parallelism + "And" + "most important" + repeated identifying subhead ("these experts have discovered . . .") (p. 142).

## Common mistakes / warnings (across both chapters)
- Forcing an unrealistic identification on a market that won't make the leap; disbelief spreads to the performance claims and kills the sale (p. 124).
- Substituting a more flattering primary image for the existing one; "people just won't believe that a product is what it isn't" (p. 119).
- Demanding the prospect jump a believability-chasm without a bridge-image (p. 125).
- Leading with the most powerful claim regardless of whether the prospect is ready to accept it (pp. 134–136); the obvious power headline failed in the TV Repair test (p. 136).
- Piling promise upon promise instead of strengthening the believability-structure of one promise (p. 144).
- Inaccurate Detailed Identification — if a single symptom doesn't ring true, the believability-net shatters (p. 146).
- Stating identification roles in words that read as "corny" rather than implying them visually (p. 123).
- Violating established belief "in the slightest degree — either in content or direction"; nothing else in the ad can save it (p. 131).

## Ethical / honesty notes
Schwartz is explicit that gradualization is structural manipulation in the service of conviction; he describes belief that, "if properly directed, [will] even support otherwise-absurd claims" (p. 131). He frames this as channeling, not deceiving — but he does not draw a hard ethical line in these chapters. The closest thing to a guardrail is the **accuracy rule** for Detailed Identification: "your copy must be accurate. You must know enough about the reader's problems to make every word you write ring true. If you don't, you'll shatter your believability-net, and he'll simply turn the page" (p. 146). The implicit defense: structural belief-building only works when the underlying facts hold up; an "absurd" claim might survive the first read but won't survive the experience of using the product. He also stresses that you cannot make people believe a product is what it isn't (p. 119) — not as ethics but as ineffectiveness. In the Identification chapter the ambiguity-of-character-roles passage is especially candid: roles are believed *because* they are "subject to fantasy" and never tested (p. 112).
