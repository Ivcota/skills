# Intensification (Technique #1) — extraction notes (Ch 7, book pp. 77–106)

## Core concept

Intensification is Schwartz's First Mechanism of Persuasion — the act of taking a desire that already exists in the prospect's mind and making it sharper, more vivid, more concrete, and more emotionally weighted by presenting a continuous series of fresh fulfillment-images. He puts it first because desire is the engine: "The force that creates sales, that powers our present economy, is desire" (p. 77). Existing desires are "indistinct... blurs — hazy, ambiguous, not yet crystallized into words or images... they have only a fraction of their true potential power" (pp. 77–78). The copywriter's first job is to fill out those vague desires with concrete images, and "to multiply their strength by the number of satisfactions that you can suggest to achieve them" (p. 78). Crucially, Intensification is not repetition — "You cannot repeat, but you can reinforce" (p. 79). Each new viewpoint must be a fresh perspective on the same dominant promise so that "with every additional new and different way that you can present that idea, it becomes sharper and more real in your prospect's mind, and it builds up more and more emotional weight" (p. 79). The chapter's running thesis: "The sharper you can draw your pictures — and the greater the number of them that you can legitimately present — the more your prospect will demand your product, and the less important will seem your price" (p. 78).

## The Thirteen Ways to Strengthen Desire (p. 77)

1. **Your First Presentation of Your Claims** (p. 80) — Open with the product or its satisfaction directly and bluntly, via "a thorough, completely detailed description of its appearance or the results it gives" (p. 80). Pile sensory specifics: counts, dimensions, colors, sounds. Example: a rose ad headlined "MORE ROSES THAN YOU EVER SAW ON ANY ROSE BUSH..." that lists "500 blooms in June... 1523 more blooms in July... 1616 more blooms in August... 437 more blooms in September... 4,076 roses all from one single plant" (p. 80). Companion examples: a Christmas Bible-slide projector painted as a glowing four-foot-wide picture of the Virgin Mary "so real that you can practically reach out and touch her" (p. 82); a Pan American plastic model jet rendered with "tail section alone stands six full inches from the table" and "JET SOUND SIMULATOR" (p. 83).

2. **Put the Claims in Action** (p. 83) — After the static description, move the product. "PUT THE PRODUCT IN ACTION for your reader. To show, not only how the product looks, and what benefits it gives the reader, but exactly how it does this" (p. 83). The model-jet ad continues: "Pick this magnificent plastic model up from the table, and hand it to your child... Before your astonished eyes, you will see one of the most thrilling sights of your entire life!" (p. 83). Self-propelled fish lure example: lure "will spring dramatically to 'life'... filled with the buzzing sound of a dying bee... like a maimed minnow, it will swim noisily downward — buzzing and humming — traveling about nine feet every fifteen seconds" (pp. 84–85).

3. **Bring In the Reader** (p. 85) — "Put your reader right smack in the middle of this product-in-action story, and give him a verbal demonstration of [what] will happen to him the first day he owns that product" (p. 85). Power-booster ad: "Picture this to yourself! Next weekend you go down to your car... when you turn on the ignition, a modern miracle of engineering science comes to life under your hood!... pull up to another car at the stop light... slam your foot down on the gas pedal!... you will leave him a full block behind... you will look in your rear view mirror and see the startled look of amazement on the other driver's face!" (pp. 85–86).

4. **Show Him How to Test Your Claims** (p. 86) — "Turn the demonstration into a test. Let your reader visualize himself proving the performance of your product — gaining its benefits immediately — in the most specific and dramatic way possible" (p. 86). SA-FIRE INJECTORS ad walks the reader through measuring crawl-speed, RPM, and low-gear speed with motor idling before and after install, ending: "no matter what you drive, here is absolute proof that you can go further, faster and cheaper..." (p. 86). Memory-course ad: "Give Me One Evening and I'll Give You a Push-Button Memory" — instructs reader to read 8 pages, then sit down and write 20 facts they could never memorize before, then amaze friends and family the next morning (p. 87).

5. **Stretch Out Your Benefits in Time** (p. 87) — Show the product working not for an hour or a day "but over a span of weeks and months. Here you extend your reader's vision further and further into time — showing him a continuous flow of benefits" (p. 88). English plant-food ad walks pellets through three sequential stages: a "tremendous new burst of growth" the first month, a second wonder-working nutrient "within one or two short weeks," then a third ingredient that releases when plants reach full height, climaxing in "AND YOU WILL BE BLINDED BY THE EXPLOSION OF COLOR THAT GREETS YOU IN YOUR GARDEN!" and "A FEELING OF GARDEN ACCOMPLISHMENT AND PRIDE THAT YOU NEVER DREAMED OF BEFORE!" (p. 88).

6. **Bring In an Audience** (p. 89) — "Other actors besides the reader are brought into the scene. Each one of them — each group of them — provides a fresh new perspective through which your reader can view the product. Seen through their eyes — experienced through their actions and reactions — the product performances become new, vivid and completely different again" (p. 89). Three sub-flavors: celebrities (testimonial), average men/women whom the reader identifies with (narration / case history — e.g. "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano," p. 89), and reducing-ad case histories where readers learn how others "lost as much as 17 and 25 and even 34 pounds... had turned back their weight clock 5, 10 and even 15 years" (p. 90).

7. **Show Experts Approving** (p. 90) — "Experts in the field — professionals — the sophisticated, the discriminating, the blasé can be called on to register their reactions. There is nothing so astounding as the astonishment of experts. Here the elements of surprise, competition and discovery all combine to sharpen the image even more" (p. 90). Nearly Wild rose ad stages a college horticulturist test against "all the highly-praised queens of rosedom" with a famous rose expert exclaiming, "Why, it's like having a complete rose garden of 20 plants — all on a single bush!" and another saying, "If we ever released these flowering figures to the public, they'd never believe it" (pp. 90–91).

8. **Compare, Contrast, Prove Superiority** (p. 91) — "The competition can be carried into contrast. The disadvantages of the old product or service can be laid side by side with the advantages of the new — throwing these advantages into sharp relief" (p. 91). Dollar Book Club radio spot: "While your friends were paying $3.95 for their copies of Frank Yerby's run-away best seller, The Golden Hawk, Dollar Book Club members were getting the same exact book for only 99¢..." (p. 91). Fuel Reclaimer direct-mail piece contrasts "polluted oil, polluted engine, polluted air" against "young-car power, young-car economy, young-car resistance to repair bills" (p. 92).

9. **Picture the Black Side, Too** (p. 92) — "There's no need to neglect the Heaven-Or-Hell approach. Here the negative aspect to every promise — the problem that you are liberating your prospect from forever — is painted in all its full black color. You irritate the wound, and then you apply the salve that heals it" (p. 92). "Thus you derive two currents of motivation — repulsion away from the former problem or inadequate product, and the attraction generated by your own product's contrasting solutions" (p. 92). Spark-plug ad: "lift one of the old-fashioned spark plugs out of your car!... what you'll see on the bottom of that plug is FILTH! The Firing Point... will be choked, strangled and fouled with BLACK, FILTHY CARBON! Carbon that robs your car of as much as 20 vital horsepower... CORRODED — PITTED — SCARRED — AND WORN!" before pivoting to the new Power Flash plug "AT LAST! A PLUG SO SMART THAT IT THINKS!" (pp. 92–93).

10. **Show How Easy It Is To Get These Benefits** (p. 94) — "At every point that your product touches the life of your prospect — price, availability, ease of use, durability, portability, replacement and maintenance, even unwrapping the carton it comes in — it furnishes you with another fresh perspective in which to reiterate and reemphasize its benefits" (p. 94). Engine-additive ad: "lift up its hood... pour its contents into the oil filler pipe of your car!... that's all the work you will do! That's all the skill you need! And yet in that 30 seconds work, you will improve that car's performance in eight different ways!" — repeating "with just 30 seconds work" three times across mileage, lubrication, and engine-life claims (p. 94).

11. **Use Metaphor, Analogy, Imagination** (p. 94) — "Nor do you have to be satisfied merely with the statement of [bare] fact. There are infinite opportunities for the use of imagination to present those facts in more dramatic form, outside of the rigidly realistic approach" (p. 94). Cited canon: Alexander Hamilton ad of the Twenties "The Glory of the Upward Path"; Big Ben alarm clock series "First he whispers, then he shouts"; and the Sherwin Cody English Course personifying its grammar manual: "Suppose he himself were standing forever at your elbow. Every time you mispronounced or misspelled a word... suppose you could hear him whisper, 'That is wrong, it should be thus and so'... 100% Self-Correcting Device" (pp. 94–95).

12. **Before You're Done, Summarize** (p. 95) — "There is an infinite number of new approaches. No list of them can ever be complete... Which of them, and how many of them you will use in a single ad, is a matter of timing and balance... As long as each additional fresh perspective continues to build the dominant desire in your prospect's mind, use it. But if the additional perspective is not different or dramatic enough to renew your prospect's interest in your claims, then leave it out" (pp. 95–96). Two conventional summary devices follow:
    - **The "catalog"** — "a brief condensed listing of all the product's performances, benefits, and/or applications, one after the other, without description, dramatization or elaboration" (p. 96). Two types: catalogs that "expand desire horizontally, among ever-enlargening groups of applications or prospects" (e.g., "ALL IN ONE! Blow-Torch, Paint-Remover, Soldering Iron... Does 101 jobs around the house" — p. 96) and catalogs that "expand desire vertically by deepening or magnifying that desire" (e.g., the memory-book "over 50 MEMORY INTENSIFIERS" sequence, pp. 97–98). The shotgun-vs-rifle metaphor: the body of the ad has been a "high-velocity rifle" hitting one dominant desire; the catalog at the end "shotguns" with every wide-angled appeal in case one of them closes the sale (p. 97).

13. **Put Your Guarantee to Work** (p. 99) — "As you close the sale, as you ask the prospect for action, as you state the terms of your guarantee, you can turn that guarantee into the climax of your ad — the last brief summary of your product's performances — reinforced at every step by the positive reassertion of that guarantee" (p. 99). Power Flash spark-plug guarantee: "Guaranteed For Two Full Years!... 1. These plugs must give you up to 9 miles more per gallon instantly — or your full purchase price back! 2. These plugs must give you up to 31 more horsepower instantly — or your full purchase price back! 3. As an extra added assurance — these plugs must continue to give you this power, performance and gas savings FOR TWO FULL YEARS — or we will send you a brand-new set ABSOLUTELY FREE!" (pp. 99–100). Magic English Pellets ad uses guarantee to triple-stack desire with "USE THEM TO CREATE SUPER-FLOWERS!... USE THEM TO CREATE SUPER-VEGETABLES!... YES! USE THEM IN YOUR ENTIRE GARDEN!" — each tied to a money-back clause (p. 100).

## Sub-techniques (each typically a section heading)

### Put the Claims in Action (p. 83)
After static description, add motion. Key quote: "PUT THE PRODUCT IN ACTION for your reader. To show, not only how the product looks, and what benefits it gives the reader, but exactly how it does this" (p. 83). The fish-lure passage is the showcase: the lure floats one minute, then water reaches the fuel charge, "the lure will seem to shudder for a second — and then it will spring dramatically to 'life'!" (p. 84). Action copy keeps adding verbs: "lift up... pour... cast... lower... swim... spring... buzz."

### Bring In the Reader (p. 85)
"If your product lends itself to this kind of treatment, put your reader right smack in the middle of this product-in-action story, and give him a verbal demonstration of [what] will happen to him the first day he owns that product" (p. 85). Done in second person, future-perfect: "Picture this to yourself! Next weekend you go down to your car..." (p. 85). Anchor in a familiar moment ("the same tired car that you've been driving for years"), then walk through a sequenced sensory script.

### Show Him How to Test Your Claims (p. 86)
"There is still more room to expand the image along these lines. Turn the demonstration into a test. Let your reader visualize himself proving the performance of your product — gaining its benefits immediately — in the most specific and dramatic way possible" (p. 86). The test must be specific (steps, instruments, numbers) so that the prospect mentally rehearses both the proof procedure and the reward. Memory-course ad shows the reader can run a self-administered 20-fact test in a single evening (p. 87).

### Stretch Out Your Benefits in Time (p. 87)
"Showing the product at work, not for just an hour or a day, but over a span of weeks and months. Here you extend your reader's vision further and further into time — showing him a continuous flow of benefits" (pp. 87–88). Sequence in chronological gates ("the very first month"... "within one or two short weeks"... "and then, the most remarkable part of all!") so the reader experiences a moving forward narrative rather than a single snapshot.

### Bring In an Audience (p. 89)
"At the end of this passage, other actors besides the reader are brought into the scene. Each one of them — each group of them — provides a fresh new perspective through which your reader can view the product" (p. 89). Three audience types: (1) celebrities via testimonial (mentioned but "too familiar to warrant an example here"), (2) average men and women via narration or case history (the famous "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano" cited as the canonical narration example, p. 89), and (3) groups of case histories used as social proof at scale (the reducing-ad sequence on pp. 89–90).

### Show Experts Approving (p. 90)
"There is nothing so astounding as the astonishment of experts. Here the elements of surprise, competition and discovery all combine to sharpen the image even more" (p. 90). The form: stage a contest in which experts gather expecting one outcome, then your product overturns it. The Nearly Wild rose ad has the experts shaking their heads in disbelief, which lets the copywriter put strong superlatives into a third-party expert mouth instead of into the brand's own (pp. 90–91).

### Compare, Contrast, Prove Superiority (p. 91)
"The competition can be carried into contrast. The disadvantages of the old product or service can be laid side by side with the advantages of the new — throwing these advantages into sharp relief" (p. 91). Two structural patterns: side-by-side price-anchor (Dollar Book Club's "$3.95... 99¢" rhythm, p. 91) and parallel before-after lists (Fuel Reclaimer: "polluted oil, polluted engine, polluted air" vs. "young-car power, young-car economy, young-car resistance to repair bills," p. 92).

### Picture the Black Side, Too (p. 92)
"There's no need to neglect the Heaven-Or-Hell approach... You irritate the wound, and then you apply the salve that heals it" (p. 92). Forms named: narration, testimonial, "pitch" technique of product ridicule, or straight description "in a perfectly parallel style, item by item" (p. 92). The spark-plug "FILTH... CORRODED — PITTED — SCARRED — AND WORN!" passage (p. 93) is the textbook irritate-the-wound move; the pivot to "AT LAST! A PLUG SO SMART THAT IT THINKS!" applies the salve.

### Show How Easy It Is To Get These Benefits (p. 94)
Apply the Intensification frame to every contact point — "price, availability, ease of use, durability, portability, replacement and maintenance, even unwrapping the carton it comes in" (p. 94). Tactical move: repeat the same friction-reducing phrase ("with just 30 seconds work") at the head of each benefit paragraph so that ease itself becomes a refrain.

### Use Metaphor, Analogy, Imagination (p. 94)
"There are infinite opportunities for the use of imagination to present those facts in more dramatic form, outside of the rigidly realistic approach" (p. 94). The Sherwin Cody English Course is given the longest treatment: a manual personified as a silent mentor whispering at your elbow ("That is wrong, it should be thus and so"), branded as the "100% Self-Correcting Device" (p. 95). The technique: take an inanimate product and give it a human role.

### Before You're Done, Summarize (p. 95)
"As long as each additional fresh perspective continues to build the dominant desire in your prospect's mind, use it. But if the additional perspective is not different or dramatic enough to renew your prospect's interest in your claims, then leave it out... perhaps the best way to measure the balance between the two — that critical turning point where reinforcement becomes mere repetition — is to re-read your ad, some days after you have first written it" (pp. 95–96). The two summary devices are the horizontal catalog (broaden applications) and the vertical catalog (deepen the same desire). Schwartz frames it with the famous shotgun image: "your ad has taken a single dominant desire, and developed that single desire to the point of absolute maximum intensity. This willingness to gamble on being precisely right... gives your ad the impact of a high-velocity rifle. But it also demands the accuracy of an expert marksman. Now — through this multi-appeal catalog listing — you have a last-minute chance to hedge" (p. 97).

### Put Your Guarantee to Work (p. 99)
"You can turn that guarantee into the climax of your ad — the last brief summary of your product's performances — reinforced at every step by the positive reassertion of that guarantee" (p. 99). Two formats demonstrated: numbered guarantees that pair each performance promise with its own money-back clause (Power Flash spark plugs, pp. 99–100) and guarantee-driven application stacks where each USE prompt resolves into "or every cent of your money back" (Magic English Pellets, p. 100).

## How to Apply Intensification Principles to the Campaign (p. 101)

Schwartz acknowledges that all chapter examples have come from mail order because mail order "compresses such a campaign into a single ad. It says all it can, everything it can think of, in this one ad" (p. 101) — making the Intensification devices visible in concentrated form. National advertising changes the rules: products live longer than two years, ads run far more frequently, and "national advertising, by its frequent insertions, soon loses its immediate novelty. The creative problem in national advertising thus changes from finding the theme for a particular advertisement to finding the theme for a series of advertisements. And the problem of Intensification shifts from building desire throughout the advertisement to building desire throughout the series" (p. 102 [paraphrased — book p. 102, OCR rendered page header as "WZ"]).

The two-fold creative problem: (1) compress the dominant image into a single statement or picture so powerful it sells the first time and so true to market that it keeps selling on repeat (p. 102); and (2) "present a series of variations or perspectives of that central image — each emerging from your dominant idea, but each so different from the rest that they impel your prospect to read through them, and so fresh that they make that dominant idea seem new again" (p. 102). Result: "Thus you have your Central Campaign Idea, and its continuous restatement in a series of fresh disguises" (p. 102).

Schwartz then walks four canonical campaigns:

- **Colgate Gardol (late 1950s)** — central idea openly stated and ritualistically repeated: "Gardol — the Invisible Shield that Protects Your Teeth" (p. 103). Variation lives only in the opening 10-second TV demo (a baseball thrown at the announcer, bouncing off the shield), with the closing line reciting verbatim: "Just as this invisible shield... protects me against this baseball, so the invisible shield in Colgate toothpaste protects your teeth against decay" (p. 103). Schwartz calls this "Rule and Example" — a precisely-repeated verbal image with constantly changing illustrations.

- **Toni** — fixed headline "WHICH TWIN HAS THE TONI — and which has the $15 permanent?" anchoring a continuously-changing stream of pretty-girl photos (p. 104).

- **Clairol** — "DOES SHE OR DOESN'T SHE? Hair-coloring so natural only her hairdresser knows for sure" (p. 104), re-invigorated by a series of glamorized everyday-life main illustrations.

- **Cadillac** — focal point is brand quality, "symbolized in every Cadillac advertisement in the identification background photography, in the typography, in the copy style, in the fashions and jewelry that are chosen to appear with the car... even the highly-stylized pen-and-ink drawings that are used when the campaign shifts to newspapers" (p. 104). The dominant image lives in the campaign as a whole, not in any single ad.

- **Volkswagen** — the ultimate "product as focal point" campaign. "The advertisements are reduced to severe simplicity. There is no background identification whatsoever. Different perspectives of the product are shown as close-up as possible (except in the 'Think Small' advertisement) against an absolutely bare background" (p. 105). Headlines listed: "THINK SMALL" (compactness as asset), "Lemon" (microscopic flaw disqualifying a unit), "Our new model" (engineering changes you can't see), "Why you should open the window before you close the door of a Volkswagen" (airtight cabin), "Our number one salesman" (the serviceman) (pp. 105–106). Each headline (a) hits the prospect's dominant desire (more value for car money), (b) expresses the campaign's common theme (this is an honest car), and (c) compels readership by setting up an apparent contradiction or unusual request that body copy must resolve (p. 106).

Schwartz closes with the "Creative Gamble" frame: in a VW-style campaign, the headline does not mention product, trademark, or performance — "the copywriter is taking the position that he can only sell adequately with the number of words allowed him by the body copy. Therefore he is willing to risk his headline in grasping the attention of his prospect and impelling him to read on, into the sales message itself, presented in convincing detail below. If he fails in his gamble, he has wasted his page. If he succeeds, he has doubled and tripled the effectiveness of his ad" (p. 106).

## Key insights (5–10)

- **Desire is raw material; intensification is the process.** "These desires, as they exist in the mind of your prospect today, are indistinct... blurs — hazy, ambiguous, not yet crystallized into words or images... they have only a fraction of their true potential power" (pp. 77–78). The copywriter's job is to crystallize them.
- **You cannot repeat — you can only reinforce.** "Once you have presented your basic fulfillment in a certain way, then you must vary your viewpoint in your second description, or not present it again. Otherwise you will lose your reader in the middle of your ad" (p. 79). Reinforcement = same promise, fresh viewpoint.
- **Sharper images = price disappears.** "The sharper you can draw your pictures — and the greater the number of them that you can legitimately present — the more your prospect will demand your product, and the less important will seem your price" (p. 78).
- **The copywriter is the script writer for the prospect's dreams.** "You are literally the script writer for your prospect's dreams. You are the chronicler of his future. Your job is to show him in minute detail all the tomorrows that your product makes possible for him" (p. 78).
- **Heaven-or-Hell creates two currents of motivation.** "Repulsion away from the former problem or inadequate product, and the attraction generated by your own product's contrasting solutions" (p. 92). Use both.
- **The catalog is a hedge for the rifle shot.** A single dominant desire developed to maximum intensity is the rifle; the closing catalog is the shotgun that hedges in case you picked the wrong dominant appeal (p. 97).
- **The guarantee is the climactic summary, not a footnote.** Every guarantee clause should re-state a benefit; the structure pairs claim → "or your money back" so the guarantee re-intensifies desire on the way out (pp. 99–100).
- **Re-read your ad days later to spot the line where reinforcement becomes repetition.** "Perhaps the best way to measure the balance between the two — that critical turning point where reinforcement becomes mere repetition — is to re-read your ad, some days after you have first written it" (p. 96).
- **Mail order forces all 13 devices into one ad; campaigns spread them across a series.** "Mail order as a rule contains the greatest number of these Intensification devices in a single ad" (p. 101); national campaigns shift Intensification from "throughout the advertisement to throughout the series" (p. 102).
- **The Creative Gamble: skip product/trademark in the headline if you trust your body copy.** VW-class campaigns risk the page on a headline that grasps attention via contradiction, then close in body copy. Fail = wasted page. Succeed = "doubled and tripled the effectiveness of his ad" (p. 106).

## Copy patterns / sentence templates

- **Sensory-stack opener** — "[ProductName] is so [adjective] that [sensory consequence with measurable number]." Schwartz: "so huge that its tail section alone stands six full inches from the table!" (p. 83).
- **Picture-to-yourself second-person hypothetical** — "Picture this to yourself! [Time anchor] you [reader action with your familiar object]. You've made only one simple change... But now, when you [trigger], a [outcome]." Based on p. 85: "Picture this to yourself! Next weekend you go down to your car — the same tired car that you've been driving for years."
- **Test-the-claim stepwise script** — "When you get your set of [Product], here is all you do. If you have [Variant A], make a note of [baseline metric]. If you have [Variant B], note [baseline metric]. Next... [install]. Now, [re-measure]. In other words, no matter what you [own/do], here is absolute proof that [transformation]." Based on p. 86 SA-FIRE Injectors passage.
- **Stretched-time stages** — "First, [Stage 1 outcome] [Time 1]. But this is just the beginning! Within [Time 2] — without your even [effort] — [Mechanism] AUTOMATICALLY begins the second stage of its work!... And then, the most remarkable part of all!" (pp. 87–88).
- **Black-side / heaven-or-hell pivot** — "[Look at the old/inadequate thing]. What you'll see is [BLACK, FILTHY DRAMATIC NOUN] — [destructive consequences]. Now look at the new [Product] — [brilliant headline]. Here is a [Product] that not only [old benefit] — but [breakthrough]" (pp. 92–93).
- **Ease refrain** — "With just [trivial-time] work, you will [Benefit 1]... With just [trivial-time] work, you will [Benefit 2]... And, with just [trivial-time] work, you will [Benefit 3]." (p. 94).
- **Personified-product metaphor** — "Suppose [Product] himself were standing forever at your elbow. Every time you [Pain Action], suppose you could hear him whisper, '[Correction line].'" Based on Sherwin Cody, p. 95.
- **Numbered guarantee climax** — "Guaranteed For [Period]! Yes! You try [Product] for [Period] entirely at our risk!  1. [Product] must give you [specific metric] — or your full purchase price back! 2. [Product] must give you [specific metric] — or your full purchase price back! 3. As an extra added assurance — [Product] must continue to [outcome] FOR [Period] — or we will send you a brand-new [unit] ABSOLUTELY FREE!" (pp. 99–100).
- **Guarantee-driven application stack** — "USE THEM TO CREATE [Outcome 1]!... USE THEM TO CREATE [Outcome 2]!... YES! USE THEM IN YOUR ENTIRE [Domain]!... if your [Domain] isn't the showplace of your neighborhood at the end of one short month — then simply return the empty package — for every cent of your money back!" (p. 100).
- **Rule-and-Example campaign anchor** — Fixed verbal rule ("Gardol — the Invisible Shield that Protects Your Teeth") + rotating opening demonstrations that change every week (p. 103).
- **Creative-Gamble headline** — Drop product, trademark, performance from the headline; lead with contradiction/unusual request that forces body-copy reading. e.g., "Lemon" (about the very car you'd want to buy) (pp. 105–106).

## Common mistakes / warnings

- **Repeating instead of reinforcing.** "Once you have presented your basic fulfillment in a certain way, then you must vary your viewpoint in your second description, or not present it again. Otherwise you will lose your reader in the middle of your ad" (p. 79). Fix: change perspective every section, never restate the same image with different words.
- **Adding perspectives that are not different or dramatic enough.** "If the additional perspective is not different or dramatic enough to renew your prospect's interest in your claims, then leave it out" (p. 96). Fix: re-read days later; if the new angle reads as repetition, cut it.
- **Picking the wrong dominant desire.** The rifle metaphor: a single dominant desire developed to maximum intensity "demands the accuracy of an expert marksman" (p. 97). Mistake: assuming you picked correctly. Fix: hedge with a closing catalog (shotgun) so secondary appeals can still close the sale.
- **Treating the guarantee as a footnote instead of as the climax.** Schwartz makes the guarantee carry a re-intensification of every key benefit at the close (p. 99). Fix: number each performance promise inside the guarantee.
- **In national campaigns, letting frequent insertions burn out the central image.** "National advertising, by its frequent insertions, soon loses its immediate novelty" (p. 102). Fix: lock the dominant image; vary everything around it ("novelty within continuity," p. 106).
- **Unwilling to take the Creative Gamble.** Mistake: stuffing the headline with product, trademark, and performance "just to be safe." Schwartz: the gamble is what doubles or triples ad effectiveness — but only if body copy is convincing enough to land (p. 106). Fix: only gamble when the body copy can carry the close.

## Ethical / honesty notes

Schwartz is not lecturing on truth in this chapter the way some writers do, but a few explicit guardrails are present:

- "The greater the number of [pictures] that you can **legitimately** present — the more your prospect will demand your product" (p. 78, emphasis added). Legitimacy of the picture is a precondition.
- The shotgun catalog "demands the accuracy of an expert marksman" (p. 97) — i.e., the dominant promise must actually fit the prospect's real desire; intensifying a wrong desire wastes the rifle shot.
- Guarantees are written as numbered, performance-bound, refundable claims (e.g., "9 miles more per gallon instantly — or your full purchase price back!" p. 99). The implicit contract: every guaranteed metric must be one the product can deliver, because each is independently refundable.
- The chapter does not contain an explicit warning against fabricating expert testimony, fake case histories, or unbacked black-side claims; those guardrails sit elsewhere in the book. For Chapter 7, Schwartz's only guardrail-language is "legitimately."
