# Finding Million-Dollar Ideas

Deep reference for Ch 3 of *Million Dollar Weekend* (Noah Kagan with Tahl Raz, Portfolio / Penguin Random House, 2024). All citations point to PDF page numbers within that chapter (roughly PDF pp. 38–57).

> "Customers don't care about your ideas; they care about whether you can solve their problems. And you should not build your idea into a business if you don't know with 100 percent certainty that it's a solution your customers will pay for." (Ch 3, PDF p. 39)

This chapter is the first step in the Million Dollar Weekend process. The deliverable is concrete: ten-plus ideas generated, narrowed to three, with the **Three Ws** (Who / What / Where) filled in for each.

---

## 1. The Customer First Approach

### Full definition

Customer First means: **before the product, before the service, before even the idea, you identify a customer and a problem you can solve for them.** Kagan attributes the principle to Steve Jobs ("You have to start with the customer experience and work backwards") and to Bezos's first Amazon Leadership Principle, Customer Obsession: "Leaders start with the customer and work backwards." (Ch 3, PDF p. 39)

"That's why, when it comes to generating business ideas, customers come first. *Before the product or service.* Even before the idea. **To build a business, you need someone to sell to.**" (Ch 3, PDF p. 39)

When people email Kagan asking "What do you think of this business idea?", his auto-reply is: **"Have you asked what the customer thinks?"** (Ch 3, PDF p. 39)

### Customer First vs Founder First

Kagan names the opposite pattern **Founder First**: entrepreneurs focus on their own experience ("Yay, I get to build something!") instead of on whether anyone will pay for it. (Ch 3, PDF p. 45)

| | Founder First (old school) | Customer First (new school) |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Business model planning and obsessing about the product | A conversation with customers, iterating before you make or spend a thing |
| First action | Build / refine in a vacuum | Talk to potential buyers |
| MVP problem | "Good luck iterating on your MVP without customers" | The customer *is* the MVP target — find them first |

Kagan's worked contrast — the dog-walking-app example — makes this concrete. (Ch 3, PDF pp. 45–46)

**Founder First version (8 steps to nowhere):**
1. Spend hours at home thinking about the app (and clever names for it).
2. Spend $100 hiring their cousin to draw a cool logo.
3. Set up an LLC.
4. Watch YouTube videos about apps and programming and business. And dogs.
5. Consider signing up for a developer boot camp and quickly realize coding is hard.
6. Buy the domain name for the snazzy website they're going to build.
7. Look into hiring a developer on Upwork, and quickly realize it's prohibitively expensive.
8. Give up. Again.

**Customer First version (3 steps to a paying customer):**
1. Call or text three people right now who have dogs and ask them to pay you to walk their dog.
2. Turns out none of them have problems walking their dog — their real problem is finding dog *sitters* when they travel.
3. Ask for their next travel dates and have them pay a deposit. They pay: jackpot.

"Pretty quickly you found out the opportunity was dog-sitting, not dog-walking. And now you have real customers paying you to solve a real problem, with real revenue flowing in, before writing a line of code or spending any money on freelancers." (Ch 3, PDF p. 46)

### The Three Ws

Working backwards from a customer forces you to nail three things at the start: (Ch 3, PDF p. 39)

- **Who** you are selling to
- **What** problem you're solving
- **Where** they are

Kagan's own Imgur/AppSumo deal worked because all three were locked in before he built anything: (Ch 3, PDF p. 41)

- **Who:** People on Reddit.
- **What:** Imgur Pro at a discount.
- **Where:** Reddit (the ad placement).

## 2. The BetArcade cautionary tale (and how it became Gambit, a $15M business)

Kagan opens the chapter with a confession. He and his partners spotted a trend — fantasy sports and sports betting were getting huge — and decided to build **BetArcade**, a fantasy sports betting site, on top of the Facebook game audience they already had. (Ch 3, PDF p. 38)

**The damage:**
- ~6 months building
- ~$100,000 paid to programmers
- ~$10,000 paid to lawyers to confirm online gambling was legal
- A site that "was absolutely beautiful. Amazing graphics. Worked great."
- "And NO ONE CAME. Crickets." (Ch 3, PDF p. 38)

He'd done the same thing once before with Disney: a verbal "of course we'd want something like that" pushed him to spend six months and $100,000 building an expanded payment product for their social gaming. Disney looked at it, said they didn't need it right away, and ghosted him. (Ch 3, PDF p. 39)

### The pivot to Gambit

Broke and desperate, Kagan asked himself: **"What was our biggest problem and did others share it? Was there a solution we were capable of creating quickly?"** (Ch 3, PDF p. 38)

The problem was right in front of him. OfferPal, the payments provider for their successful Facebook games, was charging 50% per transaction and ignoring every upgrade suggestion. He and his partner Andrew realized they could offer a better margin themselves.

So he *asked*. He called a few friends who owned Facebook games to see if they'd switch to a different payment provider with lower commissions. "Turns out it was an easy sell." (Ch 3, PDF p. 38)

**The result:**
- Beta built **in a weekend**
- Service (Gambit) **running within two weeks**
- Friends made 20% more money instantly via lower commissions
- **$15 million in top-line revenue in year one** (Ch 3, PDF p. 38)

The lesson Kagan bolds: **"It is deadly to build a business without first verifying that there are paying customers."** (Ch 3, PDF p. 38)

## 3. The Zone of Influence

> "When novice entrepreneurs search for opportunities, they too often look beyond their **Zone of Influence**. They think the action is happening somewhere else, in some other location or industry. But seasoned entrepreneurs almost always find and create opportunities within the context of who they are, what they know, and especially who they know." (Ch 3, PDF pp. 43–44)

### What it is

Your Zone of Influence is the **set of actual people in your orbit** — people with names, tribes you belong to or are interested in, communities most of which are already self-organized online, people you know how to reach *today*. (Ch 3, PDF p. 44)

Kagan's examples of how the world's biggest companies started inside it: (Ch 3, PDF p. 44)

- **Mark Zuckerberg started Facebook in a weekend by emailing friends to use it.** Version 1 did well, validating it.
- **Microsoft started with Bill Gates building software for a guy in Albuquerque. He had a CUSTOMER FIRST.**

### How to map it

The Challenge in the chapter is concrete: write out your **top three groups to target**. The prompt: **"Who do you have easy access to that you'd be EXCITED to help?"** Examples Kagan offers: neighbors, colleagues, religious friends, golf buddies, cooking friends. (Ch 3, PDF p. 44)

Later in the chapter he expands this list with examples of self-organized tribes you may already belong to: (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)

- The 150 followers you have on TikTok
- The 200 in your local Taco Aficionados group
- The 300 in the WhatsApp group for your mountain biking club
- The 143,000 in the r/mountainbiking subreddit

"Your job as a problem seeker is to go to a community of yours." (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)

### Why staying inside it beats going outside

- You can reach them today, for free — no ad budget, no cold-traffic conversion problem.
- You already understand their language, jokes, and frustrations.
- "The better you understand your target group, the better you can speak to them. The more specifically you can speak to their problems, the better and easier you can sell (or test products)." (Ch 3, PDF p. 44)
- Communication beats research: "Business creation should always be a conversation!" (Ch 3, PDF p. 45)

## 4. The Four Idea Generators

Before listing them, Kagan defines what the process is **NOT**: (Ch 3, PDF p. 48)

- Getting on TikTok or YouTube and mindlessly copying whatever the influencers say is working
- Getting struck with the perfect vision for a genius new product
- Meditating, following your passion, and brainstorming
- Following any other woo-woo method that promises inspiration in a box

What it actually is: (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)

1. What's the **most painful (aka valuable) problem** you can solve for people…
2. That you also have **passion for and/or unique expertise in**…
3. For the **largest niche possible that you belong to and understand**.

Then use the four generators to produce **at least ten** potentially profitable ideas.

### Generator 1: Solve Your Own Problems

> "When in doubt, solve your own problems. If you are willing to pay for a solution, it's likely others are, too. And at least you'll have one happy customer—yourself." (Ch 3, PDF p. 40, PRO TIP)

Anchor case: **Shane Heath / Mud\Wtr.** Shane loved coffee but hated the anxiety. Everyone around him kept saying "I want to cut down on coffee, too" — but nobody was, because there was nothing better to drink. He went to India, discovered masala chai, loved it, and invented Mud/Wtr — a masala chai coffee replacement with added health ingredients. People asked what he was drinking; he made some for friends; they got hooked. **"That tiny problem of his is now making over $60 million per year!"** (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)

#### Four prompts when you're stuck (Ch 3, PDF p. 50)

1. What is **one thing this morning that irritated me**?
2. What is **one thing on my to-do list that's been there over a week**?
3. What is **one thing that I regularly fail to do well**?
4. What is **one thing I wanted to buy recently only to find out that no one made it**?

Kagan keeps a notebook nearby at all times to capture these. He shares three of his own as worked examples: (Ch 3, PDF p. 50)

- **FIND ME AN X.** He spent ~a year buying a car. Idea: pick a vertical where people give you their requirements and you find whatever they're looking for.
- **CHEAP AND REMOTE INTERIOR DESIGN FOR YOUNG SINGLE GUYS.** Interior designers are for rich people. Send a photo + preferences, get a Pinterest page of suggestions.
- **FRIEND/ACTIVITY MATCHMAKER.** He paddleboards and goes to the gym but his friends aren't always available. Meetup is for groups; this would be Meetup at the individual level.

Other businesses Kagan personally built from his own frustrations: AppSumo (couldn't find great discounts on business apps), SumoMe (needed a tool to grow our email list), TidyCal (tired of monthly subscriptions on competitors), CommunityNext (couldn't find a good community to talk about social networks in the Bay Area), Gambit (no good in-game payment service), and a home gym product (needed weights during COVID). "Solving my own problems built a business that generates literally $65 million per year." (Ch 3, PDF p. 48)

### Generator 2: Bestsellers Are Your Best Friends

> "What products are *already* selling a crap-ton? iPads, iPhones, etc. Basically, any product you'd find on Amazon's Bestseller list would work here." (Ch 3, PDF p. 51)

Two moves: (Ch 3, PDF p. 51)

- **Accessorize** the product (e.g., stickers for an iPhone)
- **Teach** people how to use it (e.g., teaching someone how to use an iPhone)

The logic: "It's easier to sell to a large group of people who've already spent money on a product or service." (Ch 3, PDF p. 51)

**Worked ideas Kagan offers:**
1. Customizing Nike shoes
2. Video game tutorial for an Xbox game
3. Teaching computer novices how to use a MacBook

**Challenge:** write down two accessorizing ideas in your MDW Journal. (Ch 3, PDF p. 51)

PS from Kagan, important for this stage: "DO NOT EDIT YOURSELF. Do not think, *But how could that work?* Just write as many down as you can. We'll whittle them down later." (Ch 3, PDF p. 51)

### Generator 3: Marketplaces

> "One of my favorite ways to find ideas is by studying the marketplaces where people are TRYING to spend money. Your potential customers are everywhere already asking in public for solutions—on message boards, in Facebook posts, in tweets, in church groups, on and on!" (Ch 3, PDF p. 51)

Where to look: **Craigslist, Etsy, Facebook Marketplace, eBay.** (Ch 3, PDF p. 52)

How to read each one:

- **Craigslist gigs:** look for frequent requests from people actively trying to give money in exchange for particular services.
- **eBay completed listings:** see how well certain products are actually selling, what they sold for, and what % of listings receive bids. This separates real demand from wishful pricing.
- **Etsy / Facebook Marketplace:** millions of people each day wanting to pay to have their problems solved.

Worked examples from the Craigslist screenshot Kagan annotates: (Ch 3, PDF p. 52)

- *Assistant Babysitter, $20/hr* — "You don't even have to take care of the kids! Prep snacks, plan activities, and collect your cash."
- *Wash, Dry, Fold Laundry Pro, $9/load* — "One person's dirty socks is another person's money-maker."
- *I need a wall built in my house* — "Hone-in on niche gigs that no one else thinks about. Hire other people to do the work, then split the profits."

**Challenge:** visit Etsy, Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, or eBay and write at least one product/service idea in your MDW Journal. (Ch 3, PDF p. 52)

### Generator 4: Search Engine Queries

> "It's much easier to sell something when people ALREADY want it. There are 3 billion Google searches every day, giving you a direct line to customers' thoughts and needs." (Ch 3, PDF p. 53)

The method: **work backwards from a problem people want solved (a query) toward a solution they may be willing to pay for.**

Tools and tactics:

- **Google autocomplete.** Type a partial query and read what Google suggests. Kagan's example is "how do i train my cat to" → use the toilet / walk on a leash / stay off the counter / stop biting / be a therapy cat / sit / not bite / sleep with me / be an outdoor cat / stay indoors. (Ch 3, PDF p. 54)
- **AnswerThePublic.com** — finds the most-Googled questions around any keyword.
- **Reddit r/SomebodyMakeThis** — "one of the largest message boards online… where people are ACTIVELY offering up ideas." Pick the first two that interest you. (Ch 3, PDF p. 53)

Sample queries to try: "How do I train my cat to use a toilet?" / "Best places to travel with a family?" / "Where can I rent a bike in Barcelona?" (Ch 3, PDF p. 53)

**Challenge:** use search engine questions and Reddit forums to find two more ideas. (Ch 3, PDF p. 54)

## 5. Painkiller vs vitamin filter

Kagan first introduces this filter in the AppSumo origin story: he was done with commodity businesses where his product was a *nice-to-have* (a vitamin). He wanted to be a *must-have* (a painkiller). "And getting more customers is the most essential business need." (Ch 3, PDF p. 39)

He brings the filter back as an evaluation question for any search-engine-derived idea: **"Is the potential solution a vitamin (a nice-to-have) or a painkiller (a must-have)?"** (Ch 3, PDF p. 53)

The point: rank your ten-plus ideas by *pain intensity*. Painkillers sell themselves. Vitamins require you to convince people they should care.

## 6. The largest niche you belong to AND understand

The three-part filter for what to chase: (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)

1. Most **painful** (aka valuable) problem you can solve…
2. That you also have **passion** for and/or **unique expertise** in…
3. For the **largest niche possible that you belong to and understand**.

All three must be true. Passion without expertise is a hobby. Expertise without a niche you belong to is consulting at best. A niche you don't actually live in is a guess. The Zone of Influence is what makes #3 enforceable — you must be a member of the tribe, not a tourist.

Why "largest possible" matters: a niche of 50 friends caps your revenue. The 143,000-person r/mountainbiking subreddit doesn't. Kagan wants the biggest tribe where you still genuinely belong. (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)

## 7. Become a Problem Seeker

> "The best entrepreneurs are the most dissatisfied. They're always thinking of how things can be better. Your frustrations—and the frustrations of others—are your business opportunities." (Ch 3, PDF p. 46)

Kagan models this by listing things bothering him *today*: (Ch 3, PDF pp. 46–47)

- What to make for breakfast that's quick, healthy, and full of caffeine
- How to find a reliable house cleaner
- Where to go to dinner with my partner
- How to find my next therapist
- What kind of investment to make with some extra cash I received

"And these are just the problems I've encountered today. I could go on and on… and that's the point!" (Ch 3, PDF p. 47)

The crucial reframe Kagan bolds: **"The crucial first step toward entrepreneurship is to study your own unhappiness and to think of solutions (aka business opportunities) for you to sell."** (Ch 3, PDF p. 47)

Cultivate this until it becomes automatic: "When you intentionally practice problem-spotting, eventually it becomes something your mind just does automatically. It's become a game to me—a profitable one." (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)

## 8. The 10+ → 3 narrowing rubric

After running the four generators, you should have **ten ideas or more** (you can also count the business idea you asked a friend for in Ch 1). (Ch 3, PDF p. 54)

The narrowing rules: (Ch 3, PDF pp. 55–56)

1. **There's never a "perfect" idea.** AppSumo started as bundles of web tools and three years later evolved to individual deals, custom software, and courses. "Your idea will evolve over time, just as all businesses do."
2. **Eliminate the ones you're not excited about.** Excitement is fuel; you'll need it for the validation phase.
3. **If the top three are screaming "Me! Me! Me!" your work is done.**
4. **If you can't decide, choose the ones you believe will be easiest to implement — and that you (and ideally other customers) would be thrilled to spend money on.**

The chapter explicitly punctures perfectionism: "Don't worry if you think your ideas suck or are too hard. The real value is learning to create, assess, and validate ideas." (Ch 3, PDF p. 56)

## 9. Worked outreach examples (verbatim scripts)

### 9a. Imgur cold pitch (Noah → Alan Schaaf)

After spotting that every other Reddit front-page post was an Imgur-hosted image, Kagan cold-emailed Imgur's creator Alan Schaaf (a college student in Ohio at the time) and asked to resell pro subscriptions at a discount. (Ch 3, PDF pp. 40–41)

```
SUBJECT: Promoting Imgur on Reddit
TO: Alan Schaff
FROM: Noah Kagan

Hey Alan
Huge fan of Imgur and love using your product all the time.
We are launching a deal site and wanted to promote your Imgur Pro.
Think we can sell 200+ of it for you at no cost or work for you.
You free Friday to chat on AIM at 5pm PT?
Noah Kagan
```

Why it worked, per Kagan: "He jumped at the idea because, well, I promised him money at no cost!" (Ch 3, PDF p. 41)

Structural elements to copy:
- Genuine fan opener (one line, specific to their product)
- Concrete number ("200+")
- Zero risk on their side ("at no cost or work for you")
- One specific time + channel ask (Friday 5pm PT on AIM)

### 9b. Warm-intro coffee email (Noah → Chris Slowe, with Chris Smoak as connector)

Then he cold-emailed Reddit founding engineer Chris Slowe to ask for **free advertising** — not advice, not a discount. (Ch 3, PDF p. 41)

```
SUBJECT: Hey Chris—friends with Chris Smoak
TO: Chris Slowe
FROM: Noah Kagan

Hey Chris
Talked with Chris Smoak and he says hi. I LOVE what you've built with Reddit. Huge user!
Wanted to treat you to a breakfast at Pork Store Cafe to give you some suggestions on the site and run by a cool promotion I'm working on with Imgur.
Be epic,
Noah Kagan
```

Structural elements: warm-intro name in the subject line, sincere compliment with proof of use ("Huge user!"), gives value first (suggestions, breakfast), only then mentions the promotion. The reply: "Why not? Our users love Imgur. They'll be thrilled to get a discount." (Ch 3, PDF p. 42)

The economics of the resulting test: $12/hour Pakistani developer added a PayPal button (4 hours). Kagan did the rest himself. **Total time to build AppSumo.com: 48 hours. Total cost: $50.** First sale arrived within minutes of the Reddit ad going live. (Ch 3, PDF p. 42)

> "The first dollar is always the sweetest. It's momentum. It's possibility. It's fear getting its ass kicked." (Ch 3, PDF p. 42)

### 9c. Boris Korsunsky preselling a private chef service

Boris validated a private chef service via email — without knowing how to cook. (Ch 3, PDF pp. 47–48)

```
SUBJECT: Helping you, help me with food!

Hey Friends
One thing I realized is that I'm busy all the time and I don't have time to cook a quality meal. :(
I wanted to invite a few close friends to test a business idea with me.
Consider yourself the lucky chosen few. :)
Convenient and home-cooked meals.
On February 9, for $20, there will be a personal chef making us food and delivering it to you conveniently and deliciously!
If this is something you're seriously interested in, please paypal $20.
Open to all and any feedback.
Cheers,
Boris
P.S. Please let me know if you have any dietary restrictions, or any particular preferences. I promise the dinner will be delish!!!
```

**Result: five-plus paid sales from that one email.** (Ch 3, PDF p. 48)

What to copy:
- Frames the offer as solving the *sender's own* problem, which makes the audience feel they're solving it together rather than being sold to
- Specific date, specific price, specific PayPal ask (commitment is in dollars, not "interested?")
- "Lucky chosen few" + small list → social proof + scarcity baked in
- P.S. opens a conversation (preferences, restrictions)

## 10. Cases referenced in this chapter

### Anahita / Barkery (Ch 3, PDF p. 44)
Anahita loved her dogs and wanted healthier snacks for them. She took her homemade organic dog treats to her local dog park and sold out every time. A year later she has a store called **the Barkery, a dog bakery**. Pure Zone of Influence: she belonged to the local dog-park tribe before she had a product.

### Shane Heath / Mud\Wtr (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)
Solved his own coffee-anxiety problem with masala chai he discovered in India. Friends asked what he was drinking; the casual sharing became the launch. Now **over $60 million per year**.

### Jennifer Jones / cookie business (Ch 3, PDF p. 43)
A Dallas elementary school teacher in Kagan's Monthly1K class. Everyone liked her cookies, so she posted on Facebook offering cookie baskets for the holidays. Did anyone want some? "Turns out they *really* did, and she now has a $1,000 per month cookie business." (Posted on her page as "Cookie Mama & Co.": "Trying something new this week! Who wants to try some M&M oatmeal cookies?")

The replicable template Kagan extracts: "Anybody who has a skill in something like that—homemade cakes, pickles, candles, you name it—can send an email to friends, family, coworkers, and church community members asking if they'd be interested in buying whatever it is. Include a PayPal link. Then fill all the orders you receive. Voilà, a zero-risk business venture. No hiring, no website, no cooking school, no commercial kitchen." (Ch 3, PDF p. 43)

### Bill Gates / Microsoft (Ch 3, PDF p. 44)
"Microsoft started with Bill Gates building software for a guy in Albuquerque. He had a CUSTOMER FIRST." Kagan invokes this alongside the Zuckerberg-emailing-friends-from-the-Facebook-weekend story to show that the world's largest companies followed Zone of Influence + Customer First — even if their official origin myths skip that part.
