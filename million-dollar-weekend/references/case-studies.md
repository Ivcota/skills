# Case Studies — Million Dollar Weekend

Every case study Noah Kagan presents in *Million Dollar Weekend: The Surprisingly Simple Way to Launch a 7-Figure Business in 48 Hours* (Noah Kagan with Tahl Raz, Portfolio / Penguin Random House LLC, 2024). Grouped by **context** so a reader can pick the situation closest to theirs and find matching examples.

Citation convention: `(Ch <n>, PDF p. <n>)` — PDF page numbers refer to the source PDF in `sources.md`. Only figures explicitly stated in the book are included; where Kagan does not give a dollar number or outcome, none is invented.

## Coaching / Consulting

### Daniel Reifenberger — Apple store → in-home tech tutoring
Reifenberger taught baby boomers and "little old ladies" to use computers at a local Apple store; customers kept asking, "Can I just take you home with me?" — he thought it was a pass at first until he realized they wanted in-home tech training. To validate, he asked family and friends for referrals and "had three paying customers within a week." His only equipment was a phone and personal email address; he "made a profit with his first client doing the work manually and still hasn't spent a dime on his business outside of mileage." "Today, his technology consulting business is doing $20,000 a MONTH." (Ch 5, PDF p. 75)
The earlier "Start Here" intro cites him at "$250,000-a-year" tutoring business. (Start Here, PDF p. 6)
*Illustrates:* Customer First Approach, Dream Ten preselling, validation as a conversation (Listen → Options → Transition).

### Michael Osborn — Real estate consulting
Kagan cites Osborn in the "Start Here" intro as a student who "used the three steps to turn his interest in real estate into an $83,000-a-month consulting business." (Start Here, PDF p. 6)
*Illustrates:* Million Dollar Weekend process applied to an existing interest.

### Boris Korsunsky — Private chef (who can't cook)
"Look at this email my friend Boris Korsunsky sent when he was validating a private chef service (he doesn't even know how to cook!)." Boris emailed friends offering a personal-chef dinner on February 9 for $20 with PayPal up-front: "Boris got five plus sales from this email, and an opportunity was born!" (Ch 3, PDF p. 47)
*Illustrates:* Problem-seeker mindset, asking for money via email, no infrastructure required.

## Physical product / E-commerce

### Sumo Jerky — Kagan's 24-hour challenge
Kagan accepted a challenge from his Monthly1K students to make $1,000 in profit in 24 hours with a business of their choosing — they chose jerky. He pivoted from one-off bags to 3-/6-/12-month office subscriptions after running the One-Minute Business Model in the middle of the night. Final result: "$4,040 in total revenue" and "$1,135 in profit!!!" The business was "sold for $120,000 two years later. (If we had kept going, it could have hit seven figures, but I wanted to move on and keep experimenting.)" (Ch 4, PDF p. 64; Ch 5, PDF p. 73)
*Illustrates:* One-Minute Business Model, Revenue Dials, 48-Hour Money Challenge, direct preselling via cold email.

### Justin Mares / Kettle & Fire — Bone broth
As an example of doing a landing page right, Kagan's "former intern Justin Mares" validated what became Kettle & Fire with a $12 domain (bonebroths.com), an Unbounce page, a $5 Fiverr logo, and ~$50 of Bing ads. "Over the course of the two-week test, he netted almost $500 in revenue. And now Kettle & Fire is a $100 million company — selling broth?!?!" (Ch 5, PDF p. 83)
*Illustrates:* Landing-page validation method, ugly-page-with-PayPal-checkout pattern.

### Beardbrand.com — Beard oil market
Used as the worked example for the One-Minute Business Model (beard oil at $50, market ~2.5M people on Facebook, ~$12.50 profit/unit). "I know for a fact that it is — my good friend who founded Beardbrand.com has a multimillion-dollar company doing the above." (Ch 4, PDF p. 63)
*Illustrates:* Million-dollar opportunity math; passing the size × price test.

### Daniel Bliss / Belay Shades — Climbing belay glasses
Hobbyist Canadian climber, postal worker, winner of an AppSumo getaway. Goal: $4,000/month so he could quit his postal-worker job and rock-climb in Thailand. After Kagan worked backward from the goal (166 glasses/mo, ~5–6/day), Daniel ran six marketing experiments over thirty days: friends + referrals (expected 30 / actual 9), Vancouver Rock Climbing Group (20/11), wholesale (50/217), eBay (25/0), giveaways (25/0), Facebook ads (16/0) — totaling 237 sales vs. a 166 goal, with wholesaling driving "90 percent of his sales" after one inbound from Sierra Trading Post placed a $4,200 order. (Ch 8, PDF pp. 117–123)
In the closing "Start Again," Bliss writes that he became "the main distributor for a European climbing brand in America" and "over the course of the next decade, I closed the business, having achieved something approaching a million in sales." (Start Again, PDF p. 145)
*Illustrates:* Experiment-Based Marketing, working backward from goal, ruthless double-down/kill.

### Anahita / The Barkery — Organic dog treats
"Anahita loves her dogs and wanted healthier snacks for them. She started taking her homemade organic dog treats to her local dog park. She would sell out every time. A year later she now has a store called the Barkery, a dog bakery." (Ch 3, PDF p. 46)
*Illustrates:* Zone of Influence, selling in person before building infrastructure.

### Jennifer Jones — Cookies (chocolate chip / M&M oatmeal)
In "Start Here," Kagan introduces her: "Or Jennifer Jones, who launched a $20,000-a-year side-hustle cookie business (chocolate chip for me!)." (Start Here, PDF p. 6)
Later as a Customer First Approach example: "Jennifer Jones, a Dallas elementary school teacher who was part of my Monthly1K class. Everyone liked her cookies. So she posted on Facebook saying she's making cookie baskets for the holidays. Did anyone want some? Turns out they really did, and she now has a $1,000 per month cookie business." (Ch 3, PDF p. 44)
*Illustrates:* Customer First Approach, zero-risk validation via Facebook post + PayPal link.

### Mondo taco posters → Taco Shirts (failure → pivot)
Kagan pitched friends $25 limited-edition taco posters inspired by Mondo. Every friend declined ("Uh, so you want me to buy a print for $25 of a . . . taco?"). Using the four-question rejection script, the one thing that kept coming up was his taco-print shirt. He validated again by text, then "posted a picture of me in the shirt on Facebook and posted a price of $25 per shirt. After I got fifteen orders via PayPal — no e-commerce, no website; people just sent me the money — I closed off sales and started looking for a manufacturer. And the Taco Shirt was born." (Ch 5, PDF pp. 79–80)
*Illustrates:* Turning rejection into a pivot via the four-question script.

### Nick Bare / BPN Supplements — Handwritten thank-you notes
"When Nick Bare was growing BPN Supplements from $1K – $100K per month, he wrote every US customer a hand-written thank you note. While he was in KOREA." He was deployed overseas, "got up at four a.m. to do his side hustle, and made it a point to personally message every one of his customers. This was the foundation that helped him build his supplement business into a seven-figure company today!" (Ch 8, PDF p. 130)
*Illustrates:* Delight your first 100 customers; non-scalable acts as growth foundation.

### Shane Heath / Mud\Wtr — Coffee replacement
"Shane Heath, a dude who loved coffee, but hated that it made him anxious and jittery . . . went to India and discovered masala chai. . . . So Shane invented Mud/Wtr. . . . That tiny problem of his is now making over $60 million per year!" (Ch 3, PDF p. 49)
*Illustrates:* Solve your own problem; problem-seeker mindset.

## SaaS / Software

### AppSumo — Kagan's $65M/year company built in a weekend
Kagan built v1 of AppSumo.com from a basement in 48 hours, paying a $12/hr Pakistani developer for ~4 hours of PayPal-button work. "Total time to build AppSumo.com: 48 hours. Total cost to build AppSumo.com: $50." He cold-emailed Imgur creator Alan Schaaf to promote Imgur Pro at a discount ("Think we can sell 200+ of it for you at no cost or work for you") and Reddit founding engineer Chris Slowe over breakfast for free ads. "My ads went up on Reddit promoting the website I'd just built, and holy crap, my first sale arrived within minutes. . . . Before I knew it, I'd sold the 200 licenses I set as my goal. Who could have known this would lead to a company doing over $65 million just ten years later?" (Ch 3, PDF pp. 41–43)
In the email chapter: "My company AppSumo generates $65 million a year in total transactions. And you know what? Nearly 50 percent of that comes from email. This percentage has been consistent for more than ten years." (Ch 7, PDF p. 90)
AppSumo's first $10,000 day came from one Neville-written email about Kernest fonts (Garamond "boner" subject line): "We made $9,563 in profit in twenty-four hours! By putting personality into the email, we made nearly a hundred times more money!" (Ch 7, PDF pp. 88–89)
Also a pivot example: "AppSumo started as bundle software for Silicon Valley startups but moved to selling individual deals to marketing agencies." (Ch 4, PDF p. 67)
*Illustrates:* 48-hour validation, Customer First Approach, Zero to $1, power of email + personality in copy.

### Mint.com — 1M users in 6 months → $170M Intuit exit
After founder Aaron Patzer told Kagan, "Sorry, Noah, you're not good enough yet to market my company," Kagan came back with a plan and offered: "I'll get you 100,000 users in six months before you've even launched the product. . . . If I don't hit my target, you don't have to pay me." He hit "100,000 registered users before the site even launched and 1 million users six months later — which got me a full-time offer: 1 percent of the company and a $100,000 job." "Mint's product was so good that less than two years after it started, Intuit bought it for $170 million." (Ch 1, PDF p. 18; Ch 8, PDF p. 109)
*Illustrates:* Experiment-Based Marketing; outcome-based pitching; targeted bloggers + best content.

### Sumo.com — 1B impressions in 12 months
"Sumo.com reached 1 billion impressions in twelve months." (Ch 8, PDF p. 109)
*Illustrates:* Reuse the Mint marketing plan across new businesses.

### SendFox.com — 10K customers in 6 months
"SendFox.com got 10,000 customers in six months." (Ch 8, PDF p. 109)
*Illustrates:* Repeatability of the growth playbook.

### Gambit — $15M in two years
"I spent my mornings, lunch breaks, nights, and weekends creating Kickflip, a company that developed apps for Facebook, which then morphed into Gambit, a payment system for social games. In less than two years, Gambit was generating more than $15 million in revenue." (Ch 1, PDF p. 19)
Origin: "In a weekend, we put together a beta version of the site, and within two weeks after that we had the service called Gambit running. . . . In the first year, we ended up making more than $15 million in top-line revenue." (Ch 3, PDF p. 36)
*Illustrates:* Pivoting from a failure (BetArcade), solving your own complaint (OfferPal commissions).

### Buffer — Law of 100 example
"Compare that to the story of the guys from Buffer.com. . . . I don't know why I was such a hater. What I do know is, it's over ten years later now and their business is doing $20 million in recurring revenue." (Ch 7, PDF p. 100)
*Illustrates:* Law of 100 — they stuck with it, Kagan's competing podcast didn't.

### Facebook — Started in a weekend
"Mark Zuckerberg started Facebook in a weekend by emailing friends to use it. Version 1 did well, validating it." (Ch 3, PDF p. 46) Also listed in the leap-into-the-unknown openers: "Facebook: Started as a weekend project similar to Hot or Not for college students." (Start Here, PDF p. 9)
*Illustrates:* Customer-first validation via personal network; weekend MVP.

### Microsoft — Bill Gates / Albuquerque customer first
"And Microsoft started with Bill Gates building software for a guy in Albuquerque. He had a CUSTOMER FIRST." (Ch 3, PDF p. 46)
*Illustrates:* Customer First Approach predates "MVP" thinking by decades.

## Newsletter / Content / Audience

### Ali Abdaal — BMAT videos → 4.4M subs / $400K/mo
"Take Ali Abdaal. In 2017 he was a Cambridge University medical student who thought he'd try his hand at YouTube. His videos included things like tips on studying and memorization for the BMAT. . . . Today, Ali is a massive star with more than 4.4 million YouTube subscribers and earns more than $400,000 a month." (Ch 6, PDF p. 95)
*Illustrates:* Content Circle Framework — niche within a niche (Core → Medium → Large Circle).

### Justin Welsh — LinkedIn course
"But Justin Welsh has used LinkedIn to sell a course that brought in $1.3 million in the first two years." (Ch 6, PDF pp. 93–94)
*Illustrates:* Platform fit — consultants thrive on LinkedIn.

### Matt Taibbi — Substack
"Former *Rolling Stone* writer Matt Taibbi brings in 1.3 million visitors a month and over $500,000 a year on Substack, so blogging can still work." (Ch 6, PDF p. 94)
*Illustrates:* Blogging is not dead for the right unique angle.

### Harry Dry / Marketing Examples — Newsletter
"A few years ago, I contacted Harry Dry from Marketing Examples, a young kid from England. . . . Today, Harry has 100,000 subscribers on his email list, 30,000 LinkedIn followers, and 140,000 on Twitter. He's doing epic!" (Ch 9, PDF p. 142)
*Illustrates:* Target Prefluencers — connect with ambitious people before they "make it."

### Sam Parr / thehustle.co — Newsletter pivot → HubSpot exit
"Sam Parr's thehustle.co went from throwing live events to focusing just on the newsletter they used to promote the events. Fun fact: that newsletter business sold to HubSpot for a high eight figures." (Ch 4, PDF p. 67)
*Illustrates:* Examples of pivots inside the One-Minute Business Model chapter.

### Bryan Harris / Videofruit — 10K+ subs
"My former Monthly1K student Bryan Harris used it and he's been able to get to 10,000-plus subscribers at Videofruit." (Ch 7, PDF p. 87) — using the Dream Ten template message.
*Illustrates:* 0 to 10 subscribers via Dream Ten.

### Chris Von Wilpert / HubSpot teardown → $100K AppSumo gig
"Chris Von Wilpert was trying to build a content marketing agency. He knew that the marketing software company HubSpot . . . was a massive object of interest among the marketers who made up his potential client base. So he decided on a strategy: Write an insanely detailed blog post dissecting the success of and drawing lessons from HubSpot's content marketing approach. He spent forty hours working on this. . . . within two weeks, his email list grew from 0 to over 1,000 subscribers!" Kagan messaged him to do a teardown of OkDork too — "In the end, that turned into a $100,000 payday for Chris, because after I asked him to reverse engineer another company's growth strategy for *my* OkDork . . . I offered him $100,000 to live in our Austin offices for six months and execute his content marketing strategy for AppSumo." (Ch 7, PDF p. 89)
*Illustrates:* Reverse-engineer giant content + Lead Magnet CTA; one massive piece of content as a wedge.

### Jess Dante / Love and London — Free guide
"Jess Dante of Love and London runs a YouTube channel helping viewers plan their trips to London by suggesting lesser-known restaurants and stores to visit. Her superstar opt-in incentive is a free *London 101 Guide* with everything a first-time visitor needs to know. It's been downloaded more than 45,000 times." (Ch 7, PDF p. 91)
*Illustrates:* High-relevance lead magnet.

### Dustin Wunderlich / Dustin's Fish Tanks
"Dustin Wunderlich of Dustin's Fish Tanks started by literally reviewing fish tanks. And you know what? There's a real audience for that. As time went on, he expanded to all things fish . . . and now he has 150,000 subscribers and a million-dollar online business selling fish and aquarium supplies." (Ch 6, PDF p. 96)
*Illustrates:* Core Circle → expand outward.

### Kyle Lasota / kylegotcamera — 16K subs / $1M/yr
"Then there's Kyle Lasota, the Austin-based creator behind the very cool YouTube channel kylegotcamera. Kyle's into biohacking. . . . While he's got a modest audience — 16,000 subscribers on YouTube — he's built such a tight community with his core that even though a lot of his videos get only 400 views, he nets a cool $1 million a year from affiliate sales." (Ch 6, PDF p. 96)
*Illustrates:* Tight Core Circle > vanity subscriber count.

### Danny Wang Design — TikTok lawn care, 2.3M
"Take Danny Wang Design, the local lawn care company that posts riveting weekly 45-second before-and-after clips of beautifying their clients' yards to snappy music (and targets their posts to homeowners within their service area). They now have 2.3 million TikTok followers and regularly get 150,000 views — and often TONS more — for their backyard #transformations. And Danny himself is nowhere to be found in the videos." (Ch 6, PDF p. 92)
*Illustrates:* Faceless brand, geo-targeted social.

### Matt's Off-Road Recovery — 1.4M subs
"An example of this is the Matt's Off-Road Recovery YouTube channel, which has 1.4 million subscribers showing Matt just doing his regular day job — using his tow truck to help people who've gotten stuck — in a way that makes it entertaining and instructive." (Ch 6, PDF p. 98)
*Illustrates:* Be the guide, not the guru — document your real work.

### SunnyV2 — Faceless, 2M+ subs
"SO many channels have gotten massive like SunnyV2 (two plus million subscribers to his documentaries about famous people), and we've never seen his face." (Ch 6, PDF p. 94)
*Illustrates:* You don't need to show your face on YouTube.

### Andy Schneider / Chicken Whisperer
"Andy Schneider, aka the Chicken Whisperer, just loved raising chickens in his backyard outside Atlanta. People kept asking him for info and hints on raising their own, so he started holding regular meetings about raising backyard poultry (like real-life YouTube!). Five years later, Schneider had a radio show, a magazine, and a book, and now he travels around the United States, leading workshops. Hundreds of thousands of dollars followed. But it all started with serving a niche within a niche — backyard farmers who wanted to raise chickens." (Ch 6, PDF p. 96)
*Illustrates:* Core Circle, offline community → multi-channel media business.

## Service / Local

### Eric / Foothills Painting — $750K/yr from door-knocking
"My friend Eric booked $8,000 of business in two weeks by knocking on doors and handing people a flyer, saying, 'Hey, how's it goin'? I'm Eric with Foothills Painting. I noticed you had some peeling paint up here on your house, so I wanted to give you a free estimate.' That two-sentence pitch built him a $750,000-a-year business." (Ch 5, PDF p. 74)
*Illustrates:* Direct preselling in the offline world; price + benefit + time pitch.

### Robert Samuel / Same Ole Line Dudes (SOLD) — NYC line-sitting
"After getting fired from his customer service job at AT&T, he noticed the frenzy created by every new iPhone release. So he put an ad on Craigslist in 2012 to validate whether anyone would pay for him to wait in line. When he was paid $325 for his first fifteen-hour stint, he knew he had a wave. Today, his business Same Ole Line Dudes (SOLD) employs thirty dudes and dudettes, charging a $50 minimum up to two hours and $25 for each additional hour to wait in line for everything from hot new sneakers to the DMV to the newest iPhone release — and Robert himself takes home *$80,000 a year*." (Ch 4, PDF p. 55)
*Illustrates:* Tidal Wave Markets that have nothing to do with tech; Craigslist validation.

### HFG Consulting — Kagan's student discount card at UC Berkeley
"At UC Berkeley, I created a consulting company — HFG Consulting — for local businesses on how they could market to college students. . . . Pretty soon, we expanded to multiple campuses — wash, rinse, repeat — and generated $50,000 within a year. Not bad for some freshmen interns, right?" (Ch 2, PDF p. 28)
*Illustrates:* NOW, Not How; ignore conventional wisdom; sell via fraternities as fundraisers.

### Dana — Horse-industry business
"Dana, a typical wantrepreneur who enrolled in my Monthly1K course to start a horse-industry business. She turned away from building a costly app and used the preselling method to quickly and cheaply validate instead. . . . Dana officially made her first dollar (actually $1,000) the first week she went live with her 'test' by messaging friends and people in her horse group. She didn't need a full-blown website or application to start the business. Heck, she didn't even have a company bank account or an LLC corporation — just a PayPal (or Venmo or Cash App or real dollars!) for the first few months." (Ch 5, PDF pp. 74–75)
*Illustrates:* Preselling via existing community; skip the infrastructure.

### Neville Medhora — Canon 7D Craigslist test
"My good bud Neville wanted to validate whether people would pay to rent expensive cameras. He posted one for rent on Craigslist and was able to get $75 for it in a few hours." Caption on the test ad clarifies: "This simple Validation cost him $0.00 and a few minutes of time." (Ch 5, PDF pp. 81–82)
*Illustrates:* Marketplaces as zero-cost validation rigs.

## Career / Asking / Persistence

### Noah Kagan's Facebook firing — 30th employee, ~$1B in stock lost
"It had been nine months, eight days, and about two hours since I was hired as Facebook's thirtieth employee. I was just twenty-four years old. . . . I got 0.1 percent of Facebook in stock, which in 2022 would have been worth about $1 billion. It was heaven." Then: "Noah, today's your last day." The shame became the seed of the experiments that eventually became MDW. (Ch 1, PDF pp. 16–17)
*Illustrates:* Embrace failure as a calling card; "the guy who was fired by Facebook" became Kagan's brand.

### Microsoft 12-follow-up internship — Noah Kagan
"My dream when I was in high school was to work at Microsoft. . . . I found a recruiter on campus . . . and I said to her, 'I'm not an engineer, but I'm in business. Is there any type of internship I could do to work at Microsoft for the summer?' She said there wasn't, but I followed up with her and kept asking. Squeak. Squeak. Squeak. And after the twelfth follow-up, she gave in: 'Actually, we have an internship for businesspeople.' . . . It did lead to a fun lunch at Bill Gates's house." (Ch 2, PDF p. 27)
Backed by Pro Tip: "Studies show that if you initially get a no, your follow-up ask is TWICE as likely to get a yes." (Ch 2, PDF p. 28)
*Illustrates:* Follow-up persistence; "almost every no you get can eventually become a yes."

### Kyle MacDonald — Paperclip to house in 14 trades
"Kyle MacDonald turned a red paper clip into a HOUSE with a series of just fourteen asks!" (Ch 2, PDF p. 24)
*Illustrates:* The act of asking as a power all its own; the unlimited upside of asking.

### Sara Blakely / Spanx — Dinner-table conditioning + 7 years door-to-door
"When she was growing up, the father of Spanx founder Sara Blakely would ask her and her brother nightly: 'What did you guys fail at this week?' It was this early conditioning to embrace failure that, Blakely says, helped her persist through seven years of almost daily humiliation selling fax machines door to door, persist after nearly every hosiery factory in America declined to manufacture her first product, and finally persist through a seemingly endless litany of noes before finally convincing a Dallas-based Neiman Marcus buyer on a cold call to put her body-shaping panty hose in a handful of stores. The average person faces one rejection and gives up. Blakely didn't, and at age forty-one became the youngest self-made female billionaire in the United States." (Ch 2, PDF pp. 26–27)
*Illustrates:* Rejection Goals; reframe rejection as desirable from childhood.
