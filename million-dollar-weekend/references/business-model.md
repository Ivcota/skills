# Business Model — Ch 4 Deep Dive

Reference for `million-dollar-weekend` skill. Source: Noah Kagan & Tahl Raz, *Million Dollar Weekend* (Penguin Random House, 2024), Ch 4 "The One-Minute Business Model: Shape Your Idea into a Million-Dollar Opportunity," PDF pp. 57–69.

Chapter promise: a three-part process to verify a million-dollar opportunity — (1) "Is this a million-dollar opportunity?" research the market; (2) "What's my model?" sketch revenue, cost, profit; (3) "What if it turns out it's not going to work?" pivot the variables (Ch 4, PDF p. 58).

---

## 1. The Starving Crowd / Tidal Wave Market

You are a surfer; the product is the surfboard; **the market is the wave, and the wave is what matters most.** "Even if you're a great surfer with an amazing board, you will still fail if you don't have a good wave to ride" (Ch 4, PDF p. 58).

> "What I'm saying here is that your job is not to create demand for something that seems exciting, it's to find existing demand and satisfy it." (Ch 4, PDF p. 59-60)

> "You don't want to be convincing people that they need your product. You don't want to be begging them to buy. What you want when you're opening a taco restaurant is a starving crowd." (Ch 4, PDF p. 60)

Kagan's own Tidal Wave bets (Ch 4, PDF p. 60):

- **Facebook** — college students and the world wanting to connect online.
- **Mint** — people wanting a free finance tool.
- **Kickflip** — Facebook/iPhone platforms opening up to game developers.
- **Gambit** — payments for the social-game wave.
- **AppSumo** — launched "just as the world decided to be entrepreneurs."

Waves don't have to be tech. Robert Samuel got fired from AT&T, posted a Craigslist ad in 2012 offering to wait in line for an iPhone launch, was paid $325 for his first 15-hour stint, and grew **Same Ole Line Dudes (SOLD)** into a 30-person business charging $50 minimum / $25 per additional hour, taking home $80,000/year (Ch 4, PDF pp. 58-59). Codie Sanchez built 1M+ followers teaching "boring businesses" like vending machines and RV rentals (Ch 4, PDF p. 59).

**Rule:** "In order to have a million-dollar business, you need a million-dollar opportunity." (Ch 4, PDF p. 60)

---

## 2. The Two Market Questions

Before you do anything else, answer (Ch 4, PDF p. 61):

1. **Is the overall market dying, flat, or growing?** "You want flat or ideally growing!"
2. **Is this a million-dollar opportunity?** Determined by number of potential customers × price.

"Market size is the single most important variable to quickly understanding the potential of any project." (Ch 4, PDF p. 61)

---

## 3. Tools Walkthrough

Kagan uses Google Trends and Facebook Ads to answer the two questions (Ch 4, PDF p. 61). Tools may change, but the job is to surface the data on size and direction.

### Google Trends — direction

Search the category term and adjacent terms. Example for beard oil (Ch 4, PDF p. 61):

- Search: `beard`, `beard grooming`
- Compare against: `haircuts`, `razors`
- "With Google Trends you are just looking to see how the graphs are trending — ideally up and to the right."

### Facebook Ads audience estimator — size

"Facebook is pretty much the entire world, and what's phenomenal is that they actually let you type in the keyword of whatever business category you're thinking about and see the approximate audience size." (Ch 4, PDF p. 62)

Worked numbers on beards (Ch 4, PDF p. 62):

- Audience: `Beards`, United States, age 18-65+ → estimated audience size **16,700,000 – 19,600,000**
- Narrower keyword `beard oil` → roughly **2.5 million people**

Then sub-segment to find sharper wedges (Ch 4, PDF p. 62):

- **Locals:** beard owners in local cities
- **Niche:** narrow interests like ingrown facial hair
- **Demographics:** African American beard owners

### Facebook Ads Library — competitor research

"You can also use the Facebook Ads Library to see every single active ad running on Facebook for your keyword and location, which is super helpful for uncovering competitors and getting ideas for your own marketing efforts." (Ch 4, PDF p. 62)

## 4. The Million-Dollar Opportunity Formula

Three lines, no spreadsheet (Ch 4, PDF p. 63):

1. Pick a price point you think will be ideal for your customer.
2. Multiply that by the number of ideal customers.
3. Does that equal at least a million dollars? Yes or no?

> "Don't be a wantrepreneur and waste time calculating revenue to the exact cent or fretting about the optimal price point. We are just trying to see if this is a million-dollar idea, super-fast." (Ch 4, PDF p. 63)

### Worked example — Beard Oil (YES)

(Ch 4, PDF p. 63)

| Field          | Value           |
| -------------- | --------------- |
| Google Trends  | Flat with some growth |
| Size of market | 2,500,000 people |
| Cost of product | $50            |
| Total Value    | **$125,000,000** |
| Million-dollar idea? | **YES**   |

### Worked example — Home Office Setup (YES)

(Ch 4, PDF p. 63): Trends "significantly up," 50,000 people × $500 = **$25,000,000** → YES.

### Counter-example — Monthly Vietnamese Pho Broth Subscription (NO)

(Ch 4, PDF p. 64): Trends "little interest and no growth," 1,000 people × $20 = **$20,000** → NO.

> "I'm sorry if you love making pho broth and want to deliver it, but I wouldn't touch this business idea because I don't see enough demand for it! It could be a fun passion project, but it's not a million-dollar business." (Ch 4, PDF p. 64)

### Comparison table (Ch 4, PDF p. 64)

| Data / Business Idea | Home Office Setup | Nonalcoholic Drink Subscriptions | Juicy Ass Pho Broth | Crypto Taxes |
| -------------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------ |
| Google Trends        | Significantly up  | Significantly up                 | Up                  | Rocket ship  |
| Size of market       | 50,000            | 50,000                           | 1,000               | 50,000       |
| Cost                 | $500              | $200                             | $20                 | $250         |
| Market opportunity   | $25,000,000       | $10,000,000                      | $20,000             | $12,500,000  |
| Worth pursuing?      | YES               | YES                              | NO                  | YES          |

## 5. The One-Minute Business Model Formula

> "Revenue (all the money you make) – Cost (how much it costs to make it) = Profit (what you get to take home)" (Ch 4, PDF p. 64)

Then divide $1M target profit by profit per unit to get **units required**.

### Beard Oil worked example (Ch 4, PDF p. 65)

| Field                       | Value     |
| --------------------------- | --------- |
| Beard oil sells for         | $50.00    |
| Cost to make, package, ship | $37.50    |
| Profit per unit sold        | **$12.50** |

| Field                | Value      |
| -------------------- | ---------- |
| Target Profit        | $1,000,000 |
| Profit per unit sold | $12.50     |
| Total sales needed   | **80,000** |

80,000 sounds hard, but Kagan reframes it (Ch 4, PDF p. 65): it's only the people visible via Facebook; it's only the first product (you can add more grooming SKUs); it's only the first sale (existing customers are easier to resell); and a subscription would lift per-customer revenue. His friend's Beardbrand.com is a real multimillion-dollar version of this.

### Sumo Jerky worked example — model forces a pivot (Ch 4, PDF pp. 66-67)

Original 1 bag of jerky:

| Field                            | Value |
| -------------------------------- | ----- |
| Price                            | $20   |
| Cost to acquire, package, ship   | $15   |
| Profit                           | **$5** |

| Target profit | Profit per unit | Total sales needed |
| ------------- | --------------- | ------------------ |
| $1,000        | $5              | **200**            |

"Two hundred bags of jerky in twenty-four hours?! Oof, too many sales in too little time!" (Ch 4, PDF p. 66)

## 6. Recurring Revenue Priority

> "**PRO TIP:** When you're launching a business, always ask yourself: is this going to be a one-off purchase, something customers buy here and there when they want to consume it, or can you make it a monthly recurring sale?" (Ch 4, PDF p. 67)

> "It's always better to be in the reorder business." — John Paul Jones DeJoria, founder of Paul Mitchell and Patrón Spirits (Ch 4, PDF p. 67)

Kagan's pivot to a **6-month subscription** (Ch 4, PDF pp. 67-68):

| Field         | Value |
| ------------- | ----- |
| Subscription price | $120 |
| Product cost  | $60   |
| Shipping      | $30   |
| Profit        | **$30** |

Now $1,000 ÷ $30 = **33 sales** — vs. 200 single-bag sales. Same target, 6× less selling.

## 7. The Six Revenue Dials

When the model doesn't math out, turn one of these dials instead of killing the idea (Ch 4, PDF p. 68):

1. **Average order value** — increase the amount someone purchases per transaction. *Kagan's move:* sold 6-month subscriptions instead of single bags, jumping profit from $5 → $30 per order. From his conference business: "Oh, I can make a lot more money just selling package sponsorships to companies, who can then hand out tickets to employees or customers." (Ch 4, PDF p. 67)
2. **Frequency** — increase how often someone will buy. *Worked:* turning jerky from one-off into monthly reorders; "if they were all three-month subscriptions, I'd need to sell only 67. And six-month? Only 33." (Ch 4, PDF p. 67)
3. **Price point** — increase or decrease price to affect total sales. *Implied across examples:* beard oil at $50 vs. pho broth at $20 changes the same market size into a wildly different opportunity.
4. **Customer type** — approach a more lucrative / wealthier segment. *Worked:* Kagan moved jerky from individuals to "offices that offered snacks, it would be easier to find customers with larger disposable budgets" (Ch 4, PDF p. 67). Also: AppSumo "started as bundle software for Silicon Valley startups but moved to selling individual deals to marketing agencies" (Ch 4, PDF p. 68).
5. **Product line** — add additional products to make the business more attractive. *Worked:* "If you find success with beard oil, you can easily repeat the process many times with other grooming products" (Ch 4, PDF p. 65).
6. **Add-on services** — bolt services onto a product. Book's example: "If you're selling a product like cookies, can you offer a service like setting up birthday parties or cooking at the person's home?" (Ch 4, PDF p. 68)

## 8. Case Study — Sam Parr / thehustle.co

Listed under "Examples of Pivots" (Ch 4, PDF p. 68):

> "Sam Parr's thehustle.co went from throwing live events to focusing just on the newsletter they used to promote the events. Fun fact: that newsletter business sold to HubSpot for a high eight figures."

Same chapter also names Gambit's pivot: "started out making sports games on Facebook but found the real opportunity in payments for social games instead." (Ch 4, PDF p. 68)

Lesson: the wave you started chasing is often not the wave you ride to exit. The promotional channel (newsletter) was the real Tidal Wave Market, not the front-end product (events).

## 9. Worksheet — One-Minute Business Model Template

Copy this skeleton (mirrors the chapter's fill-in tables at Ch 4, PDF pp. 63-69):

```
The idea you are going to do: _______________________

— MARKET CHECK —
| Data / Business Idea          | Your Idea |
| ----------------------------- | --------- |
| Google Trends (direction)     |           |
| Size of market (FB Ads est.)  |           |
| Cost of product (price)       |           |
| Market opportunity (size×$)   |           |
| Worth Pursuing? (≥ $1M?)      |           |

— PROFIT CHECK —
| Field   | Your Idea |
| ------- | --------- |
| Price   |           |
| Cost    |           |
| Profit  |           |

— UNITS-TO-$1M CHECK —
| Field                | Your Idea |
| -------------------- | --------- |
| Your target profit   | $1,000,000 |
| Profit per unit sold |           |
| Total sales needed   |           |

— REVENUE DIALS TO TURN IF UNITS ARE TOO HIGH —
[ ] 1. Average order value (bundle / sponsorship / multi-pack)
[ ] 2. Frequency (one-off → 3-month → 6-month subscription)
[ ] 3. Price point (raise / lower)
[ ] 4. Customer type (individuals → businesses / wealthier segment)
[ ] 5. Product line (add adjacent SKUs)
[ ] 6. Add-on services (bolt service onto product)

Recurring-revenue check: Is this a one-off, or can it be a monthly recurring sale?
```

Process (Ch 4, PDF p. 69): (1) Pick one business idea. (2) Make sure it's a million-dollar opportunity. (3) Confirm your business idea is profitable. If it fails any check, "move on to your next idea and run it through the same assessment. Don't get in your own way, wondering which idea is best."

> "The hard part is not choosing which business idea. The hard part is getting customers. And that's where you'll focus first." (Ch 4, PDF p. 68)
