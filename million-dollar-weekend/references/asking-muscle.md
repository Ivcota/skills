# The Asking Muscle

Deep-dive on Chapter 2, *The Unlimited Upside of Asking: Get a Gold Medal in Rejection* (Noah Kagan, *Million Dollar Weekend*, 2024). Page numbers refer to the PDF of the Penguin Random House edition; the chapter runs roughly PDF pp. 28-45.

Kagan's claim is blunt: after teaching 10,000+ people through his Monthly1K course, "the number one thing that held people back from business success was NOT a lack of strategy . . . but *Ask Avoidance*" (Ch 2, PDF p. 29). This file unpacks the diagnosis, the antidote, and the drills.

---

## 1. Ask Avoidance

**Definition.** Ask Avoidance is the default human pattern of *wanting* something but never directly requesting it. Kagan names the substitutes: "most people don't ask for what they want. They wish for it, they make 'suggestions' and drop hints, they hope. But the simple fact of business is that only by asking do you receive what you want. No ASK? No GET. That applies to every part of life. Seriously, every part." (Ch 2, PDF p. 27)

**Why it's the #1 blocker.** Kagan frames it as a straitjacket made of imagined consequences: "The illusion of eventual pain that you associate with taking that risk — what if you're judged or look foolish or it doesn't work? — is a straitjacket on your potential. Removing the jacket — stepping forward into the uncertainty with that first ASK — is the game-changing skill that starts your million-dollar business and redesigns your life." (Ch 2, PDF p. 30)

**The mechanism.** Getting money "is NOT a matter of literally getting it. It's a matter of RECEIVING IT, which can happen only after one asks for it." (Ch 2, PDF p. 29). The transaction is gated on the request, not on worth, skill, or readiness.

**Kagan's own susceptibility.** He explicitly denies being fearless. "I'm not superhuman . . . *Noah must be fearless in the face of rejection.* But no, I get scared every single time I face rejection, and I get sad when it happens. Every day, I feel the sting of rejection. And because of that, I succeed." (Ch 2, PDF p. 30). The skill is not the absence of fear; it is the willingness to ask through fear.

**Diagnostic.** If you catch yourself doing any of these, you are in Ask Avoidance:
- Wishing ("I wish they'd notice and offer it.")
- Hinting ("I dropped a hint that I'd be open to . . .")
- Hoping ("I'm hoping they come around.")
- Researching one more thing before reaching out
- Crafting the perfect email indefinitely

## 2. Rejection Goals

**Origin: Kagan's dad.** Kagan's father, an Israeli immigrant copier salesman, hit roughly ten shops a day getting rejected. After a glorious day with two sales, young Noah was demoralized. His father replied with the line Kagan says "would change my life": "Love rejections! Collect them like treasure. Set rejection goals. I shoot for a hundred rejections each week, because if you work that hard to get so many noes, my little Noah'le, in them you will find a few yeses, too." (Ch 2, PDF p. 26)

The reframe: "My dad reframed rejection as something desirable — so you feel good when you get it. He was saying aim for rejection! . . . the upside of asking is unlimited and the downside is minimal." (Ch 2, PDF p. 26)

**Kagan's adult target: 25 rejections.** When the sting hits Kagan today, he runs the same drill: "Then I remind myself of **Rejection Goals**: 'This is going to suck. Let me aim to get at least twenty-five rejections.' That alone helps me accept that I will get rejected and turn it more into a game versus a blow to my self-worth." (Ch 2, PDF p. 30)

**Why a goal of noes works.**
1. It inverts the scoring system — every no is a point *for* you, not against.
2. It removes outcome dependency from any single ask.
3. It gamifies the muscle, which is what the muscle needs to grow.

**How to track.** The book doesn't prescribe a tool, but the implication is a tally: a notes app, a journal column, a tick-mark card. The act of marking the no is what closes the loop. See the worksheet at the end.

**The cost of stopping early.** "Remember, you could be eleven noes away from making your first million, but if you stop at the tenth rejection, you will have failed." (Ch 2, PDF p. 32)

**The desensitization principle.** "The trick is to desensitize yourself to the pain by repeatedly exposing yourself to it. Embrace the discomfort — actively seeking it out — and use it as your compass." (Ch 2, PDF p. 32)

## 3. The Coffee Challenge

This is the chapter's centerpiece drill. "This is the most powerful tool I've ever seen for improving your Ask muscle, and over ten thousand people have done it." (Ch 2, PDF p. 38)

**Protocol (Ch 2, PDF p. 37):**
> Go to any coffee shop or anyplace in person. Make a simple purchase and ask for 10 percent off. Don't say anything else. The whole point is for you to feel uncomfortable. Commit to doing this today.

**The exact script (Ch 2, PDF p. 38):**
- YOU: Hi, how's it going?
- THEM: Great, what would you like?
- YOU: I'd like a skinny low-fat vanilla latte [my fav drink, or substitute your own].
- THEM: Sure. That'll be $3.50.
- YOU: Can I get 10 percent off? *[This is key: Make the statement clear, with a smile, and don't say anything afterwards.]*
- THEM: What's this for?
- YOU: I'm taking a business course and this is one of the assignments. :)

**Why these specific design choices:**

- **Why 10%, not free, not 50%.** Small enough that the request isn't outrageous, large enough that the asker feels the discomfort. The point is not the discount; it is the feeling. Kagan emphasizes the discount itself is trivial: "asking for a discount on your cup of coffee is no big deal." (Ch 2, PDF p. 37)
- **Why "say nothing else."** Silence after the ask is where the muscle is actually built. Most people defuse their own ask with apologies, explanations, or jokes before the other person even has a chance to respond. The discipline is to hold the ask and let it land.
- **Why in person.** No hiding behind a screen. Eye contact, a real human, real social stakes.
- **Why a script for the "why" question.** "I'm taking a business course" works because (a) it's true if you count this skill as a course, (b) it removes the need for you to justify, and (c) it usually generates goodwill rather than friction.

**Excuses Kagan pre-empts:**
> "Oh, that's just too basic, I don't want to be that guy." "I don't want to put the barista in an awkward position." "I've already done sales for five years." (Ch 2, PDF p. 38)
>
> That's the whole point of this challenge: to practice *Ask*ing (and getting rejected) rather than talking yourself out of it. The worst-case scenario is really trivial. (Ch 2, PDF p. 38)

**What people report after.** Three testimonials Kagan includes (Ch 2, PDF pp. 38-39):
- Dieter S.: "I felt a lot more confident facing rejection, and it empowered me to successfully ask for sponsorship money on my bike-riding side hustle."
- Jennifer Jones: "It was scary, and I was not looking forward to it. But I did it and grew. Honestly, I'm insanely shy! So having to do things out of my comfort zone has helped me in all areas of my life and has made me a better person overall."
- Jason Blake: "Not only did I learn that rejection doesn't end your life, but I also learned to enjoy being outside my comfort zone."

**The frame Kagan gives it.** "Asking is a muscle, and this challenge is just like the gym. Learning to ask is just like building any new habit. Start small and increase slowly. The best way to overcome your fear in the long term is with short-term games of rejection." (Ch 2, PDF p. 39)

He calls the feeling on the other side **Creator's Courage**: "getting that hit of **Creator's Courage** will help you hit your rejection goals and unlock asking's unlimited upside." (Ch 2, PDF p. 39)

## 4. Follow-Up Doctrine

**The 12-follow-up Microsoft story.** Kagan as a junior at UC Berkeley wanted to work at Microsoft. He found a campus recruiter and pitched: "I'm not an engineer, but I'm in business. Is there any type of internship I could do to work at Microsoft for the summer?" She said no. He kept asking. "Squeak. Squeak. Squeak. And after the twelfth follow-up, she gave in: 'Actually, we have an internship for businesspeople.' I don't know if it was created for me or not, but I like to think it was. It did lead to a fun lunch at Bill Gates's house, a story for another time." (Ch 2, PDF p. 33)

**The AppSumo data point.** "At AppSumo.com, almost 50 percent of our sales come from our follow-up emails. Think about that. What a great example that follow-ups are as powerful as your first touch point." (Ch 2, PDF p. 33)

**The general principle.** "Studies show that if you initially get a no, your follow-up ask is TWICE as likely to get a yes." (Ch 2, PDF p. 33). And: "almost every no you get can eventually become a yes. Persistence will reveal that most noes are actually a 'not now.'" (Ch 2, PDF p. 32)

**The tools Kagan names (Ch 2, PDF p. 33).**
- **followup.cc** — email follow-up reminders (BCC the service with a date; it bounces the thread back to your inbox on that date).
- **Siri** — set verbal reminders the moment you commit to a follow-up.
- **Google Snooze** — Gmail's snooze feature returns a thread to the top of your inbox at a chosen time.
- **Write it down** — Kagan explicitly endorses the lowest-tech option.

**The doctrine in one line.** "Follow up on the things you really want." (Ch 2, PDF p. 33)

## 5. The "Selling is Helping" Reframe

**Kagan's framing (Ch 2, PDF p. 34):**
> If you believe your product or service improves the lives of your customers, sales is just education. You're helping people out. Reframing selling/asking as helping makes it exciting to offer your consulting or window-washing services or provide someone with delicious cookies. Once you accept that truth, asking becomes loads easier and feels much more like a communal gift than a selfish desire.

**The Zig Ziglar quote (Ch 2, PDF p. 34):**
> If you believe your product or service can fulfill a true need, it's your moral obligation to sell it.
> —Zig Ziglar

**Why this matters.** The reframe converts the ask from extraction (I'm taking from them) to contribution (I'm giving them access to something useful). It also flips the moral weight of *not* asking: if you truly believe in what you have, withholding it is the selfish act, not offering it.

**Worked example: HFG Consulting and the student discount cards.** When Kagan's intern Kenny suggested a student discount card business at Berkeley, Kagan tested it that day: "We just talked about how it would help them" — both the local businesses (free customer acquisition) and the student fraternities (a fundraising tool, 50/50 split of $10 cards that cost $0.50 to print). The frame was helping on both sides. Result: $50,000 in a year. (Ch 2, PDF pp. 35-36)

## 6. Sara Blakely / Spanx Case

**The dinner-table conditioning.** "When she was growing up, the father of Spanx founder Sara Blakely would ask her and her brother nightly: 'What did you guys fail at this week?'" (Ch 2, PDF p. 30)

**What that conditioning bought her, per Kagan (Ch 2, PDF pp. 30-31):** the early embrace of failure helped Blakely:
1. Persist through **seven years of almost daily humiliation selling fax machines door to door**.
2. Persist after **nearly every hosiery factory in America declined** to manufacture her first product.
3. Persist through **a seemingly endless litany of noes** before
4. **Convincing a Dallas-based Neiman Marcus buyer on a cold call** to put her body-shaping panty hose in a handful of stores.

**The contrast Kagan draws.** "The average person faces one rejection and gives up. Blakely didn't, and at age forty-one became the youngest self-made female billionaire in the United States." (Ch 2, PDF p. 32)

**The lesson Kagan extracts.** Rejection tolerance is *taught*. The Blakely dinner question is a parental version of Rejection Goals — a daily ritual that reframes failure as a desirable artifact to bring to the table. The skill is buildable in adults via the same kind of repeated, low-stakes exposure (which is what the Coffee Challenge is engineered to provide).

## 7. Kyle MacDonald: Red Paperclip to House

Kagan invokes this case once, briefly, but it carries the chapter's thesis (Ch 2, PDF p. 27):

> The ultimate sales hack, the one that lets you live your dreams, has nothing to do with finding the perfect way to ask. The act of asking is a power all its own. Case in point: Kyle MacDonald turned a red paper clip into a HOUSE with a series of just fourteen asks!

The point Kagan is making is structural, not tactical: MacDonald did not have leverage, capital, network, or product. He had a willingness to ask, repeatedly, across a chain of trades. The compounding came from the asks themselves.

## 8. Always Be Asking

Kagan extends the muscle past sales into the rest of life (Ch 2, PDF p. 32):

> Who is the type of person that starts a million-dollar business?
> - The type of person who asks for what they want.
> - If you want a new job at a new company, you have to ASK for it.
> - If you want more money from your boss, you have to ASK for the raise.
> - If you are selling something, you have to ASK the customer to buy it.
> - Even at home, if you want your spouse or kids to treat you better, you have to ASK them.
>
> Everything that signifies a growing, profitable, and fulfilling venture — a supportive network, flourishing sales, engaged employees, a healthy balance of work and play, and so forth — all requires a willingness to ASK over and over again.

**Domains where the muscle pays:**
- **Jobs** — Kagan's Microsoft internship (created by his 12th follow-up).
- **Raises** — direct ask, not hoping for the annual review to handle it.
- **Sales** — every customer transaction begins with a request to buy.
- **Family** — even relational quality requires explicit asks.
- **Hiring** — Kagan's example of six-to-eight hours a day of cold-emailing designers (Ch 2, PDF p. 30).

**The "chutzpah" frame.** Kagan attributes his dad's success to chutzpah — "the Yiddish word for moxie, nerve, audacity; it's a determined, give-no-f*cks approach to life. When Israelis say you have chutzpah, they mean you know what you want and go for it. They mean you'll do what it takes." (Ch 2, PDF p. 27). Plus his mom's line: "The squeaky wheel gets the grease" — and a personal data point: "this is a woman who tried to return her wedding-present silverware thirty years later, just to see if she could." (Ch 2, PDF p. 27)

**The immigrant edge.** "Having this ability to ask is the reason so many immigrants or children of immigrants fare well in business. Like my father, they aren't worried about the social consequences of doing something they aren't supposed to do because they don't *know* what they're not supposed to do. That means they can be naive and ask for anything, which is a business superpower." (Ch 2, PDF p. 28). Implication: the asking edge is available to anyone who decides to act as if the social rules against asking don't apply.

## 9. Worksheet

Three artifacts to make the chapter operational. Copy these into your journal or a fresh doc.

### 9a. Rejection Goal Commitment Card

```
WEEK OF: ____________________

MY REJECTION GOAL THIS WEEK: ______ rejections
(Kagan's adult target: 25/week. His dad's: 100/week. Pick yours.)

DOMAIN(S) I'M ASKING IN THIS WEEK:
  [ ] Sales / customers
  [ ] Hiring / collaborators
  [ ] Partnerships / intros
  [ ] Job / raise / promotion
  [ ] Personal (family, friends, favors)

DAILY TALLY:
  Mon  | | | | | | | | | |    (___ noes)
  Tue  | | | | | | | | | |    (___ noes)
  Wed  | | | | | | | | | |    (___ noes)
  Thu  | | | | | | | | | |    (___ noes)
  Fri  | | | | | | | | | |    (___ noes)
  Sat  | | | | | | | | | |    (___ noes)
  Sun  | | | | | | | | | |    (___ noes)

WEEK TOTAL: ___ noes / ___ yeses

SIGNATURE (to make it real): ______________________
```

### 9b. Coffee Challenge Log

Do this *today*. Do not read further until you have logged at least one attempt.

```
ATTEMPT #___        DATE: __________      LOCATION: __________________

PRE-ASK (before walking in):
  Predicted feeling (1-10 dread): ___
  Predicted outcome (yes/no/weird look): __________

THE ASK (use the script verbatim):
  YOU: Hi, how's it going?
  THEM: ____________________
  YOU: I'd like a __________________________ .
  THEM: That'll be $______.
  YOU: Can I get 10 percent off?    <-- hold the silence
  THEM: ____________________
  YOU: (if asked why) I'm taking a business course
       and this is one of the assignments. :)

OUTCOME:
  [ ] Got the discount
  [ ] Got a no
  [ ] Got a weird look
  [ ] Got a yes after explaining

POST-ASK:
  Actual feeling (1-10 dread): ___
  Gap between predicted and actual: ___
  What I'd change next time: ______________________
```

Repeat until the predicted-vs-actual gap collapses. That's the muscle being built.

### 9c. Follow-Up Cadence Template

For any ask you actually care about (job, partnership, sale, intro), pre-schedule the follow-ups *at the moment you send the first ask* — don't wait to see if you hear back.

```
THE ASK: ____________________________________________
SENT TO: ____________________________________________
INITIAL SEND DATE: __________

FOLLOW-UP SCHEDULE (Kagan: avg 12 to convert, AppSumo: ~50% of revenue from follow-ups):
  #1  +3 days   __________   [ ] sent  Tool: ____________
  #2  +7 days   __________   [ ] sent  Tool: ____________
  #3  +14 days  __________   [ ] sent  Tool: ____________
  #4  +30 days  __________   [ ] sent  Tool: ____________
  #5  +60 days  __________   [ ] sent  Tool: ____________
  ... continue until yes, hard no, or you decide to stop ...

TOOL OPTIONS (Ch 2, PDF p. 33):
  - followup.cc (BCC with a date, bounces back to inbox)
  - Siri reminder ("Hey Siri, remind me to follow up with X on Friday")
  - Gmail Snooze (snooze the sent thread to a future date)
  - Pen and paper

STOP CONDITION:
  [ ] They said yes
  [ ] They said a definitive no (not "not now")
  [ ] I decided the ask is no longer worth pursuing
```

**Operating rule.** "Almost every no you get can eventually become a yes. Persistence will reveal that most noes are actually a 'not now.'" (Ch 2, PDF p. 32). Until you hear a definitive no, the follow-up clock keeps running.
