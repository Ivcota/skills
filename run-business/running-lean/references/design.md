# Design

**Core concept:** Design a business model that could work before committing to customer validation: deconstruct the idea on Lean Canvas, stress test desirability, viability, and feasibility in that order, then communicate the resulting story. This is the Design part of the ordered Design → Validation → Growth journey; a plausible model is a starting hypothesis, not evidence that customers want it. [S1, ch. 1, "What’s Next?"; S1, ch. 3, "Running a Fermi Estimate on Your Idea"; S1, ch. 5, "Steve Shares His Business Model Pitch with Others"]

**Why it works:** The canvas makes hidden assumptions visible; existing alternatives counter solution-first bias; bottom-up estimation exposes the inputs that could break the economics; traction milestones separate starting risks from scaling risks. Together these tools direct learning toward customer and market risks before overinvesting in a technical solution. [S1, ch. 1, "Refining Your Lean Canvas"; S1, ch. 2, "Meet the Innovator’s Gift"; S1, ch. 3, "Isn’t this just funny math?"; S1, ch. 4, "Formulating a Now-Next-Later Rollout Plan"]

## Key insights

### Chapter 1 — Deconstruct Your Idea on a Lean Canvas

- **Capture how you create, deliver, and capture value.** Put the business-model assumptions on a single page rather than carrying them only in your head. Follow the chapter’s working order below; this is a completion sequence, not the spatial arrangement of boxes. [S1, ch. 1, "Sketching Your First Lean Canvas"; S1, ch. 1, "Steve Splits His Big Idea Canvas into Specific Variants"]

| Working order / field | What to capture | Decision discipline |
|---|---|---|
| Customer Segments, including Early Adopters | Identify the paying customer, other interacting actors, overall addressable segment, and specific starting subset. | A customer pays; a user does not. Model different actors’ problems, channels, and value propositions on the same canvas using colors or hashtags. [S1, ch. 1, "Customer Segments"; S1, ch. 1, "Distinguish between customers and users"; S1, ch. 1, "Model multiple perspectives"; S1, ch. 1, "Home in on early adopters"] |
| Problem, including Existing Alternatives | Prioritize pressing problems and how early adopters currently address them. | Do not restrict alternatives to obvious competitors. Evidence for scope: “prioritize the top one to three issues.” [S1, ch. 1, "List the top 1–3 problems"; S1, ch. 1, "List existing alternatives"] |
| Unique Value Proposition, including High-Concept Pitch | Explain why the product is different and worth attention; connect to the customer’s most important problem and desired outcome. | Be specific to early adopters. Describe what and who; use a subheadline for why if needed. Keep an analogy-based high-concept pitch separate from the landing-page UVP. [S1, ch. 1, "Unique Value Proposition"; S1, ch. 1, "Connect to your customer’s #1 problem"; S1, ch. 1, "Target early adopters"; S1, ch. 1, "Answer what, who, and why"; S1, ch. 1, "Create a high-concept pitch"] |
| Solution | Sketch the simplest possible response to each listed problem. | Do not fully specify the product before conversations; problems may be reprioritized or replaced. Bind the solution late. [S1, ch. 1, "Solution"] |
| Channels | Identify initial paths to potential customers and possible scalable channels. | Early channels optimize learning, not scale, but start considering and testing scalable paths from the outset. [S1, ch. 1, "Channels"] |
| Revenue Streams | State a pricing model now. | Price affects perceived value, signals which customers you seek, and makes payment available as an early validation signal. [S1, ch. 1, "Revenue streams"] |
| Cost Structure | Estimate the runway to define, build, and launch the MVP and the ongoing burn rate. | Use near-term, stage-based costs rather than distant forecasts; revise after the milestone. [S1, ch. 1, "Cost structure"] |
| Key Metrics | Select customer-centric outcome metrics and leading indicators; study relevant industry analogs. | Evidence for scope: “list the top three to five metrics.” Revenue and profit still matter but trail progress; qualified leads, trials/pilots, and churn can provide earlier signals. [S1, ch. 1, "List 3–5 key metrics"; S1, ch. 1, "Prefer outcome metrics versus output metrics"; S1, ch. 1, "Prioritize leading indicator metrics versus trailing indicator metrics"; S1, ch. 1, "Study analogs"] |
| Unfair Advantage | Record an advantage that is difficult to copy or buy, or a credible story for developing one. | Passion, features, code, and being first are not enough. Leave the box blank rather than insert a weak claim. [S1, ch. 1, "Unfair Advantage"; S1, ch. 1, "Start with an unfair advantage story"; S1, ch. 1, "Leave the unfair advantage blank"] |

- **Move from feature to outcome.** The résumé example progresses from designed templates to a résumé that stands out to the outcome of obtaining the desired job. The UVP should express the customer’s result, not make the customer translate a feature list. [S1, ch. 1, "Focus on outcomes"]
- **Go narrow within a canvas and broad across variants.** An undifferentiated canvas aimed at everybody is too broad; a single highly specific interpretation can trap you on a local maximum. Split the big-idea canvas into specific alternatives, then shortlist and test them systematically. Each canvas should tell a single business-model story. [S1, ch. 1, "Refining Your Lean Canvas"; S1, ch. 1, "So, How Do You Avoid the Goldilocks Problem?"; S1, ch. 1, "How Do You Know When to Split Your Lean Canvas?"]
- **Separate archetypes, not every actor.** Direct models convert users into paying customers; multisided models monetize value from users through a different paying segment; marketplaces connect buyers and sellers. Split intertwined business-model stories, but preserve both relevant actor perspectives within an individual multisided or marketplace canvas. The source anchors are Starbucks, Facebook, and Airbnb respectively. [S1, ch. 1, "Direct"; S1, ch. 1, "Multisided"; S1, ch. 1, "Marketplace"]
- **Prefer a defensibility story to fake certainty.** Organic search ranking differs from purchasable keywords because it must be earned. Steve replaces software-IP defensibility with a platform-effect hypothesis: developer-built applications and reusable objects could make further development easier and reinforce platform adoption. That flywheel is still a story to realize, not an existing moat. [S1, ch. 1, "Unfair Advantage"; S1, ch. 1, "Steve ponders his unfair advantage story"]

### Chapter 2 — Stress Test Your Idea for Desirability

- **Define better through the customer’s outcome.** A compelling UVP offers a better outcome, a better way to achieve it, or both. Stress test Customer Segments, Problem, and UVP together. The Lean(er) Canvas contains these fields and can suit specialized product teams removed from sales and marketing; founders should still attempt the full canvas. [S1, ch. 2, "Defining Better"]
- **Apply the Innovator’s Gift: problems worth solving come from old solutions.** Do not ask which problems your chosen technology could solve. Ask what customers are already trying to accomplish, what they use now, and what goes wrong with that old way. The music examples show shifts driven by easier song access and purchasing, not a simple progression toward better sound quality. [S1, ch. 2, "Our Innovator’s Bias Gets in the Way"; S1, ch. 2, "Meet the Innovator’s Gift"]
- **Separate an ordinary trigger from a switching trigger.** A job-to-be-done arises when a trigger activates an unmet need or want. Existing habits normally determine which solution is hired. Hiring means selecting and using a solution, not merely buying it. A switching trigger violates expectations and prompts a search for a different solution; examples include a bad experience, changed circumstances, or an awareness event. [S1, ch. 2, "Unpacking the Innovator’s Gift"; S1, ch. 2, "All jobs start with a trigger"; S1, ch. 2, "Habits define what we do most of the time…"; S1, ch. 2, "…Until we encounter a switching trigger"]

| Customer force | Meaning | Design question |
|---|---|---|
| PUSH | Dissatisfaction or expectation violation motivating departure from the old way. | What happened that made the familiar alternative no longer good enough? [S1, ch. 2, "…Until we encounter a switching trigger"] |
| PULL | The promise of a significantly better way to get the job done. | What outcome or better route gives the customer a reason to move? [S1, ch. 2, "Causing a switch starts with a promise of better"] |
| INERTIA | Resistance from habit and the status quo. | Why is remaining with the familiar solution easier? [S1, ch. 2, "Causing a switch starts with a promise of better"] |
| FRICTION | Anxiety about adopting an unfamiliar way of doing things. | What makes the new way feel uncertain or difficult to adopt? [S1, ch. 2, "Causing a switch starts with a promise of better"] |

- **Design for the balance of forces, not incremental feature superiority.** Source evidence: “PUSH + PULL > INERTIA + FRICTION” and “Think 3x–10x better.” Treat that multiplier as the author’s design heuristic for a compelling improvement, not a validated universal conversion threshold. Functional improvement addresses needs; emotionally better positioning connects with wants and outcomes in the bigger context. [S1, ch. 2, "Causing a switch starts with a promise of better"; S1, ch. 2, "Emotionally better versus functionally better"; S1, ch. 2, "Emotionally better lives in the bigger context"]
- **Rework the canvas in the prescribed order:** broad Customer Segments → Early Adopters identified by switching triggers → Existing Alternatives across categories → Problems with those alternatives → UVP that could cause a switch. Use the fewest distinguishing traits that explain buying rather than elaborate guessed personas. Problems must make sense without reference to your proposed solution. [S1, ch. 2, "Using the Innovator’s Gift to Stress Test Your Idea for Desirability"; S1, ch. 2, "Customer Segments: Keep It Simple"; S1, ch. 2, "Early Adopters: Forget Personas"; S1, ch. 2, "Existing Alternatives: Transcend Category"; S1, ch. 2, "Problems: What’s Broken with the Old Way?"; S1, ch. 2, "UVP: How Will You Cause a Switch?"]
- **Getting hired is not the finish line.** Trial can lead to firing unless the product quickly delivers value and becomes the customer’s preferred way of getting the job done. Desirability design must connect the initial promise with later value delivery. [S1, ch. 2, "Getting hired is only the first battle"]

#### Case anchor: Steve’s hammer problem

Steve’s developer variant frames customers, competition, and benefits around AR/VR. He realizes the app is only a functional intermediate result: developers want billable work, retailers want to sell furniture, and architects want clients to visualize their homes. Asking what the technology is for shifts design toward these bigger outcomes rather than assuming demand for the technology itself. [S1, ch. 2, "Steve Realizes He Has a Hammer Problem"]

### Chapter 3 — Stress Test Your Idea for Viability

- **Use a bottom-up Fermi estimate, not a detailed forecast.** Justified rough input assumptions expose whether a model is plausible; elaborate forecasts can conceal risk and force teams to defend fictional plans. Test the model against your ambition rather than claiming a small share of a large market will be sufficient. [S1, ch. 3, "Don’t Create a Financial Forecast; Use a Fermi Estimate Instead"]
- **Define traction as a rate of captured monetizable value.** It is customer-centric and predictive of revenue, not simply cumulative registrations, current revenue, or profit. In Facebook’s multisided example, user attention and data are monetizable value converted into revenue through advertisers. The customer factory visualizes the full business as a system that makes happy customers, with feedback loops rather than just a linear funnel. [S1, ch. 3, "What Is Traction?"; S1, ch. 3, "Welcome to the Customer Factory"]

| Customer-factory step | Observable event | Source example |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | An unaware visitor becomes an interested prospect. | A passerby enters the flower shop; online, prefer the point where the visitor can be uniquely identified and contacted, such as an email capture. [S1, ch. 3, "Step 1. Acquisition"] |
| Activation | The prospect has the first gratifying experience that connects the promise with the product. | Attractive flower arrangements fulfill the storefront promise; software must quickly deliver the experience implied by the landing-page UVP. [S1, ch. 3, "Step 2. Activation"] |
| Retention | Repeated use or engagement. | Returning to the flower shop or returning to use the software. [S1, ch. 3, "Step 3. Retention"] |
| Revenue | An event that gets the business paid. | Buying flowers or a subscription; a trial/refund period can complicate whether early payment is durable. [S1, ch. 3, "Step 4. Revenue"] |
| Referral | Happy customers bring new prospects through a feedback loop. | Telling a friend, a sharing feature, or an affiliate program. [S1, ch. 3, "Step 5. Referral"] |

- **Set minimum success criteria independently of the idea.** MSC is the smallest outcome that would make the project worthwhile, beyond merely breaking even. Frame it in recurring annual revenue to keep the calculation simple, with appropriate consideration of margins or the funds needed to deliver impact. Investor expectations, desired bootstrapped company size, or corporate stakeholders’ definition of success determine the goal. Evidence for the recommended horizon: “Your MSC is the smallest outcome that would make you deem your project a success three years from now.” This is a planning recommendation, not a promise of success by that date. [S1, ch. 3, "Define a Target Throughput Goal"; S1, ch. 3, "Set your minimum success criteria independently from your idea"; S1, ch. 3, "Frame your goal in terms of annual recurring revenue (ARR)"; S1, ch. 3, "Your minimum success criteria are determined by your operating environment"]
- **Ballpark the destination; build a system to reach and sustain it.** Avoid false precision. A goal supplies the desired outcome, while customer-factory inputs describe the repeatable system needed to deliver it. Establish an MSC before customer validation so you do not discover only later that a plausible idea is too small for your ambition. [S1, ch. 3, "Focus on systems, not goals"; S1, ch. 3, "Don’t chase three-digit precision"; S1, ch. 3, "Don’t go outside the building without minimum success criteria"]

#### Viability worksheet: assumptions and calculations

Run the estimate in the source’s order: Revenue → Retention → Acquisition → Activation → Referral. Record the MSC, annual revenue per customer, lifetime/churn, paid conversion inputs, and referral assumption explicitly. For multisided and marketplace models, consider both sides. Formulas below are arithmetic restatements of the prose and worked example, not independently verified benchmarks. [S1, ch. 3, "Test Whether Your Idea Can Deliver Your Target Throughput Goal"; S1, ch. 3, "Running a Fermi Estimate on Your Idea"]

| Calculation | How to apply | Assumption to challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Required active customers = MSC annual revenue ÷ annual revenue per customer | Translate a subscription price into annual revenue before dividing; compare the resulting customer count with addressable and early-adopter segments. | Does the starting segment support the count without prematurely depending on mainstream adoption? [S1, ch. 3, "Estimate the required number of active customers"; S1, ch. 3, "Steve estimates how many active customers he’ll need"] |
| Monthly churn ≈ inverse of average lifetime in months | Estimate lifetime from industry analogs and the duration of the job’s utility; treat the inverse as a simplifying estimate. | Long retention assumptions require justification. Evidence: “above five years…justify your reasoning with additional evidence.” [S1, ch. 3, "Estimate the required minimum customer acquisition rate"; S1, ch. 3, "Steve estimates his minimum customer acquisition rate"] |
| Minimum new customers per month = active customers × monthly churn | Calculate the replacement rate needed merely to sustain the model. Growth requires more. | Do not confuse maintaining the endpoint with reaching it or growing beyond it. Source formula: “Number of Active Customers × Monthly Churn Rate.” [S1, ch. 3, "Estimate the required minimum customer acquisition rate"] |
| Required monthly leads = required new customers ÷ combined paid conversion rate | Combine acquisition, activation/trial, and customer-conversion assumptions at their defined steps. | Use product-specific research when possible. The author’s fallback is an assumption, not a fact about your product: “When in doubt…assume a 1% customer conversion rate.” [S1, ch. 3, "Estimate the required number of leads"; S1, ch. 3, "Steve estimates the number of leads he’ll need to attract"] |
| Nonreferral lead burden = required leads minus the share supplied through referrals | Make the referral loop’s contribution explicit instead of assuming it will rescue acquisition economics. | Estimate a reasonable rate for the product’s actual behavior; do not assume viral growth. [S1, ch. 3, "Use your referral assumptions to lessen the burden of customer acquisition"; S1, ch. 3, "Steve tries to save his business model"] |

#### Case anchor: Steve’s Fermi estimate and pricing lever

- **Initial inputs and customer count.** Steve chooses an MSC of annual revenue of $10 million at the recommended horizon, a monthly price of $50, lifetime of four years, and paid conversion of 1%. At $600 annual revenue per customer, the required count is approximately 16,667. Compact evidence: “MSC: $10m ARR in 3 years”; “Pricing (revenue): $50/mo”; “Customer lifetime (retention): 4 years”; “Paid conversion rate…1%.” **Source arithmetic discrepancy:** the active-customer passage prints a $100 monthly denominator while stating $50 monthly pricing and 16,667 customers. The restatement here uses the stated $50 assumption; the printed denominator must not be copied as consistent arithmetic. [S1, ch. 3, "Steve fixes his business model"; S1, ch. 3, "Steve estimates how many active customers he’ll need"]
- **Replacement and leads.** The source’s four-year lifetime corresponds to approximately 2.08% monthly churn. Its rounded arithmetic produces approximately 347 replacement customers and 34,700 leads monthly; a 20% referral contribution supplies 6,940 of those leads but does not fix the model. These are endpoint-maintenance estimates using guessed inputs, not observed demand or a growth plan. Evidence: “2.08% monthly churn rate”; “347 customers/mo”; “34,700 leads/mo”; “20% of that traffic (6,940 leads).” [S1, ch. 3, "Steve estimates his minimum customer acquisition rate"; S1, ch. 3, "Steve estimates the number of leads he’ll need to attract"; S1, ch. 3, "Steve tries to save his business model"]
- **Reprice against customer economics.** Steve considers coding time and developer billing, rather than benchmarking only against free development tools. Evidence: “200 hours to build”; “$50–75/hr”; “20 hours/app versus 200 hours/app.” He hypothesizes that reducing this work could justify “$250/mo” or “$500/mo.” These are value-based pricing hypotheses; the higher price becomes a risky assumption to validate, not a proven willingness to pay. [S1, ch. 3, "Steve fixes his business model"; S1, ch. 3, "Isn’t this just funny math?"]

- **Fix meaningful levers, not the spreadsheet’s appearance.** Revisit pricing, the size or frequency of the problem, and the customer segment; reconsider the MSC if the model still cannot support it. Pricing should sit between the cost of existing alternatives and the monetary value of the UVP. Cost-plus pricing starts from the wrong reference point because customers care about outcomes, not your build cost. [S1, ch. 3, "Revise Your Goal or Fix Your Business Model"; S1, ch. 3, "Revisit your pricing"; S1, ch. 3, "Revisit your problems"; S1, ch. 3, "Consider a different customer segment"; S1, ch. 3, "Revising your goal"]
- **Do not casually solve viability by adding business models.** Moving from self-serve to enterprise can be possible, but requires different channels, propositions, and features. Prefer achieving MSC with a single model when possible. Put the revised input assumptions back on the canvas and prioritize testing the assumptions that make the model work. [S1, ch. 3, "Revising your goal"; S1, ch. 3, "Running a Fermi Estimate on Your Idea"]

### Chapter 4 — Stress Test Your Idea for Feasibility

- **Use a traction roadmap rather than a feature roadmap.** Break the MSC into shorter outcome milestones, then formulate a stage-based rollout. This addresses how to pull off the business model without pretending to know the distant product specification. [S1, ch. 4, "Chapter 4. Stress Test Your Idea for Feasibility"]
- **Model the ramp backward from the endpoint.** The source uses the early hockey-stick portion of an adoption S-curve, not a linear customer ramp. A larger assumed growth rate reduces the early count needed to reach the same fixed endpoint; it does not eliminate the later scaling burden. Evidence for the starting assumption: “set your starting growth rate to 10x/yr for the first 3 years.” Treat this as a planning model to test, not evidence that the product will achieve that growth. [S1, ch. 4, "Charting a Traction Ramp"]
- **Separate starting risks from scaling risks.** Optimizing for users you do not have, hiring senior sales leadership before customers, and raising funds before traction can consume resources while leaving demand unproven. Concentrate on the few actions with the greatest impact now; keep next less detailed and later deliberately fuzzy. [S1, ch. 4, "Formulating a Now-Next-Later Rollout Plan"]

| Horizon / stage | Objective and evidence needed | Actions and source timing |
|---|---|---|
| Now — Problem/Solution Fit | Understand needs and wants; identify the smallest product that delivers value; obtain tangible commitments such as advance payments or letters of intent; make an evidence-based build/no-build decision. | Learn deeply and use Demo-Sell-Build rather than requiring a finished product first. Evidence: “typically takes three to six months.” [S1, ch. 4, "Stage 1: Now—Problem/Solution Fit"] |
| Next — Product/Market Fit | Build and launch the MVP, demonstrate value delivery, and establish business-model repeatability. | Improve through customer feedback; many users are not necessary merely to establish repeatability. Evidence: “typically takes 18–24 months.” [S1, ch. 4, "Stage 2: Next—Product/Market Fit"] |
| Later — Scale | Shift emphasis from getting the product right to growth. | Use optimization experiments and focus on a single growth engine rather than accelerating everything. The book’s declared scope extends only just beyond product/market fit. [S1, ch. 4, "Stage 3: Later—Scale"] |

- **Translate near-term traction into an observable commitment.** Aim as close to making a customer as the relationship allows. If preorders do not fit the product or buyer, use pilots, trials, or lead collection and the Fermi conversion assumptions to estimate their required volume. The later Tesla discussion recommends a tighter initial target than the general stage description: evidence, “problem/solution fit within the recommended three-month time box.” Preserve this distinction rather than turn either passage into a universal deadline. [S1, ch. 4, "Steve Learns About Wizard-of-Oz MVPs"]

The source uses Tesla’s Roadster → Model S → Model 3 rollout to illustrate staged risk reduction. Licensing an existing Lotus design/chassis lets the first car concentrate on the uncertain electric-battery component. Premium pricing and low volume constrain early demand while value delivery can be handled more personally; later cars take on manufacturing and mainstream infrastructure. The Wizard-of-Oz pattern means assembling existing solutions instead of building everything from scratch while still delivering the promised unique value. These are the book’s illustrative claims, not independent historical verification. [S1, ch. 4, "Steve Learns About Wizard-of-Oz MVPs"]

### Chapter 5 — Communicate Your Idea Clearly and Concisely

- **Make the elevator pitch create curiosity, not explain the implementation.** Lead with the desirability story: who needs what outcome, what they normally use, what changed, what fails, and why the new promise matters. Buzzwords and compressed descriptions of the solution assume too much. Source timing evidence: “just 30 seconds to pitch them”; this describes the elevator-pitch situation, not a required duration for every conversation. [S1, ch. 5, "What’s Your Elevator Pitch?"; S1, ch. 5, "Outlining Your Elevator Pitch"]
- **Tailor the follow-up to the audience’s worldview.** Do not force the same solution-centered explanation on everyone. Investor, customer, and advisor conversations have different jobs. Begin with a learning frame, then prioritize the customer pitch before the investor pitch because customers create the traction investors seek. [S1, ch. 5, "The Different Worldviews of an Idea"; S1, ch. 5, "The Advisor Worldview"]

| Audience | Emphasis | How to conduct the conversation |
|---|---|---|
| Investor | Market size, revenue and cost intersection, growth/profitability potential, unfair advantage, and especially traction. | Use actual traction when available; otherwise use the traction roadmap and now-next-later plan as a proxy for communicating the model, not as proof of demand. [S1, ch. 5, "The Investor Worldview"] |
| Customer | Obstacles to the desired outcome, the UVP, and the exchange required. | Earn attention with the UVP, then demonstrate a narrative from the problematic current state to the improved state. Explain monetary payment or the derivative currency relevant to the model. [S1, ch. 5, "The Customer Worldview"] |
| Advisor | Learning and exposing assumptions rather than performing success. | Seek complementary perspectives, share difficulties openly, and use feedback to improve the model rather than seek applause. [S1, ch. 5, "The Advisor Worldview"] |

- **Spend the conversation learning.** Give a concise model overview, then listen, clarify confusion, and note revisions. Evidence: “Spend 20% of the time (5 minutes) delivering your pitch.” Do not obey every prescription; examine whether advice rests on unsupported belief, anecdote, or deep learning, then synthesize it. [S1, ch. 5, "Delivering Your Business Model Pitch"]
- **Keep the deck in stress-test order.** The business-model pitch deck follows desirability → viability → feasibility. State an existing unfair advantage, an advantage story under development, or honestly acknowledge that you are still searching. The extraction names the deck but omits much of the slide-topic content; do not fabricate its detailed slide sequence. [S1, ch. 5, "The 10-Slide Business Model Pitch Deck"; S1, ch. 5, "Viability"]
- **Handoff to Validation: work on the constraint as a team.** A business model is a system whose throughput is limited by its weakest step; spreading a small team across unrelated optimizations does not fix that bottleneck. Use metrics to identify the constraint, then look for the next constraint after improving it. Evidence for the suggested operating rhythm: “allocate 80% of your resources”; “monitor your metrics weekly”; “90-day cycles”; “six 2-week sprints.” Progress is traction velocity, not build velocity. These are the chapter’s transition recommendations, not a detailed substitute for the following Validation chapters. [S1, ch. 5, "Steve Shares His Business Model Pitch with Others"]

## Copy patterns

- **Outcome-led UVP — source-derived adaptation:** “[Help the customer] achieve [desired outcome],” supported by a subheadline explaining why the new way is worth choosing. Evidence: “A good UVP needs to clearly describe what your product is for and who it’s for”; résumé outcome, “landing your dream job.” Do not substitute a feature list for the desired outcome. [S1, ch. 1, "Answer what, who, and why"; S1, ch. 1, "Focus on outcomes"]
- **High-concept shorthand — source-derived adaptation:** “[Familiar product/concept] for [different use/context].” Evidence: “Flickr for video” and “Jaws in space.” Use for rapid explanation and spread when the reference is familiar; the author explicitly distinguishes this from the landing-page UVP. - **Switching story — condensed adaptation:** “[Customer] normally uses [existing alternative] for [job]. They normally use [alternatives], but [switching trigger] exposes [problems], putting [stakes] at risk. Our solution helps them achieve [outcome] through [UVP].” Evidence: “When [customers] encounter a [triggering event]” and “If these problems are left unaddressed, then [what’s at stake].” This is a customer story meant to invite a longer conversation, not a claim that every placeholder has already been validated. ## Ethical boundary

The author explicitly calls for honesty with advisors rather than sharing only good news, and for declaring the absence of an unfair advantage rather than manufacturing one. Apply those guardrails to the design conversation: distinguish an advantage you possess from a story you are working toward. [S1, ch. 5, "The Advisor Worldview"; S1, ch. 5, "Viability"]
