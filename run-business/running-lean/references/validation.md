# Validation

**Core concept:** Validation is the middle part of the ordered Design → Validation → Growth journey: turn the designed business model into a working one by testing its current limiting constraint with customers. Begin with demand and tangible commitments rather than building a finished product; use discovery to improve hypotheses before testing traction. [S1, ch. 6, "Chapter 6. Validate Your Idea Using 90-Day Cycles"; ch. 6, "Testing"; ch. 7, "The Problem/Solution Fit Playbook"]

**Why it works:** A system's weakest link limits throughput; improving other steps prematurely wastes effort. Baseline the customer factory, focus the team on its constraint, investigate causes, and reassess when the constraint moves. External accountability and time boxes prevent endless rationalization; discovery conversations expose causal details that a failed pitch or landing page alone cannot explain. [S1, ch. 6, "Chapter 6. Validate Your Idea Using 90-Day Cycles"; ch. 6, "6. Time-Box Your Experiments"; ch. 7, "Steve Calls a 90-Day Cycle Planning Meeting"]

## Key insights

### Chapter 6 — Validate Your Idea Using 90-Day Cycles

- **Model → Prioritize → Test.** Update the Lean Canvas and traction roadmap, baseline metrics, and align on a traction OKR and constraint at kickoff. Independently develop campaigns, then reconvene to pitch and select bets. Campaigns explain causes, supporting evidence, proposed solutions, and expected outcomes; sprints test them rather than execute supposedly finished plans. [S1, ch. 6, "Modeling"; ch. 6, "Prioritizing"; ch. 6, "Testing"]
- **Cadence, not a guarantee.** Maurya allocates the first two weeks to modeling/prioritizing and the remaining ten to five two-week testing sprints, ending in a cycle review. Evidence: “The first 2 weeks ... are set aside for modeling and prioritizing”; “five 2-week sprints.” These are his operating intervals, not evidence that any business can validate within one cycle. [S1, ch. 6, "A Typical 90-Day Cycle"; ch. 6, "Prioritizing"]
- **Few campaigns, shared mission.** His initial campaign-selection heuristic is team members divided by two, explicitly a starting rule of thumb. Evidence: “dividing the number of team members by two.” Build a small, complete, cross-functional team; avoid departmental local optimization, retain ownership of learning, and balance autonomy with stakeholder accountability. [S1, ch. 6, "Prioritizing"; ch. 6, "Forget traditional departments"; ch. 6, "Good teams are complete"; ch. 6, "Be wary of outsourcing your key skill sets"; ch. 6, "Good teams hold themselves externally accountable"]
- **Discovery before traction.** Generative experiments uncover insights; evaluative experiments test resulting hypotheses. Use D-AARRR-T to ask whether a problem is worth solving, enough people are affected, value is delivered, people return, revenue or another meaningful outcome improves, people refer, and traction rises. Tie campaign results back to customer-factory actions rather than treating learning as an end in itself. [S1, ch. 6, "Testing"]

#### Seven experiment habits

1. **Declare expected outcomes upfront.** Include known contextual factors rather than explaining disappointing results away afterward. [S1, ch. 6, "1. Declare Your Expected Outcomes Upfront"]
2. **Make declarations a team sport.** Estimate independently before comparing reasoning; premature consensus invites groupthink and hierarchy effects. Solo founders should write expectations down. [S1, ch. 6, "2. Make Declaring Outcomes a Team Sport"]
3. **Emphasize estimation, not precision.** Look for analogs, derive expectations from the traction roadmap/customer factory, and use ranges when uncertainty is high. Improve judgment by comparing predictions with outcomes. [S1, ch. 6, "3. Emphasize Estimation, Not Precision"; ch. 6, "Search for analogs"; ch. 6, "Use your traction roadmap and customer factory model"; ch. 6, "Start with ranges instead of absolute predictions"]
4. **Measure actions, not words.** Evaluative outcomes use AARRR actions; discovery asks what customers actually did, not whether a conversation felt positive. [S1, ch. 6, "4. Measure Actions, Not Words"]
5. **Make assumptions falsifiable.** Replace vague leaps of faith with a specific testable action and measurable outcome, whose target comes from the model. [S1, ch. 6, "5. Turn Your Assumptions into Falsifiable Hypotheses"]
6. **Time-box experiments.** Fix the review tripwire; reduce batch size or split an experiment instead of extending it until it appears successful. Maurya's common interval is two weeks. Evidence: “We instituted a two-week time box.” [S1, ch. 6, "6. Time-Box Your Experiments"]
7. **Use a control.** Compare against a prior baseline; serial cohort comparisons can be an initial option with few users or no overlapping experiments. Prefer parallel split testing when appropriate; multiple competing treatments require enough traffic. [S1, ch. 6, "7. Always Use a Control Group"]

### Chapter 7 — Kick Off Your First 90-Day Cycle

- **Align before brainstorming.** Kickoff establishes models, goals, assumptions, and constraints; independent campaign proposals come afterward. Before the customer factory is running, acquisition/demand generation is the initial constraint—not polishing downstream product steps. [S1, ch. 7, "Steve Calls a 90-Day Cycle Kickoff Meeting"]
- **Sell a promise of better.** An offer combines UVP, demo, and CTA: earn attention, demonstrate that the promise is credible with the least necessary demonstration, then seek the closest practical action to payment. Complex B2B sales may require multiple parties and buyer access; technical risk may justify a working prototype, but do not assume one is needed. [S1, ch. 7, "Customers Don’t Buy Products, They Buy a Promise of Something Better"]
- **Mafia offer campaign sequence:** problem discovery studies current alternatives without pitching; solution design targets a struggle sufficient to cause a switch; offer delivery tests the assembled pitch and seeks commitments. High-touch learning precedes scaling rather than being replaced by a cold pitch or landing-page optimization. [S1, ch. 7, "1. Problem discovery"; ch. 7, "2. Solution design"; ch. 7, "3. Offer delivery"; ch. 7, "Steve Calls a 90-Day Cycle Planning Meeting"]
- **Campaign allocation is adjustable.** Reserve up to two discovery sprints, one design sprint, and up to two delivery sprints. Evidence: “up to two sprints for problem discovery, one sprint for solution design, and up to two sprints for offer delivery”; the text immediately calls these “only guidelines.” Adapt to the product and segment. [S1, ch. 7, "Running a Mafia Offer Campaign"]
- **Problem/solution fit is not applause.** Demonstrate repeatable attraction, activation, and switching; secure tangible commitments sufficient for the traction roadmap; define the smallest MVP that will deliver value. Neither a survey ranking nor a generic commitment count substitutes for these criteria. Surveys can later verify interview-derived findings quantitatively, but discovery needs open-ended exploration. [S1, ch. 7, "When Are You Done with Problem/Solution Fit?"; ch. 7, "Mary Bursts Steve’s Bubble (Again)"; ch. 7, "Are Surveys Good for Anything?"]

### Chapter 8 — Understand Your Customers Better Than They Do

- **Discover struggles through real behavior.** Do not ask for a list of “problems.” Study existing alternatives, workarounds, friction, and gaps between desired and actual outcomes. Find the larger job beyond your product's immediate functional benefit; stop expanding when the scope exceeds viable economics or feasible competencies. [S1, ch. 8, "The Problem with Problems"; ch. 8, "Scoping the Bigger Context"; ch. 8, "Diving Deeper into a Bigger, More Specific Context"]
- **Broad-match, then narrow-match.** First interview recent buyers/users of alternatives; cluster their stories, then verify emerging early-adopter criteria with narrower interviews. Maurya's planning heuristic is twenty to thirty interviews across four weeks, not a representative-sample requirement. Evidence: “interview 20–30 people over a 4-week period.” Stop based on recurring, predictable stories rather than merely reaching the count. [S1, ch. 8, "Broad-Match Versus Narrow-Match Problem Discovery Sprints"; ch. 8, "When Are You Done with Problem Discovery?"]
- **Prospect in a learning frame.** Start with recent use or triggering events and seek warm introductions. Say you seek advice rather than pitching; scheduling can be delegated, but founders retain the learning. Ask short, open-ended follow-ups about actual past behavior rather than hypothetical future purchases. [S1, ch. 8, "Finding Prospects"; ch. 8, "Conducting Interviews"]
- **Interview meta-script, not interrogation:** welcome and establish the learning frame → anchor on selection/purchase or last use → work backward to first thought and switching trigger → explore selection, alternatives, channel, and price → first use → recurring use → whether the desired outcome was achieved and what comes next → permission to follow up and referrals. Follow the story, clarify vague terms, and slow down causal moments rather than mechanically reading questions. [S1, ch. 8, "Conducting Interviews"]
- **Reconstruct switch stories.** After each interview, use a Customer Forces Canvas to record PUSH (changed situation and consequences of doing nothing), PULL (chosen solution, channel, price), INERTIA (old habits/solutions), FRICTION (anxiety and usage obstacles), and WHAT'S NEXT. Restore chronological causality even when the conversation was nonlinear; group similar triggers, desired outcomes, and alternatives. This counters confirmation and recency biases. [S1, ch. 8, "Capturing Insights"]
- **Discovery exit:** review story clusters for repeated patterns and a big enough problem worth solving before solution design. The extracted text announces two customer/problem-fit questions but does not contain them; do not fabricate their wording. [S1, ch. 8, "When Are You Done with Problem Discovery?"]

### Chapter 9 — Design Your Solution to Cause a Switch

- **Start with a clean slate.** Design the smallest solution that causes switching, not a bundle of everything discovered. Balance desirability, viability, and feasibility iteratively; the lenses can conflict. [S1, ch. 9, "Chapter 9. Design Your Solution to Cause a Switch"; ch. 9, "Running a Solution Design Sprint"]
- **Problem and Promise—desirability.** Identify primary struggle in an unmet outcome, usage friction, or selection friction/nonconsumption. Choose the smallest problem subset that still makes the UVP materially better than alternatives; select differentiating attributes grounded in that struggle. Maurya's magnitude guidance is directional, not a universal performance cutoff. Evidence: “you need to be 3x–10x better.” [S1, ch. 9, "Addressing Desirability"; ch. 9, "Step 1: Identify the primary struggle"; ch. 9, "Step 2: Craft a compelling promise"]
- **Price and People—viability.** Reapply MSC/Fermi constraints and target annual ARPU to story clusters. Set fair rather than supposedly optimal pricing, anchored to alternatives and the UVP. Early-adopter targeting is often about *when*: seek a switching trigger plus action, then identify a reachable entry point before, during, or after the struggle. Aspirations with no action are insufficient. [S1, ch. 9, "Addressing Viability"; ch. 9, "Step 1: Set a fair price"; ch. 9, "Step 2: Identify your ideal early adopters"]
- **Packaging—feasibility.** Consider manual/service delivery, a smaller starting subsegment, and just-in-time scope instead of assuming end-user automation is necessary. A Concierge MVP delivers the outcome as a service while progressively replacing manual work with a scalable product; it is not traditional consulting as the final destination. [S1, ch. 9, "Addressing Feasibility"; ch. 9, "Steve Learns About the Concierge MVP"]
- **Delivery horizon is contextual.** Maurya proposes building and launching within two months *after* achieving problem/solution fit, because prospects may move to alternatives while waiting. Evidence: “two months ... from the point at which you achieve problem/solution fit.” Treat this as his design constraint, not permission to ignore actual delivery or regulatory requirements. [S1, ch. 9, "Addressing Feasibility"]
- **5 Ps review:** explicitly describe Problem, Promise, Price, People, and Packaging before pitching. These respectively connect the struggle, better outcome, workable economics, entry-point early adopters, and a deliverable form of the MVP. The extracted checklist questions following this heading are absent; the definitions and preceding design process are available. [S1, ch. 9, "The 5 P’s of MVP"; ch. 9, "Addressing Desirability"; ch. 9, "Addressing Viability"; ch. 9, "Addressing Feasibility"]

### Chapter 10 — Deliver a Mafia Offer Your Customers Cannot Refuse

- **Characters:** the early adopter is the hero; existing alternatives are the competition/villain; the founder is the guide and the product is the gift. Naming competition lets you frame the switching and pricing comparison instead of leaving it to happen without you. [S1, ch. 10, "Define the Characters in Your Customer Story Pitch"; ch. 10, "Running an Offer Delivery Sprint"]
- **Act 1—Setup:** establish the larger job/context, relevant external change, stakes of staying put, and the better outcome/superpower. Do not open by attacking the prospect's competence or current process. [S1, ch. 10, "Act 1: Setup (share the bigger context)"]
- **Act 2—Confrontation:** name the alternative you seek to displace and explain its familiar struggles and deeper obstacles to the desired outcome. Summarize why it falls short in the changed context. [S1, ch. 10, "Act 2: Confrontation (break the old way)"]
- **Act 3—Resolution:** demonstrate a before/after customer narrative that makes the UVP believable, not a training tour. Select the least costly sufficiently convincing format: verbal demonstration before mockups/prototypes for digital products; verbal before sketches/CAD and prototypes for physical products; verbal before a process diagram or sample deliverable for services. [S1, ch. 10, "Act 3: Resolution (demo your new, better way)"]
- **Act 4—CTA:** ask for a specific switching action, not verbal approval. Frame a staged release as early access, discuss pricing upfront even for a free trial/pilot, and explain fair pricing through alternatives and promised value. If the buyer is absent, request an introduction that costs social capital. [S1, ch. 10, "Act 4: Call-to-action (ask for the switch)"]
- **Deliver as a test.** Qualify a mix of old and new prospects, pause between acts, and investigate nonresonance instead of forcing the pitch. After the demo, ensure perceived value before progressing to pricing. Explore the reason and usage behind feature requests without automatically adding them to MVP scope. Record pricing objections, hesitation, follow-up dates, and actual next actions. [S1, ch. 10, "Delivering Your Offer"]
- **Thresholds have denominators.** Maurya recommends iterative delivery toward a qualified-lead-to-specific-CTA conversion of sixty to eighty percent, commonly across two sprints. Evidence: “60–80% conversion rate from qualified leads to your specific customer call-to-action.” Chapter 7 instead describes qualified leads to *paying customers*: “60–80% ... from qualified leads to paying customers.” Preserve the actual CTA and denominator in reports; neither formulation establishes a universal benchmark or statistical guarantee. The roadmap, not the percentage alone, sets sufficient commitments. [S1, ch. 10, "Running an Offer Delivery Sprint"; ch. 7, "When to Use a Mafia Offer Campaign"; ch. 7, "3. Offer delivery"]
- **Prelaunch customer factory:** measure new prospects (acquisition), demo calls booked (activation), post-demo follow-ups in complex sales (retention), accepted offers (revenue), and referred leads (referral). These are prelaunch operational definitions—not proof that booked calls are delivered product value or accepted offers are cash received. Manual measurement is acceptable initially. [S1, ch. 10, "Measure Your Customer Factory Metrics Weekly"]
- **Optimize the constraint.** Work backward from the CTA: waiting signals long cycle times, leaving signals abandonment. Diagnose before prescribing; waiting may suggest capacity/scheduling, leaving may suggest promise, price, or process objections. Test remedies in subsequent offers. End delivery when roadmap fit criteria are reached or cycle time expires, then review. [S1, ch. 10, "Identify Your Key Constraint"; ch. 10, "Formulate Ways of Breaking the Constraint"; ch. 10, "When Are You Done with Offer Delivery?"]

### Chapter 11 — Run a 90-Day Cycle Review

- **Review regardless of results.** Decide what comes next from both traction and learning, not founder anxiety or a wish to extend timelines. Success means a repeatable customer factory, not isolated signups. [S1, ch. 11, "Chapter 11. Run a 90-Day Cycle Review"; ch. 11, "Steve Calls a Pre-Review Meeting Just with Mary"]
- **Update without erasing history.** Collect each variant's current elevator pitch, Lean Canvas, and traction roadmap. Compare prior/current canvases, preserve the old roadmap before updating changed Fermi inputs, retain the MSC unless deliberately reconsidered with team/stakeholder input, and overlay actual traction. [S1, ch. 11, "Collect/Update Artifacts"; ch. 11, "Elevator pitch"; ch. 11, "Lean Canvas"; ch. 11, "Traction roadmap"]
- **Progress report sequence:** set context → what we thought → what we did → what we learned → what's next. Support discussion with experiment details, Customer Forces Canvases, and metrics. Seek alignment on the current constraint and next-cycle direction, not a campaign brainstorming session. The review supplies next-cycle alignment; schedule planning rather than another kickoff. [S1, ch. 11, "Assemble a Progress Report Pitch Deck"; ch. 11, "Set context"; ch. 11, "What we thought"; ch. 11, "What we did"; ch. 11, "What we learned"; ch. 11, "What’s next"; ch. 11, "Running the Meeting"]
- **3P decision:** persevere when traction meets/exceeds the goal; pivot when a missed goal is paired with evidence of a potentially corrective change; pause when resources are exhausted or evidence indicates a dead end. A pivot without learning is simply trying things at random. Keep the customer factory running while transitioning toward MVP delivery and more scalable campaigns. [S1, ch. 11, "What’s next"; ch. 11, "Steve Calls a 90-Day Cycle Review Meeting"]

## Copy patterns

- **Falsifiable experiment:** `[Specific testable action] will drive [expected measurable outcome] within [time box].` Evidence: “[Specific testable action] will drive [expected measurable outcome]”; time-box extension supported by “in two weeks.” Substitute a model-derived target and a declared interval, not the book's illustrative signup target. [S1, ch. 6, "5. Turn Your Assumptions into Falsifiable Hypotheses"; ch. 6, "6. Time-Box Your Experiments"]
- **Discovery invitation:** `I'd like to understand your current workflow for [job]. I'm not selling anything; I'm looking for advice.` Evidence: “I’m not selling anything, just looking for advice.” Use for an actual learning conversation rather than disguising a sales pitch. [S1, ch. 8, "Finding Prospects"]
- **Timeline follow-up:** `When did you last use [existing alternative]? What prompted the purchase? What happened next?` Evidence: “When did you last use [existing alternative]?” and “What did you do next?” These are starting prompts, not a rigid questionnaire. [S1, ch. 8, "Conducting Interviews"]
- **Switch-story summary:** `When [trigger] occurred, [expectation] failed; the customer considered [alternatives] and chose [solution] because [pull/UVP]. [Inertia] resisted the switch; [friction] complicated use; now [next summit].` Evidence: “they had an expectation violation” and “Where they are now (Next Summit).” Fill with observed events, not assumptions that justify the product. [S1, ch. 8, "Capturing Insights"]
- **Early-adopter entry point:** `[Customer segment] who started using [existing alternative] [elapsed time] ago`, or `[Customer segment] who experienced [switching trigger] [elapsed time] ago.` Evidence: the source uses “[x weeks] ago” and “[x days] ago” respectively; these are placeholders to discover, not prescribed waiting periods. [S1, ch. 9, "Step 2: Identify your ideal early adopters"]
- **Price rationale:** `Customers spend [amount] on [alternative] for [current outcome]. We deliver [better outcome/value]; our pricing is [model].` Evidence: “Most people spend $X on existing alternatives and achieve [current outcome].” Ground every comparison in the research and actual offer, rather than copying a high anchor or claiming untested savings. [S1, ch. 10, "Delivering Your Offer"]

## Ethical boundary

The mafia offer earns acceptance through fit, not coercion: “not because you strong-arm them.” Maurya explicitly says not to convince customers they have a problem if they do not; disqualify poor-fit prospects and investigate a pitch that does not resonate rather than brute-forcing it. Share objective progress with stakeholders instead of selective good news or “success theater.” [S1, ch. 7, "The Mafia Offer Campaign"; ch. 10, "Act 4: Call-to-action (ask for the switch)"; ch. 10, "Delivering Your Offer"; ch. 11, "Running the Meeting"]

## Case anchors

### Case anchor: Book launch

Maurya's Running Lean launch illustrates sequencing: personal offer learning informed a teaser page, followed by campaigns that supported incremental writing and sales. Its reported outcome belongs to that launch, not to every author or campaign. [S1, ch. 7, "When to Use a Mafia Offer Campaign"]

### Case anchor: Homebuyer discovery

The builder traced a purchase backward to a holiday hosting event and used the resulting journey questions for localized content advertising. The lesson is causal trigger discovery, not a universal best advertising season. [S1, ch. 8, "Case Study: Using Problem Discovery Interviews to Drive New Home Sales"]

### Case anchor: Drill bits

The drill-bit scenario contrasts a stronger tool with a way to hang a painting without drilling. It is presented as an illustrative scenario; use it to challenge product-context assumptions, not as measured commercial evidence. [S1, ch. 8, "Case Study: Using Problem Discovery Interviews to Build Better Drill Bits"]

### Case anchor: Altverse

Steve's narrative moves from software developers to home construction, discovers homeowner/architect differences, uses Concierge packaging, and disqualifies firms not using the displaced rendering alternative. Later pitches expose the larger client-education job and change the value/pricing anchor. This is the book's teaching narrative, not independent proof of market demand for AR/VR. [S1, ch. 8, "Steve Reviews the Results of the Broad-Match Problem Discovery Sprint"; ch. 9, "Steve Learns About the Concierge MVP"; ch. 10, "Steve Meets with the Team to Review the Results of Their First Offer Delivery Sprint"; ch. 11, "Steve Calls a 90-Day Cycle Review Meeting"]

### Case anchor: iPad

Maurya reads the iPad keynote as a job-based switching story: familiar alternatives, their shortcomings, a contrasting demo, and alternative-anchored pricing. This is his analysis of a public launch, not a controlled test of the pitch's effectiveness. [S1, ch. 10, "Case Study: The iPad Mafia Offer"]
