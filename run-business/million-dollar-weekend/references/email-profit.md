# Email Is for Profit — Million Dollar Weekend, Ch 7

Deep reference for Chapter 7 ("...Email Is for Profit: Use Email to Make a F*&k-Ton of Money") of Noah Kagan, *Million Dollar Weekend* (Penguin Random House, 2024). PDF pp. ~108–120 in the libgen edition.

The chapter argues a single thesis: **the email list is the only audience you actually own, and putting personality into it is the multiplier**. Everything else — landing pages, the first-100 playbook, the 3-email autoresponder, the Law of 100 — is plumbing to make that thesis real.

---

## 1. Why email beats everything (the six reasons)

Kagan opens with a forced choice: "Which of these options is the most valuable for your business? A. 100 email subscribers / B. 1,000 YouTube subscribers / C. 10,000 Instagram followers." Answer: **A**. "Email is the king and queen of communicating with customers." (Ch 7, PDF p. 111)

His six reasons, verbatim in spirit:

1. "My company AppSumo generates **$65 million a year** in total transactions. And you know what? **Nearly 50 percent of that comes from email**. This percentage has been consistent for **more than ten years**." (Ch 7, PDF p. 111)
2. "I have 120,000 Twitter followers, 750,000 YouTube subscribers, and 150,000 TikTok fans — **and I would give them all up for my 100,000 email subscribers**. Why? Every time I send an email, 40,000 people open it and consume my content. I'm not hoping the platform gods will allow me to reach them." (Ch 7, PDF p. 111)
3. Email isn't dead. "Email is used obsessively by over **4 billion people**! ... **Eighty-nine percent of people check it EVERY DAY!**" (Ch 7, PDF p. 112)
4. Algorithm risk is real. "Remember the digital publisher LittleThings? Yeah, no one else does, either. They closed after they lost **75 percent of their 20,000,000 monthly visitors** when Facebook changed its algorithm in 2018. CEO Joe Speiser says it killed his business and he lost **$100 million**." (Ch 7, PDF p. 112)
5. **You own it.** "You own your email list. Forever. **If AppSumo shuts down tomorrow, my insurance policy, my sweet sweet baby, my beloved, my email list comes with me** and makes anything I do after so much easier. Because it's mine." (Ch 7, PDF p. 112)
6. Cheap to grow and cheap to send, "whereas Facebook or Google ads consistently cost money." (Ch 7, PDF p. 112)

Kagan's regret framing: "The number one regret of just about every entrepreneur I know is this: '**I wish I started my email list sooner.**' Don't be that person." (Ch 7, PDF p. 112)

## 2. Quality > quantity — the Charlie Hoehn cautionary tale

Sheer size is a vanity metric. The cautionary tale: "One of the very first clients of my great friend Charlie Hoehn (he's worked for Tim Ferriss, Ramit Sethi, Tucker Max) was a real estate mogul who **bought an email list of 1 million subscribers** to promote his book. The list was made up of people who'd signed up for discounts at retail and restaurant chains — and had nothing whatsoever to do with the mogul. Charlie emailed them a sequence of five emails, **AND FEWER THAN 100 PEOPLE OPENED THE MESSAGES**. It's about quality, not quantity." (Ch 7, PDF p. 112)

**The target metric: "A healthy email list has a 20 percent open rate. Target that."** (Ch 7, PDF p. 112)

"Having a bond that leads people to open your emails — not the size of the list — is where the power of email lies."

## 3. Personality > utility — the Kernest/Garamond/boner teardown

This is the chapter's origin story and its biggest lesson: **emails that sell with personality earn ~100x what utility emails earn.**

Before: Emails written by Kagan and a 17-year-old Bulgarian named Nikola who "didn't speak English very well." Average: **~$100 per email. Best ever: $1,000.** Typical Nikola email: "There's a deal on this product and you get to save $1,000!" — buy button — done. "It was just 'Go buy!' — not much more than that." (Ch 7, PDF p. 108)

Then copywriter friend Neville Medhora pushed to rewrite an email for an app called Kernest (fonts — "a subject I know nothing about"). Neville's draft opened with a line Kagan "will never forget":

> "If you get a boner when I whisper the word 'Garamond' into your ear . . . you might be interested." (Ch 7, PDF p. 108)

The final shipped subject (corporate obscenity filters made them strip "boner"): **"Steve Jobs was originally obsessed with typography"** — the body told a long Jobs-loves-Helvetica story, joked about Verdana's curves turning designers on, and only *then* pitched a lifetime Kernest membership with a 48-hour countdown. (Ch 7, PDF p. 109)

**Result: "$9,563 in profit in twenty-four hours! By putting personality into the email, we made nearly a hundred times more money!"** (Ch 7, PDF p. 109)

Kagan's takeaway: "The actual monetary offer wasn't any better than the offers I put in my earlier emails. **The big difference was the copy. It was an authentic person on the page, struggling, telling jokes, laughing, and teaching.** ... Sales have repeatedly been shown to go UP when the people selling are enjoying themselves." (Ch 7, PDF pp. 109–110)

The meta-lesson — and one of the most personally pointed lines in the book: "Somewhere along the way, though, I came to believe that the audience-building, excitable nerd me and the business me had to be separate. ... **Like I had amnesia.** Neville's email changed all of that. It gave me permission to make who I am a part of how I market and sell." (Ch 7, PDF p. 110)

## 4. Set up a landing page

Audience needs somewhere to actually opt in. "The way companies, marketers, entrepreneurs, and content creators do this is by sending their audiences to a landing page." (Ch 7, PDF p. 113)

Example: Monthly1K student Julien Marion's *Sleep Sumo*. Headline: **"I sleep better than you."** Sub: "Get my 1 free tip a week to improve your sleep." Email box. Yes-please button. That's the entire page. (Ch 7, PDF p. 113)

Minimum viable spec: "an image, a few words, and a box where people can input their email address... All you need your website to do is to communicate a value proposition and provide a way to capture emails." (Ch 7, PDF p. 113)

**Tools mentioned:** SendFox.com (Kagan helped build it), Mailchimp.com, ConvertKit.com, Webflow.com. For tracking clicks from sigs/bios: Bitly.com or Linktree.com. (Ch 7, PDF pp. 113–114, 115)

## 5. Getting your first 100 subscribers — 3 phases, verbatim templates

### Phase 1: 0 → 10 — The Dream Ten

"What's the easiest way to start building your list? Use your existing network. **Yes, your Dream Ten.** These are people who know you and care about you. ... My mom, my brother, and my other close friends are on my mailing list. Always look at what assets and networks you have available before you reach out to randoms." (Ch 7, PDF p. 114)

Template Bryan Harris (Videofruit) used to get to 10,000+ subs:

```
Hey [name]!
I just wanted to let you know that I'm starting [description of your new business].
I'm going to publish [one article per week/a weekly tip] on how to [subject].
Is this something you are interested in?
Here's an easy way to sign up! [Insert landing page address] Or you can just write back with "Yes, dude, I'd like to," and I'll do it for you!
Hope things are great!
[Your name]
```
(Ch 7, PDF p. 114)

Key move: "Then, if they respond with a yes — which they most likely will, because you know them — **put their email on your subscriber list.**" (Ch 7, PDF p. 114)

### Phase 2: 11 → 50 — Lazy Marketing

Publicize the landing page in "every point of contact you have with others":

- Email signature
- Biography on Twitter, LinkedIn, TikTok, Instagram, and Facebook

(Ch 7, PDF p. 114)

Why this is leverage: "On average, a person sends *around 40 emails per day*. That means that every day you have a chance to place your new landing page address in at least 40 emails. **That's 40 lottery tickets with above-average chances of winning!**" (Ch 7, PDF p. 115)

Make the sig fun, like Kagan's: "P.S. [icon] Join my newsletter to receive actionable content marketing tips weekly. (No spam ever! I promise)" (Ch 7, PDF p. 115)

### Phase 3: 51 → 100 — Post in Your Places + targeted referrals

"Post a modified version of that email above in Facebook, Snapchat, Twitter, Reddit groups — or wherever you're active":

```
Hey Everyone
Starting a "weekly" newsletter about [subject].
Go to website.com to join the newsletter.
```
(Ch 7, PDF p. 116)

Then targeted referrals: "Ask your family and friends to refer one *specific* person who they think would like your newsletter. **The more specific you are when you describe the person, the more likely you are to make it easier for them to do it.** Take what I did for Sumo Jerky: 'Do you know someone who makes purchase decisions at an office — and likes a good laugh?'" (Ch 7, PDF p. 116)

## 6. Growing beyond 100 — Chris Von Wilpert / HubSpot teardown

Chris Von Wilpert wanted to build a content marketing agency. Strategy:

1. "Write an insanely detailed blog post dissecting the success of and drawing lessons from HubSpot's content marketing approach. He spent **forty hours** working on this."
2. "Use social media and pretty much everything else to get that post in front of his ideal customers."
3. "Put a call to action (CTA) at the end of the post telling readers to sign up to receive a download of growth hacks he'd put together."

Result: "5,000 people had viewed his blog post... within two weeks, his email list grew from **0 to over 1,000 subscribers**!" (Ch 7, PDF p. 116)

Kagan DM'd him: "hey man. LOVED the hubspot analysis. want to do one and ill promote it to 300k+ people on okdork?" That turned into a **$100,000 payday** — Kagan paid Chris to live in Austin for six months and run content marketing for AppSumo. (Ch 7, PDF p. 117)

## 7. Lead Magnets

"Using a Lead Magnet gives people an incentive to join your email list vs. just asking people to sign up." (Ch 7, PDF p. 117)

Four examples Kagan uses:

- A checklist that can be used to properly perform something explained in a video
- A template for determining, say, a business's profit margin
- An advanced guide that goes further into the details of a subject of one of his videos
- **"A unique book that provides substantial value but is offered for free. For me, it is *11 Side Hustle Ideas to Make $500/Day from Your Phone*."** (Ch 7, PDF pp. 117–118)

Other shapes by niche:
- DIY carpenter → plans to make a corner table
- Marketing YouTuber → scripts of what to say on sales phone calls
- Landscaping expert → recommendations for which kinds of grass to use around the US

Cases:
- **Nick True / Mapped Out Money** (YNAB tutorials) — "highest sign-up rates when he offers a checklist that relates to the video."
- **Jess Dante / Love and London** — free *London 101 Guide*, "**downloaded more than 45,000 times.**" (Ch 7, PDF p. 118)

Two-hour rule for the challenge: "Don't spend more than two hours on the first iteration. If you want to turn it into a big thing later on, great. But start SMALL." (Ch 7, PDF p. 118)

## 8. Set up your Cash Register — the 3-email autoresponder (verbatim)

"At the exact moment your customer is interacting with or just found your business, they are MOST excited about it. So that's when you want to funnel them into other experiences with you instead of letting them go. Because they WANT YOU." (Ch 7, PDF p. 119)

Don't wait a week — autoresponder hits immediately. SendFox.com recommended; ConvertKit.com and Mailchimp.com also work. (Ch 7, PDF p. 119)

### Email 1 — Welcome

```
SUBJECT: You're awesome
Thanks for joining OkDork. You are awesome!
Over 17 years of working online I've learned some things:
 - #30 at Facebook and helped launch mobile, status updates, and more
 - #4 at Mint and led growth to 1 million users within 1 year
 - Started AppSumo, which is now an $85M/year business
And now, I want to help you on your journey to living the life you want.
What could I write to provide value to you?
Love you,
Noah "tacos" Kagan
```
(Ch 7, PDF p. 119)

The whole point of the Welcome is the one question. "Remember, this welcome will arrive at the moment they are most willing to participate in your business. That's why every time someone joins my list, I ask one question in my Welcome Email: '**What could I write to provide value to you?**' In that way, you'll get tons of content ideas and know exactly what your subscribers want." (Ch 7, PDF p. 120)

### Email 2 — Connection

```
SUBJECT: Connect with me on LinkedIn
Howdy amigo,
Send me a connection request on LinkedIn to help share our relationships, see behind-the-scenes thoughts on marketing, startups, and more . . .
Hugs,
Noah
```
(Ch 7, PDF p. 119)

"You're explicitly asking them to connect with you on social media, by following you on Instagram, LinkedIn, Facebook, Twitter, and so on." (Ch 7, PDF p. 120)

### Email 3 — Content

```
SUBJECT: Starting an 8-figure business with $50
I started AppSumo in March 2010.
In one weekend, with $50, I launched version 1 of the site. It was simple.
12 years later, Sumo Group has grown into an 8-figure business.
Starting a business can be hard. But I want to show you an easier way:
Here's how I built AppSumo.com for $50.
Enjoy,
Noah
```
(Ch 7, PDF p. 120)

**Critical sequencing rule — send your BEST stuff first:** "I always advise sending your best *Content Email* (free course, best articles or videos, content most useful for your audience, etc.) in the beginning. The reason is simple. **For each subscriber, open rates usually start high, then decline after a few emails. So show subscribers your best work to minimize that decline.**" (Ch 7, PDF p. 120)

## 9. One-by-one early on

The non-scalable move that compounds: "**A MAJOR thing here is one-by-one marketing. This is personally engaging with each new subscriber.** When you're starting out, *every single person matters*. Frankly, everyone in your audience matters forever, but *especially* at the beginning, you should respond to every single new subscriber. **I STILL do this for nearly every single email and did for most of my YouTube comments.**" (Ch 7, PDF p. 120)

## 10. The Law of 100 (preview — see dedicated reference)

Chapter 7 closes with the Law of 100, which gets its own deep treatment elsewhere. Headline: do 100 reps of whatever the action is (emails, videos, customer pitches, days of practice) **before you even THINK of stopping.** (Ch 7, PDF p. 121)

Kagan's own cautionary tale: *Noah Kagan Presents* podcast, 2018. **~50 episodes, ~30,000 downloads each. He quit.** "(a) if I was getting 30,000 downloads today, it would be a top podcast; and (b) since I've restarted and committed, I'm at 7,500 downloads an episode. A painful but valuable lesson." (Ch 7, PDF pp. 121–122)

Contrast: the Buffer guys, who he doubted in 2010 comments on his blog — "**now and their business is doing $20 million in recurring revenue.** ... So what's the difference between my podcast and Buffer? **They stuck with it. And I didn't.**" (Ch 7, PDF p. 121)

Backed by Jerry Uelsmann's University of Florida photography study: Quantity group (100 photos for an A) "**kicked the Quality group's ass — in terms of quality**" because they "experimented more." (Ch 7, PDF p. 121)

The mindset shift: "**Lean in and commit to 100 reps. (Think of this as doing reps and practicing as opposed to failing or succeeding.) This changes your mindset and makes it much easier to sustain forward motion when things get tough.**" (Ch 7, PDF p. 122)

Applied:
- YouTube channel → publish 100 videos
- Newsletter → write 100 emails
- New hobby (chess, guitar) → practice 100 days
- New business → directly pitch 100 customers

(Ch 7, PDF p. 122)
