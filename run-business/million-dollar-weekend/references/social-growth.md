# Social Media Is for Growth — Ch 6 Deep Reference

Source: Noah Kagan with Tahl Raz, *Million Dollar Weekend* (Penguin Random House, 2024), Ch 6 "Social Media Is for Growth . . . Build an Audience Who Will Support You for Life," PDF pp. 92-108. Part 3 framing on PDF p. 91. Validation handoff from Ch 5 on PDF p. 90.

The chapter sits at the start of Part 3 ("Grow It — Make Money While You Sleep"). Part 3 promises to "create the growth machine to turn your first customers into a community of fans" (PDF p. 91). Ch 6 is the audience-creation step; Ch 7 ("Email Is for Profit") is the monetization step. You build on rented land (social) and then move them to owned land (email).

---

## 1. The thesis: 100 true fans, not 10,000 followers

Kagan's opener is a charity story used to prove a counterintuitive metric: depth of relationship beats raw follower count.

He launched a fundraiser for Bo Jackson's "Bo Bikes Bama" (annual charity ride for Alabama disaster relief that Bo started in 2012). He posted gift tiers to his audience — Amigo $10, Homie $50, Sidekick $100, Hermano $500, Inner Circle $1,000, BFF $10,000 (taco in Mexico City) (Ch 6, PDF p. 93).

> "In three days we raised $30,000 from my audience in small donations, all without going viral... here's the remarkable thing: when I went down the list of 102 names of people who donated to Bo Bikes Bama, I recognized nearly every name! I had interacted with these people. I'd given them business advice." (Ch 6, PDF p. 94)

He then names the principle and credits its lineage:

> "These weren't followers or an anonymous audience. They were what marketing guru Seth Godin calls your 'smallest viable audience' or what *Wired* magazine cofounder Kevin Kelly has called '1,000 true fans'—all built by connecting with people whose particular challenges and interests overlapped with my particular skills and passions." (Ch 6, PDF p. 94)

The math conclusion is explicit:

> "The lifetime value, not to mention the lifelong joy, generated by a community of 100 high-value, attentive fans who know, like, and trust you will dwarf whatever short-term satisfaction you may get from having 10,000 low-value, inattentive followers." (Ch 6, PDF p. 94)

**Implication for the skill:** the target for Ch 6 is 100 engaged fans in ~30 days (Part 3 framing on PDF p. 91 says "inner circle of a hundred true fans... in thirty days"). Don't optimize for follower count.

## 2. Generosity over selling

The Bo Bikes Bama ask worked because Kagan had banked years of generosity first.

> "A community who already knows you, who follows you, who is rooting for you is one of the most powerful forces in business, and it's created through generosity. Adding value without expectation. Helping them with their journey without asking for an immediate return. Sometimes it's helping them by boosting their self-esteem with a simple compliment." (Ch 6, PDF p. 94)

> "I've spent twenty years giving out free content to people through OkDork and AppSumo. So when I finally said, 'Hey, I'm raising money for charity. Do you guys want to contribute?' it was easy for me to ask, and saying yes was a no-brainer for my community. It takes time to build a real audience." (Ch 6, PDF p. 94)

He started OkDork.com in 2000 to document his journey for high school and college friends — the early posts were "all over the place—marketing, pictures of my stuffed frog Seymour, things happening in college" (Ch 6, PDF p. 94). The audience compounded because he kept showing up in public.

> "People get hooked on CHARACTERS. People do business with REAL PEOPLE. Especially those who feel like a friend." (Ch 6, PDF p. 95)

Building in public also generated meeting his "marketing idol" Seth Godin (who responded to one of Kagan's blog posts) and landed him the Mint job (Ch 6, PDF p. 95). Name-drop list of people he met by putting himself out there: Tim Ferriss, Andrew Chen, Mike Posner, Bo Jackson, James Clear, Ryan Holiday, Blake Ross, Ramit Sethi, Tahl Raz (Ch 6, PDF p. 95).

## 3. Finding your Unique Angle — Ben Kenyon's 4-sentence template

Ben Kenyon is head strength and conditioning coach for the Philadelphia 76ers and CEO/founder of Great Day Squad (Ch 6, PDF p. 95). He came to Kagan for newsletter advice. Kagan asked one question:

> "What's your unique angle in thirty seconds or less?" (Ch 6, PDF p. 97)

Ben's answer, after struggling:

> "I've been a performance coach for the last fourteen years, working with the best athletes in the world. Helping people perform better is my groove. I want to help anybody who wants to have a great day and shift them into the mentality to dominate their life. I have information to share when it comes to dealing with the best." (Ch 6, PDF p. 97)

Kagan dissects it into a reusable 4-part formula (Ch 6, PDF p. 97):

1. **Who you are**
2. **Why people should trust you**
3. **What you're passionate about**
4. **What unique thing this prepares you to do for people**

> "The first three sentences define what makes him special (fourteen years helping the best athletes in the world perform better!), and the fourth (how he's solving his customers' problems, teaching mindsets needed to dominate life) defines the kind of love and attention he'll generously dispense to cultivate a community." (Ch 6, PDF p. 97)

**Challenge** (Ch 6, PDF p. 97): write a journal pitch describing your special sauce. The challenge prompts are: (1) Who are you? (2) Why should people listen? (3) What are you passionate about? (4) What will you do for people?

The framing word Kagan uses throughout is **unfair advantage** (Ch 6, PDF p. 95) — the special sauce that will make you shine.

## 4. Pick a Platform — three filters

> "With your unique angle, you need to reach an audience, and the best way to do it for FREE is through social media." (Ch 6, PDF p. 98)

**Shortlist by profession** (Ch 6, PDF p. 98) — quote in full:

- Photographers love Instagram to showcase their newest cool stuff to the world.
- Consultants love to stand on a soapbox at LinkedIn.
- Journalists, marketers, and others like the few hundred characters of Twitter.
- Designers can show off their work at Dribble.
- Authors can start a blog for free on WordPress.com.

> "And that's just today. The platforms are going to change. But what won't change is how you pick one." (Ch 6, PDF p. 98)

**The three filters** (Ch 6, PDF p. 98):

1. Which site has the audience you want to connect with?
2. What medium do YOU enjoy creating content in?
3. What disproportionate results will you get compared to the work you put in?

**Kagan's worked example** — how he eliminated platforms (Ch 6, PDF pp. 98-99):

- **Instagram** — eliminated because he doesn't take many pics. But Kelsey Hutchins (interior designer) gets most of her business there.
- **Podcasting** — engaged audience but "growing that audience relative to the work involved was near impossible." Jordan Harbinger turned podcasting into a seven-figure business (*The Jordan Harbinger Show*).
- **LinkedIn** — "incredibly noisy, and going viral on it is really tough. But Justin Welsh has used LinkedIn to sell a course that brought in $1.3 million in the first two years."
- **Blogging** — "I love blogging on OkDork and still do it, but the volume of Google searches has gone down." Former *Rolling Stone* writer Matt Taibbi brings in 1.3M visitors/month and $500K/yr on Substack.
- **Twitter** — "the audience isn't growing—their monthly users have been flat for years." Nick Huber of The Sweaty Startup grew his storage business off provocative tweets.
- **TikTok** — "1 million followers there or 100,000 on YouTube? Not equivalent. I'd take YouTube every time. In fact, by one measure, 1 YouTube sub equals 25 on TikTok!" But if your audience is under 25, go where they are. Kagan has 150K+ TikTok followers from repurposing YouTube content but reports "no direct business impact after multiple attempts."

**Kagan's pick: YouTube** (Ch 6, PDF p. 99):

> "YouTube is the LARGEST STREAMING VIDEO SITE ON THE INTERNET. It has 122 million active daily users who consume a billion hours of video each day. PLUS, YouTube monetizes your videos for you with ads and hosts them at no cost (my favorite price)."

He counters two common objections: video is harder than tweets (a feature — less competition), and no studio needed ("I started my 750,000-plus subscriber channel shirtless in my living room... shooting with my iPhone 12. Nothing fancy needed.") Faceless example: SunnyV2 has 2M+ subs on documentaries about famous people — "we've never seen his face" (Ch 6, PDF p. 99).

> "The key principle is to start right now to build your audience and then move them to your email list (which we'll cover in the next chapter)." (Ch 6, PDF p. 100)

**Challenge**: Update your bio on your chosen platform using your unique angle pitch. Kagan's bio: "Chief Sumo at @AppSumo. #30 at Facebook. Helping entrepreneurs at Okdork.com." (Ch 6, PDF p. 100)

## 5. The Content Circle Framework — Core → Medium → Large

> "No permission is needed to build your own audience." (Ch 6, PDF p. 100)

**Anchor case: Ali Abdaal.** Cambridge med student in 2017 → tipped his hand at YouTube. Started with study tips for the BMAT (BioMedical Admissions Test, the British med school entrance exam). Then how to take Section 1, Section 2, med school interview prep. Today: 4.4M+ YouTube subscribers, $400K+/month (Ch 6, PDF p. 100).

> "His audience grew because there was a very specific group of people who were desperate for Ali's expertise on a very specific problem they faced." (Ch 6, PDF p. 100)

**The framework** (Ch 6, PDF pp. 100-101):

1. **Core Circle** — Start with a very narrow audience. Ali: med school exams for British people. "Your niche within a niche can be the most obscure thing imaginable, as long as it makes you and your audience passionate."
2. **Medium Circle** — Content should overlap somewhat with the Core, but appeal broader. Ali: studying and productivity in general (required for all students).
3. **Large Circle** — Largest related audience. Ali's most-watched videos are about his salary (made possible by med fame) or latest Apple products (used for productivity). "All the circles should still include your core audience but keep expanding your circle of influence."

**More cases** (Ch 6, PDF p. 101):

- **Dustin Wunderlich** (Dustin's Fish Tanks) — started by literally reviewing fish tanks → expanded to "what kind of fish to buy to rid your tank of algae" and top aquarium plants → 150K subs, $1M online business selling fish and aquarium supplies.
- **Kyle Lasota** (kylegotcamera, Austin) — biohacking reviews: cold plunges, red light therapy, saunas, sleep gadgets, supplements. Modest 16K YouTube subs; "a lot of his videos get only 400 views" — and he nets $1M/year from affiliate sales. "He knows his Core Circle and they LOVE him!"
- **Andy Schneider** (the Chicken Whisperer, offline example) — loved raising chickens in his backyard outside Atlanta → started meetings about backyard poultry → 5 years later: radio show, magazine, book, US workshop tour. "Backyard farmers who wanted to raise chickens."

**The formula** (Ch 6, PDF p. 101):

> **outcome you'll deliver + target market**

**Worked example — housecleaning company** (Ch 6, PDF p. 101):

- Core: How to clean your evaporative cooler + in the Southwest USA.
- Medium: How to choose laundry detergents + for new homeowners.
- Large: The ten best vacuums + for a family.

## 6. The three "unique viewpoint" prompts

After you have outcome and market, find a unique viewpoint inside that niche. The prompts (Ch 6, PDF p. 102):

1. What is something everyone thinks is true—but you think is wrong?
2. What is something nobody in your target market is talking about—but should be?
3. What are the biggest mistakes people in your market are making—but are totally blind to?

> "Ultimately, your audience wants to learn something from you that's relevant, useful, and surprising. And they want to do that by going on a journey with you." (Ch 6, PDF p. 102)

**Challenge** — Create your own Content Circle using the formula = Outcome you'll deliver + the target market. Fill in Core, Medium, Large (Ch 6, PDF p. 102).

## 7. Be the Guide, Not the Guru

> "People don't want to be lectured at by an all-knowing guru—they want to tag along with a guide. That's why I post so many videos where I reveal the nuts and bolts of my processes." (Ch 6, PDF p. 102)

Ali Abdaal's videos are titled "How I . . ." rather than "How to . . ." — he guides viewers through how *he* studies, takes notes on his iPad Pro, learned to type fast (Ch 6, PDF p. 102).

> "The goal here is to document what YOU do, not what you think everyone else should do. When you position yourself as someone who is on a journey and document your process and your progress, you become relatable, and that is what audiences long for." (Ch 6, PDF p. 102)

> "Some of my most popular videos feature me failing, often. It's fascinating that people want to see what's really going on, not the highlight reel we think they want." (Ch 6, PDF p. 102)

**Day-job example**: Matt's Off-Road Recovery YouTube channel — 1.4M subscribers showing Matt just doing his regular tow truck job, helping stuck drivers (Ch 6, PDF p. 103).

**Co-creation tactic — schoolyard dares.** Kagan dared his Monthly1K class to supply him a business idea to validate → that became Sumo Jerky. "I ask my audience to challenge me to do something difficult; then I go out and do it." Engagement boosts ranking which boosts subscribers (Ch 6, PDF p. 103).

**Devin Stone (LegalEagle)** — pushes viewers to "think like a lawyer" by leaving comments in the form of objections, which he sustains or overrules in-thread (Ch 6, PDF p. 103).

**Danny Wang Design** — local lawn care company posting 45-second before/after yard transformations to snappy music, targeted to homeowners in their service area. 2.3M TikTok followers, regularly 150K+ views. "And Danny himself is nowhere to be found in the videos." (Ch 6, PDF p. 95) — proof that "be the guide" doesn't require being on camera.

## 8. Challenge: post one piece of content

Final challenge of the chapter (Ch 6, PDF p. 103):

1. Your Unique Angle—the secret sauce nobody else has
2. The Platform you're going to post on
3. Your Content Circle—the narrow audience you'll laser-target
4. Posting it TODAY

> "This last step is obviously the hardest one. Don't worry about scripts, camera equipment, or even if it gets any views. The important part is taking the first step at building your community." (Ch 6, PDF p. 103)

## 9. The handoff to email — why social is rented land

Ch 6 ends with the bridge to Ch 7:

> "Next, we're going to show you how to turn the community into customers with an email list." (Ch 6, PDF p. 103)

The reasoning is fully exposed in Ch 7 ("Email Is for Profit"), but the seed is planted here: social media is the *acquisition* layer, email is the *ownership* layer. Ch 7 quote that makes the case (Ch 7, PDF p. 106):

> "I have 120,000 Twitter followers, 750,000 YouTube subscribers, and 150,000 TikTok fans—and I would give them all up for my 100,000 email subscribers. Why? Every time I send an email, 40,000 people open it... I'm not hoping the platform gods will allow me to reach them."

And the cautionary tale (Ch 7, PDF p. 107): digital publisher LittleThings lost 75% of 20M monthly visitors when Facebook changed its algorithm in 2018 — CEO Joe Speiser says it killed the business and he lost $100 million.

> "Social media decides who and how many people you're seen by. One tweak to the algorithm, and you're toast." (Ch 7, PDF p. 107)

So the Ch 6 → Ch 7 arc is: **use social for free reach (rented) → siphon to email (owned) → monetize there.**

## Practical 30-day map (synthesized from the chapter's challenges, in order)

1. **Write your Unique Angle** (4 sentences: who / why trust / passion / what you do for them). (PDF p. 97)
2. **Pick a platform** using the 3 filters; eliminate by audience, enjoyment, leverage. (PDF p. 98)
3. **Update your bio** on that platform using the unique angle. (PDF p. 100)
4. **Define your Content Circle** (Core/Medium/Large) with the formula outcome + market. (PDF p. 102)
5. **Pick a unique viewpoint** with the 3 prompts (wrong / unspoken / blind-spot). (PDF p. 102)
6. **Post one piece of content TODAY**, however rough. (PDF p. 103)
7. Operate as a **guide** (document your "How I…"), not a guru. Show failures. Invite dares. (PDF pp. 102-103)
8. Goal at 30 days: **100 true fans**, not 10K followers. (Part 3 framing, PDF p. 91; chapter principle, PDF p. 94)
9. Hand off to Ch 7: start moving that audience to **email** before the algorithm taxes you.
