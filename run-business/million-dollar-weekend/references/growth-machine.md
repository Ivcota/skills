# The Growth Machine — Ch 8 Reference

Source: Noah Kagan & Tahl Raz, *Million Dollar Weekend* (Penguin Random House, 2024), Chapter 8: "The Growth Machine — My Battle-Tested Growth Playbook" (PDF pp. 114–130).

This file goes deep on the chapter. All page citations use the PDF page numbers from the source file. Scripts, questions, and short distinctive phrases are quoted verbatim; long passages are paraphrased.

---

## 1. Core thesis: Experiment-Based Marketing

The whole chapter rejects "copy what worked for someone else." Kagan frames marketing as a portfolio of cheap, ruthlessly-tracked experiments — not a strategy to be guessed in advance.

Key claims:

- "Merely copying the plan and then hoping and praying won't work. That's gambling. That's luck as a strategy." (Ch 8, PDF p. 114)
- "It's impossible to know which marketing strategies will work for you. Blogging worked for me at Mint.com but has never worked for AppSumo.com. Paid ads work at AppSumo, but we couldn't make the economics work for my OkDork brand." (Ch 8, PDF p. 114)
- "Ultimately, this is all about setting up a process that helps you identify which tactics work for you." (Ch 8, PDF p. 115)

**Why "BEST GUESS" is the right input.** When you build the experiment list you don't know the answer — that's the point. The estimate exists only to force prioritization:

- "To find the thing that does work, what you need is a process of small experiments — based on your BEST GUESS of what might work. It's all about prioritizing tactics and ruthlessly testing them!" (Ch 8, PDF p. 122)
- "The most important aspect of this process is not to worry about being exact. It's to make decent guesses so you can have a framework to prioritize and double down on your sources." (Ch 8, PDF p. 124)
- "When setting your targets, the trick is to use your BEST GUESS; it doesn't have to be a super-accurate number. It's all to help you PRIORITIZE your marketing activities. Over time, you'll get better at this." (Ch 8, PDF p. 124)

The estimate is a sorting tool, not a forecast.

## 2. The credibility stack (opener)

Kagan opens by establishing he has run this exact playbook eight times:

- **Mint.com.** Aaron Patzer initially turned him down: "Sorry, Noah, you're not good enough yet to market my company." (Ch 8, PDF p. 114). Kagan came back with a plan and a guarantee: "I'll get you 100,000 users in six months before you've even launched the product. If I don't hit my target, you don't have to pay me." (Ch 8, PDF p. 114)
- **Mint result.** "I executed the marketing plan with two key components: sponsoring very targeted financial bloggers and writing the best finance content online. Six months later, in September 2007, Mint officially launched with 1 MILLION users. I had exceeded my goal ten times over and got my first six-figure salary!" (Ch 8, PDF p. 114)
- **Other proofs.** "Sumo.com reached 1 billion impressions in twelve months. SendFox.com got 10,000 customers in six months and 850,000-plus YouTube subscribers in the past few years." (Ch 8, PDF p. 114) — same plan, repeated.

The two activities at Mint matter: **targeted finance bloggers + best finance content online**. Those are the two channels he doubled down on once experiments revealed them. They are not generic prescriptions — they're what survived the experiment list for that business.

## 3. The five Growth Machine questions (verbatim)

Kagan: "There are an unlimited number of marketing strategies out there, but there are five questions I keep coming back to for every single business." (Ch 8, PDF p. 114)

1. "What is your one goal for this year?"
2. "Who exactly is your customer and where can you find them?"
3. "What is one marketing activity you can double down on?"
4. "How can you delight your first 100 customers?"
5. "If you HAD to double your business with no money in thirty days, what would you do?"

(Ch 8, PDF p. 114; restated at the TLDR, PDF p. 129–130)

These are the diagnostic he runs against every business he advises. The rest of the chapter is the playbook that answers them in order.

## 4. The 5-step Growth Machine playbook

### Step 1 — Set a single hyper-focused exact goal

Origin story: Zuckerberg shut down Kagan's Facebook-events monetization pitch by writing two things on a whiteboard — "GROWTH" and "1 BILLION" — and explained that every activity at Facebook had to serve growing the user base to 1 billion (Ch 8, PDF p. 115). That clarity became Kagan's template.

Rules for the goal:
- One number. One year (or one quarter). Examples Kagan offers: your Freedom Number, 1,000 YouTube subscribers, $1M net revenue, fifty clients (Ch 8, PDF p. 115).
- "Be specific. One of the most common mistakes I see from entrepreneurs when they set goals is they say they want 'more.' More revenue, more traffic, more downloads. But how much and by when?" (Ch 8, PDF p. 115)
- A goal without a deadline is meaningless. "I want to be rich" → no number, no time. "I want to be worth $1 million in three years." → workable. (Ch 8, PDF p. 115)
- Break the annual goal into a monthly schedule. Kagan's example for his YouTube channel: target 500,000 subscribers in one year, modeled month-by-month so July's goal is 18,833, August's 19,784, September's 20,783 (Ch 8, PDF p. 116). The monthly schedule makes the annual number real and lets you start slow then accelerate as experiments reveal winners.

### Step 2 — Create your Marketing Experiment List

Counter-example before the rule: "Ricky the Realtor" tweeted twice a day for weeks using a new auto-reply tool. Sales after all that effort: $0 (Ch 8, PDF p. 116–117). The lesson is not "tweeting is bad" — it's that he never tested a portfolio.

**Format of the list.** A simple table with these columns:

| Source | Expected Sales | Actual Sales | (optional) Time | (optional) Cost |

(Ch 8, PDF pp. 117, 124–125)

- **Expected Sales** is a BEST GUESS. It forces ranking.
- **Actual Sales** gets filled in after the experiment runs.
- **Time** column (optional) lets you spot activities that get sales cheaply in hours.
- **Cost** column (optional) for paid channels. Kagan's bias: "I encourage people to not spend money on marketing at first. Exhaust your free options." (Ch 8, PDF p. 125)

**Customer-finding formula (feeds the list).** Before you can list channels you need to answer two questions: "Who is your ideal customer? Where are they?" (Ch 8, PDF p. 122)

Kagan's own answers:
- Mint: "personal finance bloggers and tech professionals."
- AppSumo: "Marketing Agency Matt, who is a solopreneur."
- OkDork: "an underdog looking for inspiration on their business journey." (Ch 8, PDF p. 122)

Then look for patterns in your existing customers — age, interest, gender, hobbies, area (Ch 8, PDF p. 122).

The **customer interview** that finds new channels is one message Kagan still sends today:

> "Hey Maria,
> Thank you so much for being a customer.
> Where's the one specific place you'd expect to learn about my product?" (Ch 8, PDF p. 123)

That single question — "Where's the one specific place you'd expect to learn about my product?" — turns existing customers into the source of your next channel ideas.

Worked example for Daniel Bliss:
- **Who:** "People who rock climb outdoors at least once a week."
- **Where:** "North America/Canada, belong to a rock-climbing gym, purchase things from sporting goods stores, read *Outside* magazine, are fans of Alex Honnold (the free soloist climber), are in meetups/online groups to talk about climbers, watch specific YouTubers who teach new climbing techniques, and eat energy food like CLIF Bars." (Ch 8, PDF p. 123)

**Channel menu** (when you can't think of where to look, Ch 8, PDF pp. 123–124):

- **Contacting your network** — your existing network sells fastest because they already know you.
- **Paid ads** — search engines (Bing, Google) for keyword-triggered placement.
- **Social ads** — Twitter, Facebook, Reddit, TikTok, LinkedIn.
- **Content marketing** — blogs, podcasts, videos to generate interest in your product.
- **Cold outreach** — phone calls or cold email to prospects.
- **Target market blogs** — sponsor posts/content on popular blogs in your niche.
- **Influencer marketing** — high-profile bloggers or Instagrammers in your market.
- **PR** — pitch press and bloggers in your niche.
- **SEO** — keyword research on AnswerThePublic or SpyFu, then hyper-targeted content. Reliable but slow.
- **Giveaways** — epic prizes + sweepstakes page + heavy promotion.
- **Collaborations** — appear on other podcasts / shows / newsletters / YouTube channels.

### Step 3 — Double down on what works, kill what doesn't

The golden rule, verbatim: "*Find what works and double down on it; find what doesn't work and kill it.*" (Ch 8, PDF p. 125)

The 30-day cutoff: "you need to set time limits to stop if something isn't working. I find thirty days is more than enough to get results from your marketing experiments." (Ch 8, PDF p. 126)

The "lazy mindset": "If something's too hard and not working after a good try? Give up and move on!" (Ch 8, PDF p. 126)

Why ruthlessness matters: "Even $100 a day or thirty minutes a day is an opportunity cost of time and money that could be spent elsewhere." (Ch 8, PDF p. 126)

Personal worked example: in 2019, growing his online presence, Kagan tried Twitter, TikTok, blogging, Instagram, YouTube. After 30 days, he forced himself to choose. YouTube was vastly better for the work involved, so he killed everything else and went all-in on YouTube (Ch 8, PDF p. 126). Result later: 850,000+ subscribers.

Cadence: "I like to check back against my assumptions weekly (and sometimes daily) to measure my marketing plan progress." (Ch 8, PDF p. 126)

### Step 4 — Make your first 100 customers happier

Reframe question: "How would you double your business if you COULD NOT get any new customers?" (Ch 8, PDF p. 127)

Why this is leverage: "the biggest growth lever in business is customer retention and referrals. If you're just starting out, every referral can literally double your business." (Ch 8, PDF p. 127)

**Unscalable over-delivery examples Kagan uses himself:**

- **YouTube.** "When I started growing my YouTube channel, I personally replied to every YouTube comment. This made the audience feel special and connected to me." (Ch 8, PDF p. 127)
- **Gambit.** "With Gambit, I gave my personal phone number to every customer even when we were making $20-million-plus a year. That level of customer service and attention to detail is how we were able to grow so fast." (Ch 8, PDF p. 127)
- **AppSumo.** "In the first years of AppSumo.com — and even today — I personally write to customers to see what they like and dislike about us. They invariably start their reply by saying, 'You're Noah and you're writing to me? Really?' But by the end they're thrilled — and they're telling their friends." (Ch 8, PDF p. 127)

The first AppSumo personal-email template (sent 17 May 2010, Ch 8, PDF p. 128) asks three questions:
- "What made you interested in buying Imgur?"
- "What other websites/services would you want big discounts on?"
- "Any suggestions or things you would have wanted to see on our website, appsumo.com?"

Then: "Feel free to spread the word: http://appsumo.com/"

His diagnostic question for any existing customer: **"What is one thing we can do today that will make you twice as happy with us?"** (Ch 8, PDF p. 128)

Why unscalable: "This is not scalable. That's the point." (Ch 8, PDF p. 128). Happy customers refer, and they buy your next product — "The longer you retain customers, the more opportunity you have to earn more revenue from them." (Ch 8, PDF p. 128)

**Nick Bare / BPN Supplements (Ch 8, PDF p. 129).** Kagan's own quoted tweet:

> "When Nick Bare was growing BPN Supplements from $1K - $100K per month, he wrote every US customer a hand-written thank you note.
> While he was in KOREA.
> Best way to grow your business:
> Make your customers feel special."

Bare was deployed in Korea, got up at 4 a.m. to do the side hustle, and hand-wrote a note to every US customer. That foundation built BPN into a seven-figure company.

### Step 5 — Repeat

The TLDR on PDF pp. 129–130 is just the five Growth questions again. The whole machine is a loop: pick a hyper-focused goal → list experiments by best-guess expected sales → run them 30 days → double down on winners, kill losers → over-deliver to the first 100 → repeat against the next goal.

## 5. Cautionary tale: Sumo.com Instagram

The chapter is honest that Kagan himself still violates the golden rule. At Sumo.com he started "massively promoting our Instagram posts to convert our followers into customers" because (1) Sumo had 100,000+ Instagram followers with posts getting many likes, and (2) Instagram was working for tons of other people (Ch 8, PDF pp. 125–126).

Result: "it literally drove zero dollars — $0! — but it took us six months and $20,000 spent before we admitted it wasn't working and killed it." (Ch 8, PDF p. 126)

The lesson, in his words: "you need to find the tactics that are right for your business — not just what's the 'hottest' marketing strategy this month." (Ch 8, PDF p. 126)

This is the strongest evidence in the chapter that the discipline is harder than the rule. Vanity signals (follower count, likes) seduce even the practitioner who wrote the rule.

## 6. Daniel Bliss / Belay Shades — the full worked case

Daniel was a Canadian postal worker, a hobbyist climber, and the winner of an AppSumo "Make a $1,000 a Month Business" getaway to Austin. The example runs across PDF pp. 117–122. It is the chapter's spine because every step of the playbook is shown end-to-end on one real business.

### Setup
- Real problem he had: his neck hurt from belaying (looking up at the climber above). He sourced glasses with angled mirrors from a manufacturer on Alibaba.
- Already validated by hand-selling: 2 pairs to a couple he met climbing, the other ~10 to his climbing group (Ch 8, PDF p. 117).
- Stuck after that. Like most entrepreneurs, he did "the obvious yet wrong things" — tricked out his Shopify, investigated IP law, stalked competitors (Ch 8, PDF p. 117).

### Work-backwards math
- Goal: $4,000/month profit (his Freedom Number; would let him quit the post office and rock-climb in Thailand).
- Glasses sell for $60/pair with shipping. He makes $24/pair.
- $4,000 / $24 = **166 glasses sold each month** (~5–6 pairs/day) (Ch 8, PDF p. 117).
- "I can't stress how important this is, so I'll repeat it again: WORK BACKWARDS FROM YOUR GOAL!" (Ch 8, PDF p. 118)

### Marketing Experiment List (expected, totaling his 166 goal)

| Source | Expected Sales |
|---|---|
| Personal network + referrals | 30 |
| Sale to Vancouver Rock Climbing Group | 20 |
| Wholesale | 50 |
| Marketplaces: eBay | 25 |
| Giveaways | 25 |
| Facebook ads | 16 |
| **Total** | **166 (his goal)** |

(Ch 8, PDF p. 118)

### Prioritization forcing-question
Daniel had only one hour a week. So Kagan asked: **"If you could use only two of the marketing activities, what would they be?"** (Ch 8, PDF p. 118)

The two with the highest expected sales:
1. Personal network + referrals
2. Wholesale selling to climbing gyms/online stores

### Execution (verbatim scripts)

**1. Personal network message.** Daniel searched Facebook for every friend who listed climbing in their profile, added them to a sheet, and individually messaged them:

> "Hey <first name>
> Hope you've been awesome.
> I saw you like climbing. Me, too!
> My neck always hurts when I belay, so I created super affordable belay goggles.
> Have about 10 available. Are you interested?
> Climb on,
> Daniel"

(Ch 8, PDF p. 118). A few sales came in just from this. "SCORE."

**2. Climbing-store list build.** Then he listed every rock-climbing store offline and online in Canada:
- Search Google for "rock climbing Vancouver" or search "rock climbing" on Yelp.
- 2A. Go to listed websites and get owner's name (if possible), email, phone.
- OR 2B. Hire someone on Fiverr or Craigslist to scrape every listing onto a sheet. (Ch 8, PDF p. 118)

**3. Wholesale split-profit pitch to climbing gyms.**

> "SUBJECT: Helping you make an extra $1,000 at your climbing gym
> Hey Colleen,
> Hope things have been amazing with you.
> I've been working with climbing gyms like yours and wanted to hook up your members with my new belay glasses.
> www.belayshades.com (people go nuts over them)
> Was thinking, we can email your members with a special discount just for you and we split the profit evenly.
> Be a great way for you to make a profit and hook up your members at the same time.
> Can you let me know by this Friday if this sounds appealing to you?
> Rock on,
> Dan Bliss"

(Ch 8, PDF p. 119). The pitch shape: subject line shows the gym's upside in dollars; pitch frames it as helping their members; deadline ("by this Friday") forces a yes/no.

**4. Marketplaces.** Posted on eBay/Etsy/Craigslist/Amazon (all free). Listing shown: "Belay Glasses for Rock Climbing — Brand New — $9.88 or Best Offer +$2.99 shipping." After a few days — no sales (Ch 8, PDF p. 119).

**5. Facebook & Google ads.** Ran a Facebook ad: "Belay Glasses - on sale now. Protect your neck and enjoy your climbs!" with a Shop now button. "This drove 0 sales." (Ch 8, PDF p. 120)

**6. Giveaway / free-sample influencer pitch** to Facebook pages, Meetup groups, and bloggers related to climbing:

> "SUBJECT: Free belay Glasses for you and your <group name>
> Hey <Club Leader>
> Your group looks awesome! Glad to see the climbing community growing in <location>.
> Wanted to let you know about these cool new belay glasses for rock climbers, called Belay Shades.
> Would love to send you a free pair to try out. If you like them, I'll happily give your group a special price you can share with your members.
> Just email me by <two days from when email is sent> with an address we can ship to, and we will send you a pair.
> Belay On!
> Daniel"

(Ch 8, PDF p. 121). The pitch shape: compliment the group, lead with a free gift, ask only for a shipping address, deadline. "This did not produce ANY results."

### The hit
"But then . . . Daniel got an email from a large online site he'd contacted named Sierra Trading saying they were interested in the glasses." (Ch 8, PDF p. 121). After weeks of silence from small local stores, the big online provider responded in a day. **The order placed was for $4,200** — shown as a Sierra Trading Post purchase order for 200 units at $21.00/pair (Ch 8, PDF p. 121).

### Actuals vs Expected after 30 days

| Source | Expected | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Reach out to friends + referrals | 30 | 9 |
| Sale to Vancouver Rock Climbing Group | 20 | 11 |
| Wholesale options | 50 | **217** |
| Marketplaces — eBay | 25 | 0 |
| Giveaways | 25 | 0 |
| Facebook ads | 16 | 0 |
| **Total** | **166** | **237** |

(Ch 8, PDF pp. 121–122)

### Reading the table
- Wholesale was guessed at 50 → delivered 217. **About 90% of his sales came from one channel.** That's the channel to double down on.
- eBay, giveaways, Facebook ads = **0 sales each.** Kill all three within the 30 days. They each cost time that could have gone into more wholesale outreach.
- Personal network (9) and Vancouver Rock Climbing Group (11) did sell, just below estimate — keep as supporting channels, not main bet.
- The estimates were wrong in every row. That is fine. The list's job was to force the ranking and the ruthless cut, not to be accurate. As Kagan summarizes: "Daniel tried six different experiments in thirty days. He thought wholesaling would make 50 sales. Turns out it made 200+. He thought eBay would do 30 units; turns out it did a whopping 0. So he modified his plan to go wholesale and doubled down on that, since it made 90 percent of his sales." (Ch 8, PDF p. 122)

### Long-term outcome
At the end of Chapter 9, Daniel reports in his own words: the MDW process sparked his entrepreneurial spirit; over the next decade he became the main distributor for a European climbing brand in America; he eventually closed the business "having achieved something approaching a million in sales." The earnings funded freediving instructor training in Egypt and CS/programming education; he still works part-time at the post office by choice for "time freedom." (Ch 9, PDF p. 132)

The point of including the closure: success isn't the brand surviving forever. Success is the Freedom Number being hit and the optionality it buys.

## 7. How these pieces fit

The chapter is structured as one nested mechanism:

1. **Hyper-focused goal** gives you a number to back-solve from (Daniel: $4K/mo → 166 units).
2. **Customer questions** ("Who? Where?") generate channel candidates, supplemented by the channel menu when stuck.
3. **The customer "Where would you expect to learn about my product?" interview** keeps the channel list grounded in reality, not in your guesses.
4. **Marketing Experiment List with BEST GUESS expected sales** ranks the candidates so a time-constrained operator (Daniel had one hour/week) bets on the top two.
5. **30-day run + actuals column** reveals which guesses were right (Daniel: wholesale 217 vs 50) and which were 0s (eBay, giveaways, FB ads).
6. **Double down on winners, kill losers** — the lazy mindset, with Sumo Instagram ($0 / six months / $20K) as the cautionary tale of waiting too long.
7. **First 100 customers happier** via unscalable acts (YouTube comment replies, personal phone number at $20M+/yr, AppSumo founder emails, BPN hand-written notes from Korea, "twice as happy" question) compounds via referrals and repeat purchase.
8. **Repeat** the loop on the next goal.

Each step's output is the next step's input. Skip any step and the machine stops:
- Skip the goal → no way to size experiments.
- Skip the expected-sales guess → no prioritization, you do everything (Ricky the Realtor).
- Skip the kill → you bleed money on Instagram for six months.
- Skip the first-100 over-delivery → you have to refill the funnel from scratch every month.

The chapter's real claim is not "do these things." It is: **marketing is a search process, and the people who win run the search with discipline.**

## 8. Reusable scripts (verbatim, ready to adapt)

- **Personal-network DM** (Ch 8, PDF p. 118) — "Hey <first name> / I saw you like <shared interest>, me too / Here's the thing I made for <problem> / Have ~10 available, interested?"
- **Wholesale split-profit pitch** (Ch 8, PDF p. 119) — subject line names the partner's $ upside; frames as helping their members; even profit split; deadline by Friday.
- **Free-sample influencer pitch** (Ch 8, PDF p. 121) — compliment the community, offer a free pair, conditional discount for members if they like it, two-day reply deadline.
- **Existing-customer channel interview** (Ch 8, PDF p. 123) — "Thank you so much for being a customer. Where's the one specific place you'd expect to learn about my product?"
- **Founder-to-customer email** (Ch 8, PDF p. 128) — thank, ask three questions about purchase motivation + adjacent wants + suggestions, invite sharing.
- **Twice-as-happy diagnostic** (Ch 8, PDF p. 128) — "What is one thing we can do today that will make you twice as happy with us?"
