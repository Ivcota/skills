# Jobs, Outcomes, Constraints — the input taxonomy

The first move in ODI is replacing the customer's vocabulary of "wants" with three measurable input types. Until your team uses these three words consistently, every downstream step is corrupted.

## The Three Inputs

### 1. Job

> A task or activity individuals or businesses are trying to get done.

A job is *what the customer is trying to accomplish*, never a product attribute. Customers "hire" products to get jobs done — they buy mowers to cut lawns, MP3 players to manage music, insurance to limit financial risk.

**Three types** (Figure 2.1):

| Type | Question | Razor example |
|------|----------|---------------|
| **Functional Job** | What task must the customer accomplish? | Remove facial hair |
| **Emotional — Personal Job** | How does the customer want to *feel*? | Feel attractive |
| **Emotional — Social Job** | How does the customer want to *be perceived*? | Be seen as well-groomed |

Functional jobs are the focal point for value creation; emotional jobs are the focal point for positioning and messaging.

**Ancillary and related jobs are a hidden growth lever.** Razor users are also trying to "prevent dry skin," "prevent wrinkles," "stop bleeding when nicked." iPod's dominance came from solving the *related* jobs of MP3 ownership (download, organise, sync) — not just playback. Always map adjacent jobs.

**Test for a clean job statement:**
- No product name in it
- A functional verb + an object
- Stable over decades — people cleaning their teeth in 1850 have the same job as today

### 2. Desired Outcome

> A metric the customer uses to measure how well a job is getting done.

Outcomes are the customer's measures of value. Customers have them in their heads but seldom articulate them. A given job has **50–150 desired outcomes** — not a handful. Capturing fewer means hiding the underserved ones.

**The strict format** (Figure 2.3):

```
[Direction] + [Unit of Measure] + [Outcome Desired]
   Minimise         the time it takes      to prepare the skin for hair removal
   Increase         the number of cuts     that can be made with a single blade
   Minimise         the likelihood         that the blade will snag
   Minimise         the frequency          with which the cord gets in the way
```

**Direction must be only "Minimise" or "Increase".** Words like "reduce," "eliminate," "prevent," and "avoid" each shift the customer's importance rating in surveys — they imply a target of zero, distorting the data. Pick two words and stick with them.

**Units of measure** are always one of: *time, number, frequency, likelihood, amount*.

**The outcome desired** is the *thing measured* — never a feature, never a solution.

Bosch's circular-saw example captured ~80 outcomes. Rohm and Haas (house paint) captured ~100. Motorola (one of the cell-phone jobs) captured ~120. Plan for that scale; if you have 12 outcomes after interviews, you stopped too early.

### 3. Constraint

> A roadblock that prevents customers from getting a job done altogether or under certain circumstances.

Constraints are physical, regulatory, or environmental. *Can't make a cell phone call from inside a building* is a constraint. They are often excellent innovation targets because removing them *unlocks new jobs* rather than improving existing ones.

Cell-phone constraints captured by Motorola included: service-area dead zones, dropped calls during handoff, inability to use during airline flight, regulatory bans in certain venues. Each became an R&D target.

## The 1-Hour Outcome Interview

The single skill ODI training centres on: extracting 20–30 outcomes from one customer in one hour without leading them.

### Setup

- **Dissect the job into process steps first.** A circular-saw job ("cut a piece of wood") has six process steps: plan the cut → adjust the saw → start the cut → operate the saw → maintain the saw → complete the cut, with "ensure safety" as a continuous-process step (Figure 2.2 — the **Customer Value Model**). Capturing outcomes step-by-step prevents gaps.
- **Interview the *end user* directly.** Not OEMs, not channel partners, not the sales force. Each layer launders solutions and specs into the data.
- **Pick the right "customer" in the value chain.** Many products have multiple customers (printer: end user + IT director + procurement). Capture outcomes from all of them.

### Interview moves

| Customer says | You translate to outcome |
|---------------|--------------------------|
| "I want a lighter razor." | "What does lighter help you do?" → "So you don't get fatigued during a long shave" → *Minimise the amount of fatigue experienced during a shave.* |
| "It should be reliable." | "What does reliable look like? How would you measure that?" → *Minimise the likelihood that the blade fails mid-shave.* |
| "I want a lubrication strip." | "What does the lubrication strip do for you?" → *Minimise the number of nicks when shaving.* |
| "It should be easy to use." | "Walk me through the steps. Which one is hardest?" → likely 5+ distinct outcomes hiding behind one adjective. |

### Stop conditions

- You have ≥ 20 outcomes from this customer
- New customers stop surfacing new outcomes (saturation across ~20–30 interviews)
- Every process step in the Customer Value Model has at least 5 outcomes attached

### After the interview

- Rewrite every outcome in the strict format
- De-duplicate aggressively (synonyms collapse)
- Group by process step
- Feed the cleaned list into the quantitative survey (Step 3 of ODI) — importance and satisfaction ratings on a 1–5 scale, ≥ 180 respondents per segment

## What you do *not* capture

Train the team to spot and reject:

- **Solutions** ("add a rubberised handle")
- **Specifications** ("make the handle 4 inches long")
- **Needs** as adjectives ("reliable," "durable," "powerful")
- **Benefits** ("better," "faster," "easier")

All five appear in customer speech. None are useful. Translate every one up the stack into an outcome statement before it enters the data set.

## Why this is the whole game

> *"Customer metrics make possible the systematic and predictable creation of breakthrough products and services."*

Every step after this one — opportunity ranking, segmentation, targeting, positioning, pipeline prioritisation, ideation, concept evaluation — operates on the outcome list. Garbage in, garbage out. Get this right and the rest of ODI runs almost mechanically. Get it wrong and the Opportunity Algorithm is ranking noise.
