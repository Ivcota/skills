# Cold Outreach — Study Summary

*Source: $100M Leads, Alex Hormozi (2023), Chapter #3 Cold Outreach (PDF pp. 155–186). Educational summary for personal study.*

## Definition

Cold outreach is one-to-one *private* contact with strangers — people who do not know you — using any method that lets you talk to one person at a time: email, phone call, direct message, SMS, voicemail, even a knock on a door (p. 162). Hormozi frames it as the more advanced cousin of warm outreach: the same private 1-to-1 quadrant of the Core Four, but no longer limited by who already knows you. He calls it "the more advanced cousin of warm outreach, no longer limited by your warm audience" (p. 162). Cold outreach has one defining variable that warm outreach does not: trust. "Cold outreach has one key difference from warm outreach: trust. Strangers don't trust you" (p. 163). The whole chapter is built around solving the three problems that flow from that single fact: you don't have a way to contact strangers, even if you do they ignore you, and even if they pay attention they're not interested (p. 163).

## When To Use

Cold outreach is the move when (a) you've exhausted warm outreach and content, or (b) you want to *grow faster* or *make lead flow more predictable* than warm channels allow (p. 162). Hormozi explicitly orders the Core Four so that cold outreach comes after warm reach outs and posting content — by the time you start, you've already had reps writing scripts and offers (p. 178). It's especially well-suited to *higher-ticket* sales, because cold outreach teams are expensive and the math only pencils out at sufficient ticket size; for low-ticket products, Hormozi recommends using cold outreach to land *affiliates* who in turn sell to consumers, effectively converting a low-ticket sale into a high-ticket one (Author Note, p. 179). The chapter epigraph — Napoleon's "Quantity has a quality all of its own" (p. 155) — sets the temperament: this is for operators willing to grind volume.

## List-Building Principles

Hormozi names *three* methods for assembling a targeted list, in order of preference: **Scrape 'em** (software), **Buy 'em** (list brokers), and **Look for 'em** (manual / "elbow grease"), p. 167. He works the list waterfall top-down — fastest first, hardest last — and only drops to the next tier if the previous one can't deliver his exact criteria. Software scrapes names against your criteria; brokers assemble lists to spec and send samples to test; elbow grease means joining interest groups and communities and pulling contacts manually (p. 168–169).

Two principles govern the whole list-building stage:

1. **Specificity is the upside of cold outreach.** Unlike warm reach outs or content, cold outreach lets you pick *exactly* who you contact — "Now *we* get to pick our targets rather than them picking us" (p. 167). He uses examples like "hedge fund managers managing over $1B" or "golf apparel retail store owners over $3M in sales" (p. 167) to underline how surgically narrow you can go.
2. **Freshness beats convenience.** Manually-built lists outperform commodity lists because they haven't been hammered by every other cold outreacher: "if you assemble a list of names yourself, it's less likely that person has already received many cold reach outs from other companies. So they're the freshest" (p. 169).

He also flags **interest groups** as the highest-quality source: "Interest groups contain the *highest quality leads* because they are concentrated pools of people looking for a solution" (Pro Tip, p. 170).

## Personalization Principle

Hormozi's framing is that strangers introduce two trust problems and personalization solves the first one: "They Don't Know Us → Personalize (Act Like You Know Them)" (p. 170). The goal is to make a *cold* reach out feel like a *warm* reach out. The mechanism is doing one to three pieces of light research per lead — something a friend might know about them — and opening with it, ideally complimenting them on it and showing how it benefited *you* (p. 172). "Even if someone doesn't know you, they will appreciate the time you took to research them before contacting them. This tiny effort goes a long way" (p. 173).

The second principle is the **personalization-vs-volume tradeoff**: "Generally speaking, you sacrifice personalization for scale. You get a higher response rate with personalized messages. *The fewer leads you have, the less automation you should use*" (p. 176). If your TAM is 1,000 hedge fund managers, personalize every one. If it's tens of millions of women trying to lose weight, you can afford less personalization — but if you *can* personalize at scale with tech, that's the "deadly effective lead-getting combo" (Pro Tip: Personalization Tech, p. 177).

## Volume Math

Cold outreach is treated as an explicit numbers game. Key ratios Hormozi cites:

- **Phone benchmark**: 100 cold calls/day → ~20% pick-up → ~25% of those take the lead magnet → 4 engaged leads/day, roughly 1 engaged lead per hour (p. 180).
- **Email benchmark**: "100 personalized emails per day. From there, thirty percent open our email. From there, 10% reply showing interest. That means we'd have three engaged leads (30% x 10% = 3%)" (p. 180). Target is **3% of your list turning into engaged leads**.
- **Portfolio email screenshot**: 801 contacted → 428 opened (53%) → 48 replied (6.0%) → 21 opportunities (p. 181).
- **DM benchmark**: 100 personalized voice memos/videos → 20% reply = 20 engaged leads (p. 182).
- **Unit economics example**: rep paid $15/hr + $50/show, calling 200 leads/day, ~2 shows/day → $120/show fully loaded → $360 cost per closed client at 33% close → with $3,600 profit/client = **10:1 return** (p. 183).
- **Profitability rule**: "you do well when you make at least *three times* the lifetime profit of a customer compared to what it costs you to get them" (p. 180); a tuned operation can hit "over 30:1" (p. 182).
- **Belief-breaking volume**: one VA sending 2,000 emails/day with multiple software tools generates 40 engaged leads/day → 4 customers/day at a $20K-per-customer business (Author Note, p. 187).
- **Floor**: "Don't set a daily goal below 100. And don't stop for 100 days minimum" (Pro Tip: Count in 100s, p. 188).

A 50% email response-rate bump came from rewriting scripts at a third-grade reading level (Pro Tip, p. 173).

## Channels

Hormozi's channel diagram on p. 162 lists five private 1-to-1 methods. One sentence each:

- **Email**: His preferred *first touch* because most people don't reply, which legitimately earns you a follow-up by another method — "*Hey I'm calling you to follow up about my email*" (p. 175).
- **Phone call**: High-bandwidth but trust-gated — "you don't have their phone numbers. Even if you do, they don't pick up. If they pick up, they hang up on you" (p. 164).
- **DM (direct message)**: Used for personal voice memos and personal videos with a name + one personal line before the standard message, hitting roughly 20% reply on a tuned list (p. 182).
- **SMS / text**: Templated text to the prospect's phone; treated as an automatable distribution channel (p. 175).
- **Voicemail (voicemail drops)**: Pre-recorded voicemail dropped to a voicemail box, a fully automatable touch (p. 175).
- **Door knock / handwritten letter**: Mentioned as legitimate manual variants — "You knock on 100 doors… handwritten letter to every person on the list" (pp. 162, 175).

The channel principle: "The more ways you try to contact someone, the more likely you are to contact them. People respond to different methods" (p. 174). Hormozi himself says he ignores phone calls but replies to DMs (p. 174).

## Message Structure

Cold message anatomy = **Personalize → Big Fast Value** (p. 170, p. 173). Personalization makes the cold reach look warm in the first few seconds (subject line, opening DM, opening sentences of a call). Big Fast Value then overcomes the trust gap by handing over something the prospect would normally pay for — a lead magnet or offer so generous "they feel stupid saying no" (p. 174). Hormozi's one-line summary: "*the goal is to demonstrate big value as fast as possible*" (p. 174). The underlying anatomy slots into the warm-outreach **A-C-A** (Approach → Connect → Ask) framework he references on p. 182, now front-loaded with personalization and a heavier-than-normal value drop because trust is lower. He's explicit on the bar: "we're not trying to tickle their interest, we're trying to blow their minds in under thirty seconds" (p. 173).

## Illustrative Excerpts

1. **Personalized cold call opener** (p. 171): "*it's Alex…then pauses…I watched a few of your videos and read that recent blog post you wrote on dog training. It was killer! Really helped me out with my doberman.*"
2. **Pivot to ask after personal opener** (p. 171): "*I work for a company that helps dog trainers fill up their books… Do you offer similar services… If you got a boatload of high paying new dog training customers you wouldn't be upset with me would you?*"
3. **Cross-channel follow-up line** (p. 175): "*Hey I'm calling you to follow up about my email.*"

## Mistakes

- Treating cold outreach as a one-shot — most operators don't follow up at all, when they should be contacting each lead multiple times in multiple ways (p. 174).
- Quitting before the curve compounds; Hormozi's own ramp was 0 → 2 → 4 → 6 → 10 → 14 → 20 → 30 sales/month over 9 months, and his team twice asked him to pull the plug (pp. 159–160).
- Expecting one conversation to close — "expect two to three conversations before a higher ticket sale" (p. 175).
- Writing scripts at adult reading level; rewriting at third-grade level produced a 50% response bump (p. 173).
- Offering a weak lead magnet — Hormozi's first lead magnet ("game planning session" = sales call in disguise) failed for four months until he swapped it for genuine free service, tripling take rates (p. 174).
- Hiring outreach reps who don't track metrics — "the two times I failed at cold outreach I hired people who never tracked metrics well. The third person did. And cold reach outs succeeded" (p. 180).
- Giving each rep too many leads, so they waste them — "If someone can work one hundred leads at full capacity, I'll give them seventy-ish" (Pro Tip, p. 184).
- Iterating before replicating — new reps should shadow the best rep and *double* their inputs before getting creative ("Replicate before you iterate," p. 178).
- Overthinking scripts — "Phone and chat scripts are never more than a page or two, and cold emails rarely more than half a page… There are no awards for prettiest script" (p. 175).

## Ethical Guardrails

- **Stop when asked.** "If someone asks you not to contact them, don't contact them again… you don't want a bad reputation. That kind of stuff follows you. Try hard because you have a genuine desire to solve their problems, but be respectful." (Pro Tip: Don't be a nincompoop, p. 176).
- **Legal methods only.** Hormozi underlines that what he built used "every (legal) cold outreach method we knew" (p. 161) — the parenthetical is doing intentional work.
- **Genuine intent over going through the motions.** "Act like you're *actually* trying to get ahold of these people, rather than going through the motions, and you probably will" (p. 175). Volume without genuine helpful intent is the wrong frame.
- **Compliance is your responsibility even when platforms aren't policing.** Cold outreach escapes platform-rule headaches, but "You still need to be FTC compliant" (p. 185) — the rules don't disappear, they just shift to the law.
- **Automate when ethical and available** (p. 176). Hormozi flags this as a *conditional* — automation is encouraged, but only when the channel and the method are ethical, not as a license to spam.
- **Don't milk communities.** When sourcing from interest groups, "I prefer to find contact information outside the group so I don't come off as someone solely trying to milk the group for business *but I will if I have to*" (p. 168) — a soft norm about respecting community spaces.
