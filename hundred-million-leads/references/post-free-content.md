# Post Free Content — Hook, Retain, Reward

Reference deep-dive for **Section 3** of the parent skill. Expands the Core Four channel that turns strangers into a warm audience by broadcasting valuable content one-to-many. Source: Alex Hormozi, *$100M Leads* (Acquisition.com, 2023), pp. 113–158.

## 1. Definition

In Hormozi's Core Four advertising matrix, **Post Free Content** is the *1-to-∞ / Public* quadrant — broadcasting valuable content to people who don't yet know you (and to those who do) so that strangers convert into a "warm audience" you can later monetize.

The single most important reframe in the chapter is this: **the content you create is not the compounding asset — the audience is**. Hormozi states it directly: *"the content you create isn't the compounding asset — the audience is. So even though the content may disappear in time, your audience keeps growing"* (p. 113). Posts disappear off the feed; followers don't. Treat each post as advertising spend (paid in time) whose deliverable is one more engaged stranger added to a list you own permission to talk to.

Hormozi opens the chapter by re-positioning content as advertising, not branding or self-expression: *"Posting free content grows your warm audience… constantly posting free content means you'll have a constantly growing audience of people more likely to buy your stuff"* (p. 117). This is the operative definition for the rest of the section. If a piece of content does not move someone closer to becoming an engaged lead, it is not doing the job.

## 2. Why It Works

The mechanism is leverage. Warm outreach is one-to-one and doesn't scale: *"If we want to reach ten people, we have to repeat ourselves ten times. Lots of effort. By posting free content, we can say it once and reach all ten"* (p. 116). After Hormozi 10x'd his volume, his audience grew 10x in six months. *"Volume works. Content works. A growing audience is the result"* (p. 116).

Free content compounds in three directions:
1. People who find a post valuable join your warm audience.
2. Those people share it, recruiting more strangers into your warm audience.
3. Every other Core Four channel — warm, cold, paid — converts better when prospects can verify you exist and are credible: *"If you reach out to someone and they can't find content related to your services, they're less likely to buy. On the other hand, if they find lots of valuable content, they are more likely to buy"* (p. 118).

Hormozi reinforces the case with a hard datum from his own portfolio. A survey of his gym-coaching clients revealed *"78% of all clients had consumed at least TWO long form pieces of content, prior to booking a call"* (p. 142). His prior belief — that paid ads alone drove the business — was false. Free content was nurturing the demand that paid ads merely captured. The implication: paid-ad attribution lies, and most operators under-invest in long-form content because they can't see the assist.

## 3. The Content Unit

Hormozi reduces all audience-growing content to one atomic structure he calls the **content unit**: *"the smallest amount of material it takes to hook, retain and reward attention is a content unit. It can be as little as an image, a meme, or a sentence"* (p. 120). A tweet, a meme, a thumbnail caption — each is one content unit. A long-form video is just *many content units linked together* (p. 128). All three jobs (Hook, Retain, Reward) can happen at once inside the same line.

The practical consequence: long-form content is not a different species from short-form — it is short-form repeated until the reward is large enough. Every two or three sentences need their own internal hook-retain-reward, or the viewer leaves.

## 4. HOOK

The objective of the hook is *"to give them a reason to redirect their attention from whatever they are doing towards us"* (p. 121). Hormozi engineers the hook through three levers operating together: **topic**, **headline**, and **format**.

### 4a. Topic — the five categories

Hormozi prefers personal experience because *"no one else has lived your life but you"* (p. 122). He divides topics into five sources, ordered by how easy they are to mine on demand:

1. **Far Past** — life lessons, anecdotes from years ago, formative moments. Hooks here trade on retrospection and irony. Example: *"I complained to a friend that I didn't have enough time to do something while glued to my phone"* (p. 122).
2. **Recent Past** — recent meetings, conversations, and calls. *"There's gold in these conversations"* (p. 123). The freshness reads as authentic because it is.
3. **Present** — capture ideas the moment they arrive. The principle: when an insight strikes, write or record it before the feeling fades. Most insights die because they weren't captured.
4. **Trending** — ride attention already moving. Latch your point onto a topic the algorithm is already pushing.
5. **Manufactured** — create a new experience just for content. Example: *"I lived on $100 for a month"* (p. 124). The constraint itself is the hook.

### 4b. Headline — the seven attention components

Hormozi extracts seven attention components from news media and prescribes including **at least two in your headline** (pp. 125–126):

| Component | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Recency** | Signals "this just happened." Time-stamped freshness beats evergreen feel. |
| **Relevancy** | Speaks to a specific avatar's identity, problem, or situation. |
| **Celebrity** | Borrowed credibility from a known name (person, brand, place). |
| **Proximity** | Local. Geographically or socially close to the viewer. |
| **Conflict** | Two forces in tension. People stop for fights. |
| **Unusual** | Pattern-break. Numbers that don't fit, claims that violate expectation. |
| **Ongoing** | Implies more to come — the story isn't over. |

The threshold of two-or-more is enforcement against bland headlines. *"5 Mistakes I Made"* has Unusual (number) and Relevancy. *"What I Learned From [Famous Person] Last Tuesday"* has Celebrity, Recency, and Relevancy. The more components stacked into one short headline, the higher the click-through.

### 4c. Format-matching

Format must *look like what the audience expects will reward them on this platform*. Mismatch is fatal: *"better-looking content will hook them before yours even has a chance"* (p. 127). LinkedIn-formatted content fails on Tinder and vice-versa. Vertical-video natives scroll past horizontal as if it were an ad.

The operational rule (p. 127): *"Format your content for the platform first. Then, tweak it so it hooks your ideal audience. Use the best content on the platform that targets your market as your guide."* In practice: pick the platform, study the top three posts in your niche on that platform, copy their structural format (length, aspect ratio, caption style, hook line, thumbnail style), then layer your differentiation. This is not creative theft — it is matching the implicit contract the platform's audience has with the format.

## 5. RETAIN

*"My favorite driver of retention is curiosity"* (p. 130). Hormozi retains attention by embedding unresolved questions — explicit or implicit — into the viewer's mind so they need to keep watching to resolve them. He names three favorite vehicles:

- **Lists** — e.g., *"Top 10 Mistakes."* Flexible. The reward is implicit (each item could be the one that helps) and the open count creates a natural countdown.
- **Steps** — sequential actions that produce a result, e.g., *"3 Steps to Creating a Great Hook."* Rigid order. The reward is explicit (the result at the end) and missing one step breaks the chain.
- **Stories** — events real or imagined. *"All three drive curiosity because people want to know what happens next"* (p. 131). Stories interweave both: a beginning sets up a question, a middle deepens it, an end pays it off.

The action step (p. 132): *"Embed questions in their minds to make them want to know what happens next."* Practical tactics inside any of the three vehicles: open loops ("I'll explain why in a second"), forward references ("the third one surprised me"), counter-intuition ("I thought X — I was wrong"). Each is a small unresolved question that keeps the viewer in their seat.

Choosing between the three: lists for browsable/snackable formats, steps for instructional content where order matters, stories for emotional content and long-form. A 20-minute video is typically a story holding a list, holding several mini-steps.

## 6. REWARD

The hook and retain stages are wasted if you don't pay off the curiosity you created. Hormozi defines content quality in one underlined sentence: *"How good your content is depends on how often it rewards your audience in the time it takes them to consume it"* (p. 132). Think **value per second**.

The chapter's most quoted line follows: *"there is no such thing as too long, only too boring"* (p. 132). A 90-second post that rewards twice is worse than a 20-minute video that rewards twenty times. Length is not the variable — reward density is.

Reward means *satisfying the exact promise the hook made*. Promise seven ways, give seven ways. Promise method, give method. Promise transformation, show transformation. Don't promise spouse advice and deliver to single guys — *"deliver to the audience you hooked"* (p. 133).

The empirical test is brutal and self-correcting: *"Here's how you know if you succeeded: your audience grows. If it's not growing, your stuff isn't that good. Practice and you'll get better"* (p. 133). The audience decides, not the creator. Lagging audience growth is the diagnostic — content quality is the disease.

## 7. Audience-Quality Principle — Build for Strangers

Hormozi distinguishes the audience that *grows* from the audience that *engages but doesn't grow*. The single quality test is whether content is built for strangers.

In a Pro Tip box (p. 129): *"If you want to grow your warm audience, then you need to make content assuming the people consuming it have never heard of you before… consider the alternative, you litter your content with 'inside jokes' that no one gets besides your audience. Cool for you guys, but no one else will feel welcome. And your audience growth will slow down."*

Inside jokes, recurring characters, and unexplained references reward existing fans but tax newcomers. Newcomers carry the share-loop. Without them, the audience plateaus.

A related rule: switch from **"How to" to "How I"** (p. 142). *"How to"* implies authority you may not have earned and reads as preachy when challenged. *"How I"* is unassailable — no one can question what you personally did. The framing also keeps the content concrete (a specific story) instead of abstract (general advice).

## 8. Volume Rule — 100 Minutes/Day, 7 Days/Week, 12-Month Minimum

The volume principle is the chapter's blunt heart. Hormozi paid an influencer $120,000 for content advice and got back one sentence: *"Bro, anyone telling you there's some secret is trying to sell you something. We just put out as much as we possibly can… You just gotta do more bro"* (p. 115).

The daily checklist (p. 152):
- **How Much:** 100 minutes per day creating content
- **How Many:** As many times as the platform shows it (≥1 release/day/platform)
- **How Long:** As long as it takes
- **Cadence:** Every morning, 7 days a week

The honest reference point: *"I posted a new podcast twice a week for four years before even getting picked up on the Top 100 list"* (p. 147). The principle reduces to an aphorism inside the chapter: *"Most people don't make money because they can't wait 12 months"* (p. 135). Twelve months is the floor, not the goal.

## 9. Platform Strategy — Depth-then-Width vs. Width-then-Depth

There are two opposing scaling strategies (pp. 139–141):

- **Depth-then-Width** — pick one platform, maximize it, then add the next. Compounds faster on the chosen platform because your output is concentrated. Risk: single-channel dependence; if the platform changes, you lose the audience.
- **Width-then-Depth** — be on every platform from the start, then deepen them all together. Reaches broader audiences faster. Risk: *"lots of bad content everywhere. Sucky fluff"* (p. 141) — output is spread thin and quality drops.

When to use each:
- **Depth-first** when you are early, solo, time-constrained, or learning. Master the format on one platform before duplicating. This is the default for individuals.
- **Width-first** when you have a team or budget for repurposing, or when your audience is genuinely split across platforms (e.g., LinkedIn for buyers + TikTok for end-users). Format must still match the platform; never cross-post raw.

Hormozi's own minimalist process (p. 141): pick topics (~1 hour), batch-record short clips twice a month, and record 2–4 longer videos on the same day to anchor longer-form content. Long-form is the mine; short-form is the ore.

## 10. Give-to-Ask Ratio ≥ 3:1

The economic constraint on a content channel is goodwill. Asking too often drains the well faster than content can refill it. Hormozi's rule: **maintain a give-to-ask ratio of at least 3:1**. *"The moment you start asking for money is the moment you decide to slow down your growth"* (p. 137).

Reference benchmark: television runs about **3.5:1** (commercial blocks vs. show content); growing platforms run far higher. *"Give far far more than you ask"* (p. 134).

Two operating principles:
- **Give in public, ask in private** (p. 136). Public posts are pure give. The ask happens in DMs, email, sales pages, or one-on-one. *"Let the audience self-select when they're ready to give you money."*
- **One CTA at a time** (p. 145). *"A confused mind doesn't buy."* Don't stack like-share-subscribe-comment-buy in the same ask. Pick one action.

The mistake to avoid is over-monetizing. Hormozi's friend's podcast shrank after he started asking too often inside content (p. 138). Goodwill is finite per period; spend it slower than you make it.

## 11. Free Goodwill — The Sub-Chapter

"Free Goodwill" is a short standalone chapter (pp. 153–158) that is *itself* an instance of the give-until-they-ask doctrine — Hormozi practicing what he just preached. After spending two chapters teaching content strategy, he stops to make a soft ask on behalf of someone the reader has never met: leave the book a review.

He frames it as a low-cost goodwill act that compounds into business outcomes for a faceless entrepreneur — *"one more small business provides for their community… one more dream come true"* (p. 156). The ask is structurally a demonstration: a 30-second action that costs the reader nothing but signals reciprocity to Hormozi.

Why is it separate from "Post Free Content"? Because **content builds your audience; goodwill leverages your audience to build someone else's**. Hormozi: *"If you provide something of value to another person, it makes you more valuable to them. If you'd like goodwill straight from another entrepreneur — and you believe this book will help them — send this book their way"* (p. 159).

Free Goodwill is the chapter that earns the right to have asked, by modeling the *"give in public, ask in private"* principle (p. 137). The lesson generalizes: introduce two of your audience members to each other, share another creator's work, write a referral testimonial. Each goodwill transaction outside your own funnel deepens the trust your funnel rests on.

## 12. Two Audience Metrics, Not One

Vanity metrics lie. Hormozi prescribes tracking *both* (pp. 144–145):

- **Total followers and reach** — How big.
- **Rate of growth (% per month)** — How fast.

A 50% monthly growth rate from 1,000 to 1,500 is a stronger quality signal than a flat-but-large follower count. Growth rate is the empirical proof that strangers are still being rewarded — because only the reward step recruits new strangers via shares. A flat audience means the share-loop has died, even if absolute size looks good.

## 13. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Treating content as the asset | Content disappears off the feed; only the audience compounds (p. 113) | Audience-first metrics: followers + monthly growth rate |
| Inside jokes for existing fans | Newcomers feel excluded; share-loop dies (p. 129) | Write assuming the reader has never heard of you |
| "How to" preaching when new | Reads as arrogant; invites challenge (p. 142) | Switch to "How I" — own a story, not a doctrine |
| Stopping before 12 months | Audience growth is long-tailed (p. 135) | Commit to a year minimum; benchmark = Hormozi's 4 years pre-Top-100 |
| Pre-scheduling all posts | Removes feedback pressure; quality drops (p. 144) | Manual posting; respond live to comments |
| Multiple CTAs in one ask | *"Confused mind doesn't buy"* (p. 145) | One CTA per piece, period |
| Over-asking in content | Drains goodwill faster than content deposits it (p. 138) | Keep give:ask ≥ 3:1; ask in private |
| Bait-and-switch headlines | Hook promise unmet → no shares (p. 133) | Reward the exact promise the hook made |
| General content too early | Doesn't rank against established generalists (p. 143) | "Puddle" content — narrow niche/town first |
| Blaming "short attention spans" | Externalizes the failure to reward (p. 144) | *"Make good stuff people like and reap the rewards"* |
| Format mismatch | Better-formatted competitor wins the slot (p. 127) | Format for the platform first, then differentiate |

## 14. Ethical Guardrails

- **Always reward what you hook.** *"You did a bad job of rewarding. People will not want to watch again, and certainly won't share it"* (p. 133). Don't bait-and-switch on the headline's promise.
- **Give until they ask.** *"The moment you start asking for money is the moment you decide to slow down your growth"* (p. 137). Don't drain goodwill faster than you deposit it.
- **Maintain a 3:1+ give-to-ask ratio.** Television runs 3.5:1; growing platforms run far higher. *"Give far far more than you ask"* (p. 134).
- **Give in public, ask in private.** *"Let the audience self-select when they're ready to give you money"* (p. 136). Don't make every public touchpoint a sales pitch.
- **Don't kill the golden goose.** *"It's a balancing act. Over-give to protect your most valuable asset — the goodwill of your audience"* (p. 138).
- **One CTA at a time.** *"A confused mind doesn't buy"* (p. 145).
- **Talk about your experience, not their obligation.** Switch from "How to" to "How I" — *"When you tell a stranger what to do, it's hard to avoid coming off preachy or arrogant"* (p. 143). Don't moralize.
- **Make good content, not whining content.** *"Make good stuff people like and reap the rewards rather than whining about people's 'short attention spans'"* (p. 144). Don't blame the audience for your own failure to reward.

## 15. Quick Reference — The Content Unit Checklist

Before publishing any unit, verify each layer is doing its job:

| Layer | Test | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook — Topic** | Drawn from one of the five categories (Far Past / Recent Past / Present / Trending / Manufactured) | pp. 122–124 |
| **Hook — Headline** | Includes ≥2 of the seven attention components (Recency, Relevancy, Celebrity, Proximity, Conflict, Unusual, Ongoing) | pp. 125–126 |
| **Hook — Format** | Looks native to the platform's top-performing posts in your niche | p. 127 |
| **Retain** | Uses Lists, Steps, or Stories — embeds at least one open loop | pp. 130–131 |
| **Reward** | Pays off the exact promise made by the hook; high value per second | pp. 132–133 |
| **Audience** | Comprehensible to someone who has never heard of you; "How I" not "How to" | pp. 129, 142 |
| **Ratio** | Give-to-ask ≥ 3:1 across the channel; this unit is a give (or a permitted ask) | pp. 134, 137 |
| **CTA** | At most one, S-P-E-L-L-ed out, in private channels for monetary asks | pp. 145, 136 |

If a unit fails any row, fix it before posting. If it passes all eight and the audience still doesn't grow over 90 days, the upstream problem is volume (Section 8) — not the unit.
