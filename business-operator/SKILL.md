# Business Operator

Operate the business from one cockpit: **cash, conversations, offers, capacity, cost curve**. The job is not to make a clever plan; it is to pick the next move that improves profit, learning, or capacity without making each additional customer harder to serve.

## Operating principle

A move is operator-good only if it improves at least one curve:

- **Acquisition curve** — the next qualified conversation costs the same or less.
- **Delivery curve** — the next customer requires the same or less founder/team time without hurting results.
- **Value curve** — each customer buys more value through price, upsells, continuity, expansion, or referrals.
- **Proof curve** — each delivery creates proof that lowers future acquisition cost.

Use rough numbers. If exact numbers are missing, ask for estimates or mark them unknown; never let missing precision block a 7-day test.

```
Marginal contribution = gross profit per new customer - marginal CAC - marginal cost-to-serve
Scale-safe = marginal contribution > 0 and delivery quality holds
Great = marginal CAC or marginal cost-to-serve falls as customer count rises
```

## Intake — always start here

Ask only what is needed for the current branch, but collect enough to avoid vibes-only advice:

1. **Goal** — cash, customers, retention, capacity, or clarity?
2. **Current offer** — who it is for, price, promise, and delivery format.
3. **Current numbers** — leads/conversations per week, close rate, CAC if known, gross margin, delivery hours per customer, churn/renewal, capacity.
4. **Bottleneck guess** — no leads, weak closes, delivery overload, cash gap, or unclear priority.

**Exit:** one sentence naming the likely bottleneck and the branch to run.

## Branch selection

| If the bottleneck is... | Run... | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Unclear priority or competing options | Decision branch | Decision memo |
| Not enough qualified conversations | Outreach branch | Sendable campaign |
| Leads exist but buying is weak | Offer branch | Offer test |
| Sales work but delivery gets heavier | Cost-curve branch | Curve-bending experiment |
| Growth feels chaotic | Cadence branch | Weekly operator scoreboard |

When a branch needs a deeper framework, hand off deliberately: `grand-slam-offer` for building one offer end-to-end, `hundred-million-money-models` for a full offer ladder, `hundred-million-leads` for channel strategy, `million-dollar-weekend` for preselling validation, and `e-myth-revisited` for owner-independent operations.

## Decision branch — pick the next move

1. **Name the constraint.** State the one thing most limiting growth now: attention, trust, conversion, cash, fulfillment, retention, or focus.
2. **List 2-4 options plus doing nothing.** Include the cost, upside, risk, and time-to-signal for each.
3. **Classify reversibility.** Two-way doors get 7-day tests. One-way doors need more proof, smaller pilots, or delay.
4. **Score operator fit.** Prefer moves that improve cash quickly, create reusable proof/assets, reduce future workload, or expose a false assumption.
5. **Choose one bet.** Define owner, first action, success metric, stop condition, and review date.

**Exit:** a decision memo with: constraint, chosen move, rejected alternatives, assumptions, 7-day test, success metric, kill criterion.

## Outreach branch — create conversations

Build one tight campaign, not a broad marketing plan.

1. **Narrow the list.** Pick one ICP, one trigger event, and 30-100 named targets. If the user has no list, define how to build the first 30 today.
2. **Pick the pain.** Use a concrete situation the buyer recognizes, not a generic benefit.
3. **Choose the ask.** Default to a low-friction conversation, diagnostic, or specific offer. Avoid vague networking asks.
4. **Draft the sequence.** Produce one opener and 2 follow-ups. Keep it short, specific, and easy to reply to.
5. **Instrument it.** Track target, trigger, message version, send date, reply, booked call, outcome, objection, and next step.
6. **Learning loop.** After 20 sends or 5 replies, revise only one variable: ICP, trigger, pain, proof, or CTA.

**Exit:** a campaign the user can send today: target definition, list source, opener, follow-ups, tracking columns, first-20 send block, and review threshold.

## Offer branch — improve buying economics

Use the offer to raise value and lower selling friction before adding complexity.

1. **Anchor the buyer and outcome.** One ICP, one urgent situation, one measurable result.
2. **Map the current offer.** Price, promise, vehicle, proof, guarantee/risk reversal, bonuses, delivery cost, time-to-value.
3. **Check 30-day payback.** Estimate: 30-day gross profit per customer minus CAC and 30-day cost-to-serve.
4. **Find the weak lever.** Diagnose whether the offer lacks dream outcome, likelihood/proof, speed, ease, risk reversal, urgency, or price logic.
5. **Choose one offer test.** Examples: narrower ICP, stronger promise, faster first win, proof asset, premium tier, setup fee, payment plan, downsell, continuity, referral incentive.
6. **Protect margins.** Do not add high-touch bonuses unless they raise price, conversion, or proof enough to justify delivery cost.

**Exit:** one offer change to test, the before/after economics, the selling script or page section to change, and the metric that decides keep/kill.

## Cost-curve branch — bend scale economics

A cost-curve move reduces marginal effort, marginal cost, or marginal CAC while protecting the result customers bought.

1. **Map the customer path.** Acquire → qualify → close → onboard → deliver → support → renew/expand → refer.
2. **Attach marginal cost.** For each stage, estimate team hours, founder hours, vendor/tool cost, rework, and quality risk per additional customer.
3. **Find the steepest curve.** Pick the stage where one more customer adds the most pain, cost, delay, or failure risk.
4. **Select one bend mechanism:**
   - **Template** repeated work.
   - **Batch** similar work.
   - **Automate** handoffs, reminders, reporting, or data entry.
   - **Delegate** standardized work to a lower-cost role.
   - **Self-serve** education, onboarding, status, or troubleshooting.
   - **Group** delivery where peer context improves value.
   - **Scope** the offer tighter.
   - **Price** for expensive edge cases.
   - **Referralize** proof and introductions.
5. **Protect the value driver.** Never remove the component that creates the promised result or the buyer's confidence in it.
6. **Run a small experiment.** Apply the bend to the next 3-5 customers or next 7 days.

**Exit:** one curve-bending experiment with baseline marginal cost, expected reduction, quality guardrail, owner, and review date.

## Cadence branch — run the weekly operator review

Use this when the business feels noisy. Keep the scoreboard small enough to update weekly.

**Scoreboard:**

- Cash collected
- Qualified conversations
- Offers made
- New customers
- Gross profit per customer
- CAC or time-to-acquire
- Delivery hours per customer
- Time-to-first-value
- Churn/renewal/referrals
- Current bottleneck

Review in order:

1. What improved?
2. What got worse?
3. Which curve is steepening?
4. What is the single bottleneck this week?
5. What 7-day move will change it?

**Exit:** updated scoreboard, one bottleneck, one 7-day move, and one metric to review next week.

## Rules

- One bottleneck at a time.
- Prefer cash and conversations over strategy theatre.
- Prefer 7-day reversible tests over big plans.
- Numbers before vibes; estimates beat silence.
- Do not scale a broken delivery system; fix the delivery curve first.
- Do not cut delivery cost by cutting the thing that creates the customer's result.
- Do not add offers before the current offer's economics are understood.
- Every outreach plan must end in sendable copy and a tracking loop.
- Every offer change must state its expected effect on conversion, gross profit, CAC, or cost-to-serve.
