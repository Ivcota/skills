# Experimentation — Deep Dive

> "Explore Outside Your Comfort Zone." (Ultralearning, ch. XII)

## What it is

Past the basics, learning becomes scientific. You form hypotheses about methods, techniques, and styles; you try them aggressively; you measure results; you discard what doesn't work. Mastery — and originality — require deliberately exploring outside the well-trodden paths that early learners share.

Young opens the chapter with **Vincent van Gogh**, the unlikeliest of artistic icons: a late starter at 26, abrasive personality, no apparent drafting talent, largely self-taught. His pattern was relentless: identify a method or style, pursue it with brutal intensity for dozens or hundreds of works, then — aware of remaining deficiencies — switch to a new method and start over. Young calls this pattern an unintentional parallel to the scientific method: hypothesis → experiment → results → repeat.

Experimentation is the **ninth and final principle**, and Young positions it as the principle that ties all the others together. The right balance between directness and drill, between retention and intuition, between focused practice and exploratory play — none of those balances can be settled by reading a book. They have to be tested in your specific project, against your specific skill, with your specific personality and constraints.

Experimentation is also the bridge between proficiency and originality. Most fields reward people who can do things others can't, not people who can do things others already can. That requires going somewhere the well-trodden path doesn't reach.

## Why it works (the science)

- **Carol Dweck's growth mindset (Stanford).** Dweck distinguishes a **fixed mindset** (traits are innate; no point trying to improve them) from a **growth mindset** (capacities can be actively improved). Young positions the **experimental mindset** as an extension of growth mindset: not just a belief that improvement is possible, but an **active strategy** for exploring how to reach it.
- **Diminishing curriculum support past the basics.** The early stage of any skill has the most shared curriculum because everyone starts in roughly the same place. As skill develops, two novices' trajectories look alike; two experts' trajectories diverge. Past a point, fewer teachers and fewer peers can help, and personalized experimentation becomes the only path.
- **Learning shifts from accumulation to unlearning.** Early learning is adding new facts and procedures. Past the basics, getting better is increasingly about discarding stale habits — and you don't know which habits are stale unless you test alternatives.
- **The high-dimensional volume argument.** A geometric fact: as dimensionality rises, more of a sphere's volume sits near its surface. In **2D, ~20%** of a circle's mass lies in the outer 10% of the radius. In **3D, ~30%** of a sphere's mass. In **10D, ~75%** of the volume is in that outermost shell. By analogy, in a complex skill with many qualitative dimensions, **most viable approaches are extreme on at least one dimension.** Playing it safe in the middle explores almost none of the possibility space. Van Gogh became van Gogh by being extreme along several dimensions at once.
- **Originality rewards exploration.** Great mathematicians solve problems others cannot, not problems already solved. Successful business leaders spot opportunities others miss. Van Gogh was celebrated for skill *and* originality — and originality is what exploration buys you.

## The named tactics

### Copy, Then Create

Start by emulating an exemplar — copy a piece of work you admire, technique for technique. Van Gogh copied Jean-François Millet's *The Sower* repeatedly, and other Millet pieces besides, throughout his career.

Two benefits:

1. **It simplifies the experiment.** Infinite possibility space is paralyzing. Starting from a fixed point gives you a foothold from which to deviate.
2. **It forces deconstruction.** To copy effectively you have to figure out *why* the original works — which often surfaces invisible craft moves and dispels illusions about which aspects mattered.

Use it at the start of a new technique, style, or domain. Don't stay in copy mode forever — the "then create" is load-bearing.

### Compare Methods Side-by-Side

A controlled A/B comparison: same task, same time budget, vary one factor.

Young's own example: learning French vocabulary. Each day for a month he picked 50 new words from his reading. For half he just looked at the dictionary translation. For the other half he constructed a visual keyword-method mnemonic. On a later test with randomly picked words from each pile, the mnemonic recall came in at **almost twice the rate** of the look-it-up recall.

Two advantages of split tests:
- You get cleaner information about what actually works for *you*, because you've controlled the obvious confounds.
- You broaden your skill in the process — running both methods makes you competent in both.

Use it when you have a real choice between two plausible methods and the cost of doing both is bearable. Don't use it on trivial method choices where the answer doesn't matter.

### Introduce New Constraints

Add a restriction that makes your usual routine impossible. The Year Without English was this — no English from day one forced a method neither Young nor Vatsal Jaiswal would have chosen otherwise. Designers know this axiomatically: "the best innovations come from working within constraints."

Concrete constraint ideas Young's framework implies:
- "No notes, retrieve from memory."
- "One pomodoro, no resources."
- "Use only the 1,000 most common words."
- "Solve this without that particular technique."
- "Ship something every day for a month."

Use it when you suspect you're stuck in a routine groove. Test: if you can predict in advance what your next session will look like in detail, you probably need a constraint.

### Find Your Superpower in the Hybrid of Unrelated Skills

Combine two skills neither of which you're world-class in, but whose intersection nobody else occupies. **Scott Adams**, creator of *Dilbert*, attributes his success to combining engineering + MBA + cartoonist. He wasn't the best engineer, the best businessperson, or the best cartoonist — but the intersection was an open lane.

Young notes this plays out across multiple ultralearning projects, not within one. After the MIT Challenge he could write scripts that auto-generated Chinese flash cards because he had the programming. The synergy compounds.

The implication for project planning: don't always pick the skill where you can climb fastest in isolation. Sometimes pick the skill that pairs with one you already have.

### Explore the Extremes

Push some dimension of the skill to an extreme — even if you eventually pull it back to moderate. Most of the option space in a multi-dimensional skill lies far from the middle (the high-dimensional volume argument above).

Van Gogh's signature: extreme on multiple dimensions at once. Thick impasto application of paint vs. Renaissance glazes. Rapid brushwork vs. careful strokes. Bold, often garish complementary colors vs. subtle muted tones. The middle would have made him a forgettable competent painter.

Use it when you have plateaued, or when the field rewards distinctiveness. Even if you decide an extreme doesn't suit you, the trip gave you information you can only get by going there.

## Anchor story

**Vincent van Gogh** — late starter, no obvious drafting talent, abrasive personality, mostly self-taught.

The biographical pattern Young uses:

- **Late start, age 26**, after failing as an art dealer and as a preacher. A family friend, H. G. Tersteeg, told him "you started too late . . . of one thing I am sure, you are no artist."
- **Crude initial drafting.** A classmate at the Parisian atelier where he briefly studied: "We considered his work too unskillful. His drawings had nothing remarkable about them." He left after less than three months.
- **Home-study courses, page by page.** Charles Bargue's *Exercices au fusain* (Charcoal Exercises) and *Cours de dessin* (Drawing Course), plus Armand Cassagne's *Guide de l'alphabet du dessin*. "I have now finished all sixty sheets," he wrote his brother Theo. "I worked almost a whole fortnight, from early morning until night."
- **Copying.** Millet's *The Sower* he copied over and over.
- **Method experiments through mentors.** Anthon van Rappard convinced him to try reed pen and ink and short fast strokes. Anton Mauve persuaded him to try charcoal, chalk, watercolor, Conté crayon. Paul Gauguin pushed him toward painting from memory, muted colors, and new materials — those didn't stick.
- **Philosophical experiments.** Started convinced of muted gray tones — "scarcely any color is not gray, in nature one really sees nothing else but those tones or shades" — as in *The Potato Eaters*. Later flipped to bright, bold, complementary colors imposed onto a scene. Same posture toward art movements: first traditional over Impressionist, later avant-garde over verisimilitude.
- **Intensity inside each experiment.** Sometimes a new painting every day. Variation paired with aggressive production.
- **Death at 37.** Bullet wound to the stomach; suspected suicide, but his biographers Naifeh and Smith consider accident or foul play more likely. On four separate occasions a van Gogh has become the most expensive painting ever sold.

Young's read: **two factors enabled the breakthrough — variation and aggressive exploration.** Without variation, he'd have failed at one style and quit. Without aggressive production inside each variation, he'd have stayed a dilettante. He was both at once.

## Worksheets / templates

### A/B experiment-design template

Use this when you have a real choice between two plausible methods. Modeled on Young's French vocabulary mnemonic experiment.

```
EXPERIMENT NAME: _______________________________________________

HYPOTHESIS (one sentence, falsifiable):
  "Method A will outperform Method B on [specific outcome] by
   at least [magnitude]."

VARIATION:
  Method A: __________________________________________________
  Method B: __________________________________________________
  (Vary only ONE factor between them. Same time budget, same
   material, same test conditions.)

MEASUREMENT:
  What outcome am I measuring? _______________________________
  How will I measure it? _____________________________________
  When will I measure it? ____________________________________
  (Young's example: 50 words/day for a month, randomly sampled
   recall test at the end.)

DECISION CRITERION (commit BEFORE running):
  "I will adopt Method A if ________________________________.
   I will adopt Method B if ________________________________.
   I will reject both and try a third if __________________."

DURATION: ______________________________________________________
  (Long enough for real signal, short enough to act on the result.)

RESULT:
  Date completed: _____________
  Outcome: ____________________________________________________
  Decision: ___________________________________________________
```

Run at least one A/B experiment per ultralearning project. Don't run more than a few at a time — running too many simultaneously confounds the signal.

### Three Levels diagnostic

The three levels of experimentation, in order of skill maturity. Identify which level you're at now and which tactic fits.

| Level | Question | When it applies | Fit tactics |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Resources** | Which book, class, or method do I use? | Beginner. Curricula widely shared; choice is real. | Pick a resource, apply rigorously for a fixed period, then evaluate. Copy, Then Create. |
| 2. **Technique** | Which subtopic, which approach do I pursue next? | Intermediate. Many directions open; not all matter equally. | Pick a subtopic, learn aggressively, evaluate. Compare Methods Side-by-Side. Introduce New Constraints. |
| 3. **Style** | What voice, aesthetic, signature do I develop? | Mastery. No single right answer; originality rewarded. | Study masters, dissect what makes them work, emulate then deviate. Explore the Extremes. Find Your Superpower in the Hybrid. |

Walk down the table from your current level. If you can't answer the question for your level yet, you may be one level earlier than you think.

### Variation + intensity audit (the van Gogh pattern)

For your current project:

- **What method am I using right now?** ________________________
- **For how long have I been using it without varying anything?** ___________
- **What's the next method I could try if this one stops yielding?** ____________
- **Am I producing aggressively inside this method, or just dabbling?** ____________
  - Concrete proxy: how many finished outputs in the last week? _______
- **What would the extreme version of this approach look like?** ______________
- **Have I run at least one A/B comparison in the last month?** [ ] yes / [ ] no

If the answers add up to "same method for a long time, low output, no extremes tested, no A/B," you are in the comfort-zone trap the chapter is written against.

## Anti-patterns specific to this principle

- **A flurry of random activity instead of experimentation.** "Trying lots of stuff" without a hypothesis, controlled variation, or honest evaluation isn't experiment — it's dabbling. Real experiments have a question and a decision criterion.
- **Experimenting without intensity.** Van Gogh's lesson is that variation + intensity is the unit, not variation alone. Without the aggressive production phase, you never learn whether the method actually worked. Trying ten methods half-heartedly tells you nothing.
- **Locking into the first method that works.** The challenge of late-stage learning is that you think you already know what to do. Early success makes the method feel mandatory; it usually isn't.
- **Playing it safe in the middle.** The geometric argument is uncomfortable but real — most viable approaches are extreme on at least one dimension. The middle explores a tiny slice of the option space.
- **Confusing growth mindset with experimentation.** Believing improvement is possible isn't the same as having a plan. Growth mindset is the precondition; experimental mindset is the active strategy.
- **Refusing to discard methods that failed.** Young's failed Chinese mnemonic system with colored tones — total failure, openly reported. Experiments must be allowed to fail honestly; that's most of their value.

## When to push this principle vs. when to relax it

**Push experimentation hard when:**

- You've plateaued. Same method, no movement, no new insights.
- You've finished the basics and the curriculum stops offering you a clear next step.
- The field rewards originality (art, design, research, writing, entrepreneurship).
- You're working at a high enough level that no one ahead of you has your exact combination of strengths.
- Multiple competing methods are plausible and you don't have data to choose between them. Run an A/B.

**Relax experimentation when:**

- You are early in the skill and the well-trodden curriculum is genuinely the right path. Premature experimentation is a way to avoid the boring early work.
- You're inside a high-intensity production burst (van Gogh's pattern alternates exploration with concentrated production — don't break the production phase to try a new method).
- Time is short and stakes on the current method are low. The cost of experimenting may exceed the upside.
- You're using "I'm experimenting" as a cover for not finishing anything. Experimentation without commitment is dilettantism. Pair every variation with an aggressive production phase before evaluating.

**The chapter's deepest warning:** "The challenge of learning in the beginning is that you don't know what to do. The challenge of learning in the end is that you think you already know what to do." Both failure modes are real. The cure for the first is curriculum; the cure for the second is experimentation.
