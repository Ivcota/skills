# Intuition — Deep Dive

> "Dig Deep Before Building Up." (Ultralearning, ch. XI)

## What it is

Intuition — the apparently magical ability to see solutions immediately — is not innate magic. It's the product of an enormous library of organized, deeply understood examples in long-term memory, paired with a relentless refusal to fake understanding.

Young opens with Richard Feynman, who his colleague Mark Kac called "a magician of the highest caliber." Feynman walked out of the Putnam exam early and won. He computed the cube root of 1,729.03 faster than an abacus salesman. He broke into Manhattan Project filing cabinets as a joke. The book's project is to show that these feats had **method, not magic** behind them, and that the method is teachable.

The chapter's title is its method statement: dig deep before building up. Don't accumulate a wide thin layer of "understood" material and hope it congeals. Force every piece of knowledge through enough re-derivation, concrete examples, and dumb questions that it actually becomes yours.

Intuition is the most cognitive-feeling of the nine principles — and the one with the most countercultural posture. The social pressure not to look stupid is the main thing standing between most learners and the kind of understanding Feynman had.

## Why it works (the science)

- **Chi et al. — physics problem categorization study.** Advanced PhDs and undergraduate physics students sorted problems into categories. Beginners sorted by surface features (pulleys, inclined planes). Experts sorted by underlying principle ("this is a conservation of energy problem"). Experts could start with the right approach; novices needed trial and error.
- **Chess-chunks study.** Masters re-create real game positions on a blank board in **chunks** — several pieces at a time, recognized as a pattern. Researchers estimate **~50,000 stored chunks** to reach expert status. On randomized boards (positions that never arise in real games) the expert advantage vanishes — the chunks come from real play, not from "better memory."
- **Levels-of-processing effect.** In one cited study, participants reviewing a word list either judged each word for pleasantness (deep processing of meaning) or scanned for the letter "e" (shallow processing). Half in each group were told the review was for a test. The motivation manipulation made no difference. The processing depth nearly doubled retention. *How* you think about information matters more than *how long* or *how motivated*.
- **Rebecca Lawson — illusion of explanatory depth.** Asked participants to draw a bicycle (seat, handles, tires, pedals, chain). Most failed badly, even though they ride bicycles all the time and rated their understanding high beforehand. Repeat with a can opener if a bicycle is too easy. Young uses this as a thought experiment in the chapter.
- **Dunning-Kruger effect.** The less you know about a subject, the less you can assess your own knowledge of it. Asking more dumb questions correlates with knowing more, not less.
- **Feynman's own account.** He understood things by re-deriving rather than reading along. When he first encountered Lee and Yang's work he thought it was too complicated; his sister observed that the problem wasn't comprehension, it was that he hadn't invented it. He went back and worked through it and it wasn't actually so hard.

The Chi study and the chess-chunks study together build the chapter's core claim: **intuition is the surface behavior of a deep, well-organized pattern library.** Bounded by the patterns you've stored — Feynman tripped on counterintuitive theorems from pure math because the patterns he had came from physics — but within its domain, the library produces what looks like magic.

## The named tactics

### The Struggle Timer

When you feel like giving up on a hard problem, set a timer for an additional **ten minutes** and push past the urge. Two benefits:

1. Often you solve it. The "I can't do this" feeling clusters at moments of frustration that are not actually the same as "the problem is impossible."
2. Even if you fail, the struggle primes later recall — the desirable-difficulty effect from the retrieval chapter applies. You'll remember the solution far better when you finally see it.

Feynman's own posture, from the radio-fixing era of his childhood: "If [the owner] had said, 'Never mind, it's too much work,' I'd have blown my top, because I want to beat this damn thing, as long as I've gone this far."

**Contraindication.** The struggle timer is not "thrash for an hour because you didn't research." If you've timed out three times on the same wall, the bottleneck is probably a missing prerequisite, not a missing minute of effort. Drop back, learn it, return.

### Proof-driven understanding

Rather than reading along with someone else's derivation, re-derive it yourself. Feynman did this with Lee and Yang. Einstein, as a child, proved the Pythagorean Theorem from similar triangles. The cost: you sometimes reinvent existing work. The benefit: what you "understand" is actually yours.

Use it when the result matters and you'll need to extend it. Skip it when the result is incidental scaffolding you'll never have to defend.

### Concrete-example-first

When following an abstract argument, **mentally construct a specific example** before the speaker finishes. Update the example as new conditions are added. If the final claim doesn't fit your example, either you misunderstood or the claim is wrong.

Feynman: "As they're telling me the conditions of the theorem, I construct something which fits all the conditions. You know, you have a set (one ball)—disjoint (two balls). Then the balls turn colors, grow hairs, or whatever, in my head as they put more conditions on. Finally they state the theorem, which is some dumb thing about the ball which isn't true for my hairy green ball thing, so I say, 'False!'"

This also doubles as a self-feedback mechanism: if you can't construct any concrete example for a concept, that's strong evidence you don't understand it yet.

### "Dumb" Questions

Ask the questions that everyone in the room is assuming have obvious answers. "Is a cathode plus or minus?" "Why does this step follow from the previous one?" Feynman asked them deliberately. His Brazilian students complained when he asked questions they thought they all knew — and then it turned out none of them actually did.

Two reasons this works. First, dumb questions surface hidden gaps in *your* understanding before they become invisible foundations under more advanced material. Second, the not-so-obvious implications of obvious-seeming answers are often where insight lives.

**Social cost is the binding constraint.** Most learners know this works and refuse to do it because they don't want to look stupid. Young's framing — and Feynman's — is that the price of looking stupid for ten seconds is much smaller than the price of fooling yourself for a year.

### The Feynman Technique

Young's signature distilled method. Three steps:

1. Write the concept or problem you want to understand at the **top of a sheet of paper**.
2. In the space below, **explain it in plain language as if teaching someone who hasn't heard of it.**
   - If it's a concept, write it for a beginner.
   - If it's a problem, walk through the solution step by step, and — crucially — explain *why* each step makes sense.
3. **When you get stuck**, the stuck point is the gap. Go back to your book, notes, or reference; fill the gap; return to your explanation.

The technique works because it forces articulation. Most of our "understanding" is never put into words, which is why the illusion of explanatory depth survives. Writing it out kills the illusion fast.

Three variants Young uses:

- **Variant A — For things you don't understand at all.** Keep the book in hand; go back and forth between your explanation and the source. You lose the retrieval-practice benefit, but you survive the wall. Young's example: photogrammetry, in a machine vision class during the MIT Challenge.
- **Variant B — For problems you can't seem to solve.** Walk through the problem step by step *alongside* the explanation you generate. Don't summarize — summarizing skips the hard part. Young's example: grid acceleration in a computer graphics class; he drew a snowman with rays shooting from an eyeball-camera.
- **Variant C — For expanding intuition on important ideas.** Don't focus on explaining every detail. Focus on **generating illustrative examples, analogies, and visualizations**, as if you're being paid to write a magazine article for a layperson. Young's example: voltage in electromagnetism, which he eventually mapped as "troughs of water at different heights" — batteries as pumps, resistors as hoses.

## Anchor story

**Richard Feynman.** Nobel laureate, lock-picker, abacus-beater, "magician of the highest caliber" per Mark Kac.

The biographical facts Young uses to demystify the magic:

- IQ measured at 125 — only modestly above the college-graduate average of 115.
- College grades in history were in the bottom fifth of his class. Literature in the bottom sixth. Fine arts worse than 93% of his peers. He once cheated on a test to pass a humanities course.
- His cube-root-of-1,729.03 trick relied on knowing that **1,728 cubic inches = 1 cubic foot** and a calculus interpolation rule for small fractions ("the cube root's excess is one-third of the number's excess").
- His "e to the power of 1.4" feat used the fact that he had memorized **log of 2 to the base e = 0.69315** (from radioactivity / half-life work), so he knew e^0.7 ≈ 2 and could compose.
- His lock-picking was practiced rhythm — 400 possible back numbers in under half an hour, average safe time of four hours.
- The French curve story: Feynman tricked classmates into thinking a French curve was "special" because its bottom was tangent to a horizontal line — an elementary fact of calculus, true of any smooth shape, that they should have spotted instantly. They had memorized procedures without relating them outside the textbook.

The lesson: Feynman possessed gifts but also an enormous library of stored patterns, an aggressive method of re-derivation, and a willingness to ask dumb questions. The magic is the **method**, applied with relentless playful intensity.

## Worksheets / templates

### Feynman Technique template (fillable, all three variants)

```
Concept / problem: _________________________________________

WHO am I explaining this to?  [ ] Total beginner  [ ] Bright high-schooler  [ ] My past self before this project

EXPLANATION (in plain language, step by step, no jargon):

  1. ___________________________________________________
  2. ___________________________________________________
  3. ___________________________________________________
  ...

  When you reach a sentence where you'd hand-wave, slow down.
  When you reach a sentence you can't finish — STOP. That's the gap.

GAP I HIT (Variant A or B):
  Where did I get stuck? ____________________________________
  What did I look up to fix it? _____________________________
  How did I rewrite the explanation? ________________________

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE (required for Variant C, optional for A/B):
  Concrete situation: ______________________________________
  Analogy or visualization: ________________________________
  (Young's voltage example: troughs of water at different heights,
   batteries as pumps, resistors as hoses.)

CHECK: Could a stranger read this and understand the idea?
  [ ] Yes  [ ] Not yet — what's still missing?
```

Run this once for each high-leverage concept in your subject. The high-leverage list is short — usually fewer than ten per chapter or unit. Don't Feynman-technique every footnote.

### Struggle-timer protocol

When you hit "I want to give up":

1. **Name what you're stuck on** in one sentence. Write it down. ("I don't see why this step follows from the previous one.")
2. **Set a timer for 10 minutes.**
3. **Push.** Try concrete examples. Try a dumb question. Try re-deriving the previous step. Try drawing it.
4. **At the buzzer, decide:**
   - Solved → write the resolution next to the original sentence.
   - Progress → reset the timer for another 10 minutes (max two more rounds).
   - Wall, with no progress → drop back one level: what prerequisite am I missing? Add it to the queue, move on, return.
5. **Log the wall.** Even if you couldn't solve it, the act of struggling primes recall. Note where you got stuck — that's a high-priority entry point for the next session.

### Dumb-questions audit

Before any teaching, presentation, or major exam, ask yourself the five most obvious-seeming questions about the subject. If you can't answer all five crisply, you have an illusion-of-explanatory-depth problem in exactly that spot.

Example prompts:
- "Why is this true?"
- "How would I draw this if I had to?"
- "What's the simplest case where this matters?"
- "What changes if I remove this assumption?"
- "What's the dumbest thing someone could ask, and what's the answer?"

## Anti-patterns specific to this principle

- **Nodding along while reading.** Feynman's failure mode on Lee and Yang until his sister called him out. "Understood" should mean you can demonstrate it yourself.
- **Pattern-matching without understanding ("brittle" learning).** Memorizing solutions that fall apart on the slightest variation. The French curve classmates were brittle.
- **Avoiding dumb questions to look smart.** The social cost is small; the cost of fooling yourself is large. Feynman explicitly asked them.
- **Confusing fluency for understanding.** You can re-read a passage and feel like you know it (that's the Judgments-of-Learning failure from ch. VIII). Articulating the idea — the Feynman Technique — is the cure.
- **Stopping at the first plausible-sounding explanation.** Especially in soft fields; Feynman attacked "philosophical gobbledygook" by reading one sentence at a time until it either made sense or revealed itself as fluff.
- **Building up before digging deep.** Layering new material on top of shaky understanding accelerates collapse. The chapter's title is a load-bearing instruction: dig deep first.

## When to push this principle vs. when to relax it

**Push intuition hard when:**

- The skill rewards transfer — you'll need to handle problems that differ from the textbook examples.
- The domain has a small number of foundational ideas that everything else hangs off (calculus, physics, programming concepts, statistical reasoning). Intuition on the few foundations pays compounding dividends.
- You'll be teaching, debugging, or extending the material — anywhere the work is non-routine.
- A piece of material keeps feeling slippery despite multiple exposures. That's a Feynman Technique signal.

**Relax intuition when:**

- The material is genuinely arbitrary and there's no underlying principle to recover. (Vocabulary, regulatory minutiae, list of countries by capital.) Retention + retrieval are the right tools, not intuition.
- The cost of re-deriving is large and the result will only be used once.
- You're in the volume phase of a skill — get reps in first, then deepen the cores that turn out to matter.
- Time is critically short and "good enough to use" is sufficient (a one-off exam, a tight deadline). Acknowledge you're trading depth for speed; plan to come back if the skill matters long-term.

The chapter's deeper warning: don't relax intuition by *default*. Most learners under-invest here because the work is uncomfortable, not because they correctly judged it unnecessary.
