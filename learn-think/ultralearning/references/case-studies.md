# Case Studies — 24 Named Ultralearners

These are the named exemplars Scott H. Young uses in *Ultralearning* (HarperBusiness, 2019). Each entry pulls goal, method, timeframe, and lesson directly from the source — quoted where the wording matters, paraphrased where not. Citations point to the chapter (or Appendix / Foreword) where the case appears.

---

## Scott Young's flagship projects

### The MIT Challenge (Oct 2011 – Sept 2012)
**Goal:** "Learn the material taught in MIT's undergraduate curriculum for computer science, using their freely provided materials and used textbooks." (Appendix)
**Method:** Pass all final exams (>50% unless otherwise specified) and complete the programming projects via OpenCourseWare; benchmark intensity against an actual MIT student where possible; substitute lab classes with theory equivalents and humanities with self-gradable economics. (Appendix)
**Timeframe:** ~12 months. (Appendix)
**Principles in play:** Metalearning, Focus, Directness, Retrieval. (Ch. I, XIII)
**What it shows:** A full self-built curriculum, executed in roughly 25% the calendar time of a normal degree, is possible when you plan ahead, schedule aggressively, and grade yourself honestly. Young later re-graded under a stricter rubric and found 6 of 33 classes would have flipped to fail — he documented this rather than hide it. (Appendix)

### The Year Without English (Sept 2013 – Aug 2014)
**Goal:** "Learn Spanish, Portuguese, Mandarin Chinese, and Korean." (Appendix)
**Method:** Three months each in Spain, Brazil, China, and South Korea; no English from day one; ~25–50 hours of Pimsleur + flashcard prep per language before arrival (~100 hours for Mandarin). Done with Vatsal Jaiswal. (Appendix)
**Timeframe:** 12 months. (Appendix)
**Principles in play:** Directness, Focus, Experimentation. (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** Directness is non-negotiable for language. Outcomes: B2 Spanish, slightly weaker Portuguese, HSK 4 Mandarin, lower-intermediate Korean — accuracy on level claims matters as much as the project itself. (Appendix)

### Portrait Drawing Challenge (July 2016)
**Goal:** "To improve my ability to draw faces realistically." (Appendix)
**Method:** Rapid feedback — drawing quick sketches and overlaying them on semi-transparent reference photos; supplemented by Betty Edwards's *Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain* and Vitruvian Studio's Portrait Drawing class. (Appendix)
**Timeframe:** One month, ~100 hours. (Appendix)
**Principles in play:** Feedback (rapid, comparative), Drill, Directness. (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** A one-month sprint with a tight feedback loop can produce visible skill in a domain Young had no background in. The overlay-photo technique is the canonical example of engineered fast feedback.

## Polyglots and language learners

### Benny Lewis
**Accomplishment:** Irish polyglot behind "Fluent in 3 Months"; speaks Spanish, Italian, Gaelic, French, Portuguese, Esperanto, German (attempted C2), plus conversational Arabic, Hungarian, Mandarin, Thai, ASL, and Klingon. (Ch. I, II)
**Signature method:** Speak from day one with a phrasebook and visual mnemonics; aim at B2 in three months. Always have a self-imposed challenge running. (Ch. I, II)
**Principles illustrated:** Directness, Focus, Experimentation. (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** Direct practice beats classroom prep — but Lewis's *failure* to hit C2 in German is also instructive: he "spent too much time listening to the radio" instead of active listening drills. Hours don't equal practice. (Ch. I)

### Tamu (Chinese-forums.com user)
**Accomplishment:** Passed HSK 5 (China's second-highest Mandarin proficiency exam) in four months from scratch. (Ch. I)
**Signature method:** 70–80+ hours per week of self-imposed study, with deliberately harsh deadlines. (Ch. I)
**Principles illustrated:** Focus, Drill. (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** Intensity has a top end most learners never approach. Tamu's case is the upper bound — a full-time, unstructured commitment that still produced an externally verifiable result.

### Nigel Richards
**Accomplishment:** New Zealand engineer; multi-time World Scrabble Champion in English, who then won the French World Scrabble Championship — without speaking French. (Ch. X)
**Signature method:** Reads long word lists and mentally rehearses them for hours while cycling. (Ch. X)
**Principles illustrated:** Retention (active recall plus spaced rehearsal in a non-trivial form). (Ch. X)
**What it shows:** Retention can be ruthless and procedural when the task is procedural. Richards is the extreme-case study for memory without comprehension.

## Self-directed academic learners

### Roger Craig
**Accomplishment:** Won a then-record $77,000 single-day on *Jeopardy!* and nearly $200,000 over a five-game streak. (Ch. I)
**Signature method:** Text-mined the entire Jeopardy! question archive, used data visualization to find his weak topics (e.g., fashion), and drilled them with spaced-repetition flashcards built on Piotr Woźniak's algorithm. (Ch. I)
**Principles illustrated:** Metalearning, Drill, Retrieval, Retention. (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** "You can practice haphazardly, or you can practice efficiently." (Craig, quoted in Ch. I) The ultralearner's edge is finding the rate-limiting step and engineering practice against exactly that.

### Diana Jaunzeikare
**Accomplishment:** Self-replicated a Carnegie Mellon computational linguistics PhD — including original research, not just coursework — while keeping her full-time Google job. (Ch. I)
**Signature method:** Used CMU's doctoral curriculum as her external benchmark; ran the project part-time. (Ch. I)
**Principles illustrated:** Self-direction, Metalearning. (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** Ultralearning need not be full-time. It can run on the side for years if the metalearning research front-loads the structure a degree would otherwise impose.

### Steve Pavlina
**Accomplishment:** Took a triple course load at Cal State Northridge and finished CS + math degrees in three semesters — pre-OpenCourseWare. (Ch. I)
**Signature method:** Compressed schedule with extreme focus. (Ch. I)
**Principles illustrated:** Focus, Metalearning. (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** One of Young's early inspirations; demonstrates that institutional structure can be accelerated, not just bypassed.

## Domain pivots and career transitions

### Vatsal Jaiswal
**Accomplishment:** Architecture grad facing the post-2008 recession with no offers; built a portfolio of a single three-tower residential building, taught himself Revit, then submitted to two firms — both offered him jobs. (Ch. VI)
**Signature method:** Took a print-shop job specifically to study real blueprints; worked on the actual artifact (a building project) instead of generic exercises. (Ch. VI)
**Principles illustrated:** Directness. (Ch. VI)
**What it shows:** The canonical Directness case — practice the real task, not the textbook proxy. Reading design theory is not drafting in Revit.

### Colby Durant
**Accomplishment:** Web-development employee who ran an ultralearning project on copywriting, then used it to win a promotion. (Ch. II)
**Signature method:** Skill-stacked an ultralearning project onto an existing job. (Ch. II)
**Principles illustrated:** Metalearning, Directness. (Ch. II)
**What it shows:** "Accelerate the career you have" use case — ultralearning as internal-mobility tool.

### Vishal Maini
**Accomplishment:** Tech marketer who ran a ~6-month ultralearning project on AI research, then switched into the field. (Ch. II)
**Signature method:** Structured project as a deliberate career transition. (Ch. II)
**Principles illustrated:** Metalearning (career-transition variant). (Ch. II)
**What it shows:** "Transition to a new career" use case — ultralearning replacing a return-to-grad-school move.

### Diana Fehsenfeld
**Accomplishment:** New Zealand librarian facing job obsolescence; ran two ultralearning projects — statistics + R, then data visualization — to become indispensable amid government cutbacks. (Ch. II, IV)
**Signature method:** Two sequential projects against an instrumental motivation. (Ch. II)
**Principles illustrated:** Metalearning (instrumental). (Ch. II, IV)
**What it shows:** "Cultivate a hidden advantage" use case — ultralearning as defensive moat-building in a role being eroded by technology.

## Solo creators

### Eric Barone
**Accomplishment:** Solo-developed *Stardew Valley* over ~5 years — programming, pixel art, music, sound, story, design. Released February 2016; 3M+ copies sold in the first year; Forbes "30 Under 30." (Ch. I)
**Signature method:** Worked a minimum-wage theater-usher job to fund the project; learned pixel art "completely from scratch" and redid character portraits 10+ times. (Ch. I)
**Principles illustrated:** Directness, Drill, Experimentation, Feedback (selective self-critique against work he admired). (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** Multi-skill ultralearning on the real artifact. Each redo of a portrait was a drill cycle against a feedback loop he ran himself.

### Trent Fowler
**Accomplishment:** STEMpunk Project — a one-year self-built curriculum spanning computation, robotics, AI, and engineering. (Ch. I)
**Signature method:** Modular hands-on project units instead of formal classes. (Ch. I)
**Principles illustrated:** Directness, Experimentation. (Ch. I)
**What it shows:** When the territory is too wide for a single discipline, break it into project-shaped modules, not class-shaped ones.

### James Clear (Foreword)
**Accomplishment:** Bought a camera in Scotland in 2009 and "took over 100,000 photos that first year"; became a finalist for Travel Photographer of the Year for an aurora borealis image. Later self-taught writing and entrepreneurship en route to *Atomic Habits*. (Foreword)
**Signature method:** No class, no book — sheer volume of active practice. (Foreword)
**Principles illustrated:** Directness, Drill, Feedback. Clear explicitly maps his writing career to principles 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6. (Foreword)
**What it shows:** "Passive learning creates knowledge. Active practice creates skill." (Clear, Foreword)

## Coached and coach-side projects

### Tristan de Montebello (+ Michael Gendler)
**Accomplishment:** Musician/entrepreneur recruited as Young's "guinea pig" *before* the project; in under 7 months went from near-zero public speaking experience to top-10 finalist at the Toastmasters World Championship of Public Speaking. Later co-founded UltraSpeaking with his coach Michael Gendler. (Ch. III)
**Signature method:** Recorded and analyzed every speech; delivered the same speech in radically different styles (angry, monotone, rap); used middle-school audiences for "ruthless feedback"; trained in improv and theater for stage presence. Gendler's signature push: when asked which version of a task to do, "do whichever was scariest for him." (Ch. III)
**Principles illustrated:** All nine; especially Feedback, Drill, Experimentation. (Ch. III)
**What it shows:** A coach amplifies every principle. The famous Gendler note to de Montebello — "Make me care" — captures what high-quality feedback looks like.

## Historical exemplars

### Dan Everett
**Accomplishment:** Linguist; can begin learning a new language in 30 minutes using Kenneth Pike's "monolingual fieldwork" demonstration; one of only a handful of outsiders fluent in Pirahã, an Amazonian language. (Ch. IV)
**Principles illustrated:** Metalearning ("first draw a map"). (Ch. IV)
**What it shows:** The metalearning move — knowing how a domain is structured before diving in — is itself a meta-skill that compounds across projects.

### Mary Somerville
**Accomplishment:** 18th–19th-century Scottish polymath; first woman (with Caroline Herschel) elected to the Royal Astronomical Society; translated and expanded Laplace's *Traité de mécanique céleste*. (Ch. V)
**Signature method:** Worked through Euclid mentally at night when denied a candle. Could leave and resume a difficult problem "like putting a mark into a book." (Ch. V)
**Principles illustrated:** Focus. (Ch. V)
**What it shows:** Deep focus is trainable to the point that it becomes resumable — context can be reloaded into working memory at will.

### Benjamin Franklin
**Accomplishment:** Self-taught writing through component drills — reconstructing essays from memory, rewriting them in verse and back, scrambling and reordering arguments — to fix specific weaknesses his father had pointed out. Foundation of his later success as writer, scientist, statesman. (Ch. VII)
**Principles illustrated:** Drill. (Ch. VII)
**What it shows:** The drill principle in its purest form — break the skill into components, attack the weakest one, then reassemble.

### Srinivasa Ramanujan
**Accomplishment:** Self-taught Indian mathematician working from G. S. Carr's *Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure and Applied Mathematics* — a list of theorems *without proofs*, which forced him to derive every result himself. Produced extraordinary mathematical intuition. (Ch. VIII)
**Principles illustrated:** Retrieval, Intuition. (Ch. VIII)
**What it shows:** Constraint as feature, not bug. A textbook with proofs would have produced a competent learner; one without proofs produced a mathematician.

### Chris Rock
**Accomplishment:** Tests new material unannounced at small clubs like NYC's Comedy Cellar, working from hand-scribbled cards. (Ch. IX)
**Signature method:** Deliberately understates his own ability so audiences won't laugh out of celebrity-deference — he wants signal, not noise. (Ch. IX)
**Principles illustrated:** Feedback (filtering signal from noise). (Ch. IX)
**What it shows:** Feedback quality depends on the conditions you stage. The ethical move is to *suppress* flattering signal so the real one can come through.

### Richard Feynman
**Accomplishment:** Nobel-winning physicist; built deep intuition by re-deriving results himself rather than nodding along, refusing to call something "understood" until he could reconstruct it. IQ measured at 125 — not extreme — but his pattern library and dogged problem-solving produced what colleagues called "magic." (Ch. XI)
**Principles illustrated:** Intuition. (Ch. XI)
**What it shows:** "Understood" must mean you can demonstrate it yourself. Feynman initially thought Lee and Yang's papers were too complex — turned out he was just afraid to work through them.

### Vincent van Gogh
**Accomplishment:** Started painting at 26 with no obvious talent; produced an enormous body of work in roughly a decade. (Ch. XII)
**Signature method:** Aggressive experimentation — Bargue exercises, copying Millet, reed pen via Rappard, varied media via Mauve, painting-from-memory via Gauguin (which didn't stick). Massive volume per method tried. (Ch. XII)
**Principles illustrated:** Experimentation. (Ch. XII)
**What it shows:** Mastery is reached by being extreme along several dimensions, not by sticking to the middle. Methods that don't stick are still informative.

### Judit Polgár (and László + Klára Polgár)
**Accomplishment:** Youngest-ever chess grandmaster, beating Bobby Fischer's record by a month; peaked at world #8; only woman ever to compete in the World Chess Championship; defeated Kasparov in rapid chess, Moscow 2002. Raised under her father László's experiment to "manufacture geniuses." (Ch. XIV)
**Signature method (the Polgárs' system):** Early specialization; play-not-coercion; positive reinforcement; intense direct practice. László was "entirely against coercing learning" — discipline had to come from the daughters themselves. (Ch. XIV)
**Principles illustrated:** All nine, applied educator-side. (Ch. XIV)
**What it shows:** The 24th case is a family unit, not an individual. The Polgár experiment demonstrates that the principles generalize to *teaching* others — but only when the learner remains in the driver's seat.

## Coverage note

This roster covers all 24 named exemplars in the case-study catalog: Young's 3 flagship projects + 3 polyglots/language learners + 3 self-directed academics + 4 domain pivots + 3 solo creators + 1 coached pair + 8 historical exemplars (Polgárs counted as one family unit). Terence Tao appears in the book as a *counter-example* used to frame the talent-vs-method question (Ch. II) — he is not an ultralearning exemplar and is therefore not included here.
