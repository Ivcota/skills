# Drill — Deep Dive

> "Take care of the bars and the piece will take care of itself." — Philip Johnston, epigraph (Ultralearning, ch. VII)

## What it is

Drill is the deliberate isolation and intensive practice of the sub-component of a skill that most limits your overall performance. Young borrows the **rate-determining step** concept from chemistry: in a multi-step reaction, the slowest step controls the speed of the whole. Learning works the same way — one component (your arithmetic in math, your vocabulary in a foreign language, your sense of rhetorical order in writing) is the bottleneck, and fixing that one thing buys outsized returns on the whole skill.

Drill sits in productive tension with Principle 3 (Directness). Directness pulls toward practicing the whole skill in its real context; drill pulls toward decomposing it and practicing one piece in isolation. The way Young resolves the tension is by cycling: **Direct → Drill → Direct again**. The first direct pass tells you where the bottleneck lives. You then carve off the rate-limiting component, drill it without the cognitive load of the surrounding skill, and re-integrate by going back to whole-skill practice.

Drill is uncomfortable because it forces you to attack what you're worst at, not polish what you're good at. Young notes that when most of us "engage in drills, it's often in subjects where we feel competent and comfortable." Real drilling, by contrast, "requires the learner not only to think deeply about what is being learned but also figure out what is most difficult and attack that weakness directly."

## Why it works (the science)

- **Rate-determining step (chemistry analogy).** "Certain aspects of the learning problem [form] a bottleneck that controls the speed at which you can become more proficient overall." Improve the bottleneck, you improve the whole.
- **Cognitive load.** Complex skills spread your attention, memory, and effort across many simultaneous demands. Try to improve one component while doing the whole, and the others get worse — performance can flatten or regress. Drilling simplifies enough to "focus your cognitive resources on a single aspect."
- **Deliberate practice (Ericsson).** Targeted improvement on isolated sub-skills is the deliberate-practice mechanism behind expert attainment.
- **Strenuous-beats-easy.** "Something mentally strenuous provides a greater benefit to learning than something easy" — a recurring ultralearning theme. Drills are strenuous by construction.

## The named tactics

### Time Slicing

Isolate a slice in time of a longer sequence and drill it until it's perfect, then reintegrate. Musicians do this on the hardest bars of a piece; athletes do it on layups and penalty shots; language learners obsessively repeat a few key phrases until they're embedded in long-term memory. **When to use:** the rate-limiter is a specific moment, not a continuous quality. **Contraindication:** if the difficulty is diffused across the whole task, slicing in time won't isolate it — try Cognitive Components instead.

### Cognitive Components

Isolate one cognitive layer while suppressing the others. Young's worked example: drilling Mandarin tones by pronouncing pairs of words with different tones, recording himself, and *ignoring* meaning and grammar entirely. Grammar, pronunciation, and vocabulary "occur at all moments, but they form different cognitive aspects that must be managed simultaneously." Cognitive Components lets you train one without the distraction tax of the others. **Contraindication:** the component must be one that can genuinely be trained in isolation — some sub-skills only exist in relationship to others and degrade when extracted.

### The Copycat Method

Borrow the parts of the work you're *not* drilling — from someone else, or from your own past work — so you can focus only on the target component. Franklin's example: he wanted to drill the *ordering* of arguments in essays. Writing whole essays would have forced him to also do word choice, content, and grammar. Instead he took notes on *Spectator* essays, jumbled the order of the hints, and reconstructed the sequence from his notes. Young's parallel: when learning to draw, he started by drawing from *other people's drawings* rather than photos, so the composition decision was already made for him and he could focus on rendering. **When to use:** creative skills where you can't practice one component without producing all of them. **Contraindication:** the copied scaffolding has to be of higher quality than yours, or you reinforce its flaws.

### The Magnifying Glass Method

When you can't edit or strip a component out, spend *disproportionately long* on it inside the integrated task. Young's example: writing articles, he spent roughly 10× his normal time on the research component, even though he still had to do everything else. Your overall performance may temporarily drop or your input time balloon, but a much higher share of your cognitive resources lands on the target sub-skill. **When to use:** components that are inseparable from the whole. **Contraindication:** doesn't work when the bottleneck is a discrete moment — that's a Time Slicing problem.

### Prerequisite Chaining

Start with the target skill above your current level. Fail. Drop back one step to learn the missing foundational topic. Try the target again. Repeat. Eric Barone started his pixel art experiments by just making pixel art; when he struggled with color choices, he stepped back, studied color theory, then resumed. Benny Lewis starts speaking a new language from a phrasebook on day one, then learns the grammar that explains why the phrases work later. **When to use:** when you don't know what the prerequisites are until you hit them. **Contraindication:** can be wasteful for skills with brittle prerequisites (math sequences) where you really do need step N-1 solid before N.

## Anchor story

Benjamin Franklin, taught by his brother's print shop and pushed by his father's criticism of a teenage debate essay, decomposed the skill of writing into pieces and built a drill for each:

1. **Sense-of-prose drill** — Took notes on *The Spectator* articles, set them aside for days, reconstructed the originals from notes, compared with the source, found and fixed his faults.
2. **Vocabulary drill** — Turned prose into verse, forcing himself to substitute synonyms that matched meter or rhyme — then turned the verse back into prose.
3. **Rhetorical-order drill** — Jumbled his notes so he had to reconstruct the sequence of ideas, not just the words.
4. **Persuasion drill** — After reading about the Socratic method in an English grammar book, adopted it deliberately as a "humble inquirer and doubter" to drill argument-by-question.

What's striking is that Franklin chose each drill specifically because of a known weakness — his father had told him he lacked persuasive ability; he himself noticed his vocabulary was limited; he could feel his sense of rhetorical flow was off. The drills were tools custom-built for known bottlenecks, "at night, after work or before it began in the morning." The lesson: drills are powerful when chosen by the learner because they target a known bottleneck, and inert when imposed by someone else for an opaque reason.

## Worksheet — Rate-Limiting Step Diagnosis

```
Project: __________________________
Skill being learned: __________________________
Today's date: __________________________

1. WHAT WHOLE-SKILL ATTEMPT DID I JUST MAKE?
   (1-2 sentences describing a recent direct-practice session)

2. WHERE DID I FAIL OR FEEL SLOWEST?
   List the 3 places performance broke down, in order of severity:
   a. _____________________________________________
   b. _____________________________________________
   c. _____________________________________________

3. WHAT'S THE RATE-LIMITING STEP HYPOTHESIS?
   If I improved ONE thing above by 50%, my overall performance
   would jump by: _____ %
   (If less than 20%, you've picked the wrong bottleneck — go back to #2.)

4. WHICH DRILL TACTIC FITS THIS BOTTLENECK?
   [ ] Time Slicing      — the failure is a specific moment
   [ ] Cognitive Components — the failure is one cognitive layer
   [ ] Copycat           — I can borrow the parts I'm not drilling
   [ ] Magnifying Glass  — I have to keep doing the whole, but spend 5-10x on this part
   [ ] Prerequisite Chain — I'm missing something below this in the stack

5. DESIGN THE DRILL
   What I will do: ___________________________________
   For how long / how many reps: ____________________
   How I'll know it's working: ______________________
   (Metafeedback metric — see Feedback ref)

6. DIRECT → DRILL → DIRECT
   Next direct-practice session date: ________________
   (Drills without return to context become drudgery.)
```

## Anti-patterns specific to this principle

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Drilling what you're already good at | Comfortable; no rate-limiting step relief; you don't improve. Pick the bottleneck, not the favorite. |
| Drilling without ever returning to direct practice | Becomes "mind-numbing drudgery" with no transfer back to the whole skill. The cycle requires the integration step. |
| Inventing a drill that has the difficulty stripped out | The drill must still train the *hard* part. Easy drills produce nothing. |
| Drilling components that don't actually drive performance | Wastes time. If you don't see the dial move after a few cycles, your hypothesis was wrong — abandon and re-diagnose. |
| Pushing pure drill mode while you're still a beginner | Cycle fast early — drill briefly, return to direct quickly. Long detours into drills are for late-stage learners. |
| Imposing drills externally on someone else | Drills work because the learner has identified a personal bottleneck. Externally imposed drill = the homework experience most people hated in school. |

## When to push this principle vs. when to relax it

**Push drill harder when:**
- You can name a specific bottleneck that's holding the whole skill back.
- You're late in skill development — gains require finer isolation.
- The whole skill has high cognitive load and you can't make any one component improve while doing all of them.

**Relax drill (favor direct practice) when:**
- You're early in the skill — cycle fast, drill briefly, get back to context.
- You can't name a specific bottleneck yet — do more direct practice first to surface one.
- The drill you've designed doesn't actually replicate the hard part of the real task — you're training the wrong thing.
- The drill is becoming an avoidance pattern away from messy real-context practice — drill-comfort is a real failure mode.
