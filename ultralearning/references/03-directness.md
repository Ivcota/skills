# Directness — Deep Dive

> "He who can go to the fountain does not go to the water jar." — Leonardo da Vinci (epigraph, Ultralearning, ch. VI)

## What it is

Directness is the practice of **tying learning as closely as possible to the context in which the skill will eventually be used**. If you want to speak a language, speak it. If you want to make video games, make video games. If you want to pass an exam, work problems that look like exam problems.

The opposite — the dominant mode of formal schooling and most self-education — is *indirect* learning: studying facts, concepts, and skills in some idealized setting, then hoping the knowledge will somehow "transfer" later. Young's verdict on this hope, drawing on a century of educational psychology, is that it almost never works.

Directness sits at the philosophical core of the book. Young calls it "the hallmark of most ultralearning projects" (ch. VI). It's also why ultralearning is hard: direct practice is more frustrating, challenging, and intense than the comfortable proxies — books, apps, lectures — that feel like learning. The discomfort isn't a side effect. It's the mechanism.

## Why it works (the science)

- **Robert Haskell on transfer (ch. VI).** Transfer — using what you learned in one context in another — is called "the Holy Grail of education." Haskell's review of the literature: "Research findings over the past nine decades clearly show that as individuals, and as educational institutions, we have failed to achieve transfer of learning on any significant level. . . . Without exaggeration, it's an education scandal."
- **Thorndike & Woodworth, 1901 (ch. VI).** "The Influence of Improvement in One Mental Function upon the Efficiency of Other Functions" — the paper that demolished *formal discipline theory* (the brain-as-muscle idea that training Latin or geometry would generally improve "thinking"). Verdict: transfer is much narrower than was assumed.
- **High-school → college psychology study (ch. VI).** Students who took an introductory psychology course in high school do *no better* than students who didn't when they take it in college. Some do *worse*.
- **Economics study (ch. VI).** College graduates who took an economics class showed no difference in reasoning about economic issues vs. those who didn't.
- **Michelene Chi on worked examples (ch. VI).** "In almost all the empirical work to date . . . a student who has studied examples often cannot solve problems that deviate slightly from the example solution."
- **Howard Gardner, *The Unschooled Mind* (ch. VI).** Honors physics students "are frequently unable to solve basic problems and questions encountered in a form slightly different from that on which they have been formally instructed."
- **John H. Zenger, Times Mirror Training Group (ch. VI).** Corporate training has the same problem: "Researchers who rigorously evaluate training have said that demonstrable changes following training are hard to find."
- **Wilbert McKeachie (ch. VI).** "Transfer is paradoxical. When we want it, we do not get it. Yet it occurs all the time."
- **Haskell's "welded to a place" claim (ch. VI).** Knowledge becomes more transferable with *depth*, but in the early going it is "welded to a place or subject matter." The way to escape the welding is either depth (Principle 8, intuition) or directness (this principle).
- **Cognitive features, not surface features (ch. VI).** What matters for transfer is the cognitive structure of the practice — the decisions, cues, and discriminations the learner has to perform — not the room they were in, the clothes they wore, or the graphics on the screen.

## The named tactics

### Project-Based Learning

Organize the learning around producing **something concrete**. A game. An app. A blueprint. An essay. A thesis paper. A photography exhibit.

"The rationale is simple: if you organize your learning around producing something, you're guaranteed to at least learn how to produce that thing. If you take classes, you may spend a lot of time taking notes and reading but not achieve your goal" (ch. VI).

Works naturally for skills with tangible outputs: engineering, design, art, music, carpentry, writing, programming. But Young is explicit that intellectual subjects can also be project-organized — one ultralearner he interviewed turned a military history goal into producing a thesis paper, because the end goal was "being able to converse knowledgeably about the subject."

The selection question: what would you have to produce to *prove* you have this skill? Build that.

### Immersive Learning

Surround yourself with the target environment so that volume, breadth, and unavoidability of practice are forced.

The canonical case is language: live in the country, refuse English from day one (Young's Year Without English project; Benny Lewis's method). But immersion generalizes: novice programmers joining an open-source project; a writer joining a daily-writing community; a designer working at a print shop to absorb how real architectural blueprints are constructed (Vatsal Jaiswal's move, ch. VI).

Immersion has two effects: (1) it guarantees more practice volume than you would otherwise do, and (2) it exposes you to a *broader range of situations* than any curriculum would think to include. Young's no-English example: "Being able to fluently use a dictionary or translation app on my phone, so I could fill gaps in my linguistic knowledge in mid-conversation . . . it's exactly this kind of practical skill that is rarely covered in a language-learning curriculum" (ch. VI).

### The Flight Simulator Method

When the real thing is impossible, illegal, or unsafe to practice (flying a plane, performing surgery, going on *Jeopardy!*), build a simulation that is **faithful to the cognitive features of the task** — the decisions you have to make, the cues you have to read, the discriminations you have to draw.

Surface features (graphics, sound effects, the room, the lighting) matter only insofar as they change cognition. Roger Craig studied for *Jeopardy!* by using questions from past shows; the blue background color didn't matter, the question style and difficulty did. Reading random Wikipedia articles would have failed — same content, wrong cognitive task.

The diagnostic question: what mental processes does the real task demand, and does my simulation force me to perform them? If yes, the simulation transfers. If no, polish on the simulation will go nowhere.

Young's worked comparison (ch. VI): preparing for a trip to France, Skype tutoring transfers more than flash cards — because the cognitive task in real conversation is *producing* speech under time pressure, not recognizing translations from a word bank.

### The Overkill Approach

Set the target *above* the level you strictly need, so you can't accidentally miss anything important.

Worked examples from the chapter:

- **Tristan de Montebello**, preparing for the Toastmasters World Championship of Public Speaking, gave early versions of his talk at middle schools — "the feedback he received at Toastmasters clubs might be too soft or congratulatory to cut deep at what worked and didn't work . . . middle school students, in contrast, would be merciless. If a joke he said wasn't funny or his delivery was boring or cheesy, he would be able to tell immediately from their faces what needed to be reworked."
- **Benny Lewis** in his German project sat for the *highest-level* exam available — "his awareness of that goal would push him to study more than he might if he were satisfied with comfortable conversations alone."
- A photographer-friend of Young's exhibited her work publicly to "push her skills and talent" beyond what private practice would have demanded.

The mechanism: the higher target *changes how you study*. You can't checkmark your way to the highest exam by completing a beginner workbook. The environment selects for the skill you actually need.

Overkill targets to use depending on skill type:
- **Languages**: the highest-level certification exam (CEFR C2, HSK 6, JLPT N1).
- **Speaking / presenting**: the hardest audience (kids, hostile experts, public exhibition).
- **Writing**: publishing in venues that will reject mediocre work.
- **Programming / design**: shipping to real users who will report bugs.
- **Sports / performance**: competition above your current rating.

## Anchor story

**Vatsal Jaiswal**, fresh out of architecture school in Canada in the post-2008 recession, has sent hundreds of résumés and gotten no offers. From the snippets of feedback he can pry out, he realizes the firms don't see him as useful: his program taught design and theory, but his portfolio looks nothing like the detailed technical documents real architects work with. Firms don't want to retrain a grad.

His plan: get *closer to the real thing*.

1. He takes a low-paying, low-skill job at a **large-format print shop** — the kind that prints actual architectural blueprints — so he can study real plans every day and absorb how they're constructed.
2. At night, he teaches himself **Revit**, the design software firms actually use.
3. He builds a new portfolio — not a collection of school projects, but a **single building of his own design**: a three-tower residential structure with raised courtyards.

He submits the new portfolio to two firms. **Both immediately offer him jobs** (ch. VI).

The lesson runs through three layers. (1) Four years of formal architecture education failed at the directness test — what he learned wasn't welded to what the job required. (2) A self-directed project of a few months, organized around the *exact* cognitive task firms cared about, beat the degree. (3) Direct practice doesn't require unlimited resources — Jaiswal couldn't get hired, so he found the next-closest environment (a print shop) and built up from there.

## Worksheets / templates

### Directness audit (run on any learning activity)

For each thing you're currently doing to "learn" the skill, answer:

| Activity | Cognitive task it forces | Cognitive task the real skill requires | Match? |
|---|---|---|---|
| (e.g.) Reading a book on copywriting | Recognizing ideas about copy | Producing copy under deadline that converts | No |
| (e.g.) Duolingo word-bank tapping | Selecting correct word from 4 options | Producing a word from memory in conversation | No |
| (e.g.) Watching MIT lecture videos | Following an exposition | Solving problems on the exam | No |
| (e.g.) Working past *Jeopardy!* questions | Recalling answers from cryptic clues | Recalling answers from cryptic clues | Yes |

For every "No," ask: what's the smallest change that converts it to a "Yes"?

### Flight-simulator design checklist

Before building or accepting a simulation as a proxy for the real task, verify:

- [ ] What are the **decisions** the learner has to make in the real environment? Does the simulation force those decisions?
- [ ] What are the **cues** the learner has to read? Does the simulation present cues with similar discriminations and time pressure?
- [ ] What is the **time pressure or feedback latency** of the real task? Does the simulation reproduce it?
- [ ] What's the **failure mode** — what goes wrong if you do the wrong thing in the real environment? Is the simulation calibrated to similar consequences (or close enough that the practitioner cares)?
- [ ] What surface features are present in the real environment that I'm tempted to reproduce but that don't actually change cognition? (Don't waste effort on these.)

### Overkill targets by skill type

| Skill type | Comfortable target | Overkill target |
|---|---|---|
| Language | Have a 15-minute conversation | Sit the highest-level certification exam |
| Public speaking | Speak at a Toastmasters club | Speak in front of middle schoolers / hostile crowd / televised competition |
| Writing | Write a blog post | Publish in a venue that will reject mediocre submissions |
| Programming | Build a toy project | Ship a product real users will use and complain about |
| Drawing | Sketch in private | Exhibit publicly / sell prints |
| Sales | Roleplay a pitch with a friend | Cold-call live prospects with quota |
| Music | Play for friends | Open mic / paid gig |

Pick the overkill target *first* — then design the practice plan backward from what it demands.

### Project-Based Learning starter

Answer in writing before starting:

1. What is the **single concrete thing** I will produce by the end of this project?
2. Who will I show it to / submit it to / put it in front of? (Real audience, not "my notebook.")
3. What is the **deadline**?
4. What sub-skills does producing this thing require? (Run a Metalearning C/F/P breakdown on those.)
5. What is the **simplest version** I could ship in week one, even badly?

## Anti-patterns specific to this principle

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Comfortable proxy substituted for the real thing | Duolingo isn't speaking. Reading copywriting books isn't writing copy. Watching lectures isn't doing problems. Comfort wins, transfer fails. |
| "I'll learn the theory first, then apply it" | The theory doesn't transfer until you've done the application. Build outward from a real situation. |
| Assuming a credential implies the skill | An economics class doesn't predict economic reasoning. A degree doesn't predict job-readiness (Jaiswal). |
| Over-optimizing simulation surface features | Better graphics on the flight simulator don't help unless they change a decision. Realism for its own sake is wasted effort. |
| Avoiding direct practice because it's frustrating | "Learning directly is hard. It is often more frustrating, challenging, and intense than reading a book or sitting through a lecture. But this very difficulty creates a potent source of competitive advantage." |
| Picking too-easy overkill targets | The middle-school students are merciless *because* you can't bluff them. A friendly audience is information-free. |

## When to push this principle vs. when to relax it

**Push it harder** when you find yourself reaching for the indirect proxy because it's comfortable — that's exactly the moment directness is paying off and you're tempted to abandon it. Push when your "learning" feels like progress but you can't yet demonstrate the skill in its real context. Push when you've been studying long enough that you should have something to show, and you don't.

**Relax it** when the real environment is genuinely impossible (flight, surgery, *Jeopardy!*, the World Championship of Public Speaking) — use the Flight Simulator Method instead, faithful to cognitive features. Relax it briefly when you're so new to a domain that you literally don't know enough to attempt the direct task; do the smallest possible direct attempt anyway (Benny Lewis: phrasebook + speak from day one), then iterate.

**The depth route.** Haskell points out — and Young endorses — that with enough depth, knowledge eventually does become flexible and transfers more broadly. Dan Everett can pick up new languages partly because his linguistic depth is enormous. So directness and depth are *both* paths out of the transfer problem; one buys you flexibility early, the other buys you flexibility late. Most ultralearners need to start with directness.

**The focus tension.** Direct practice is usually more cognitively demanding than indirect proxies. The temptation is to sacrifice directness for focus comfort — to switch from speaking to Duolingo because Duolingo is easier to sit through. Young is explicit: don't do this. Keep directness; manage focus with the crutch ladder (ch. V).
