# Feedback — Deep Dive

> "Everybody has a plan until they get punched in the mouth." — Mike Tyson, epigraph (Ultralearning, ch. IX)

## What it is

Feedback is information about how you're doing — but only certain *kinds* of feedback actually accelerate learning, and more isn't automatically better. Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi's meta-analysis of hundreds of feedback studies found the overall effect positive but flagged that **feedback was negative in over 38 percent of cases**. The discrepancy traces to *what kind* of feedback was given, and how the learner processed it.

Ultralearners distinguish themselves not by getting more feedback in the abstract but by deliberately exposing themselves to **immediate, intense, sometimes uncomfortable** feedback — and then filtering signal from noise. They go where the punches are. The fear of feedback is usually worse than the feedback itself, and diving into a high-feedback environment desensitizes you faster than any amount of preparation can.

Crucially, feedback aimed at the *task* (informative, corrective) helps; feedback aimed at the *ego* ("you're so smart," "you're lazy") tends to hurt — students who get ego feedback may reject it, lower their standards, or quit. Some of the negative-impact studies in the Kluger and DeNisi meta-analysis traced to learners simply mishandling the information they received.

## Why it works (the science)

- **K. Anders Ericsson** on deliberate practice: immediate feedback is an essential ingredient in expert attainment. Without feedback, even experienced practitioners stagnate or decline — Young cites medical practitioners whose diagnostic accuracy drifts down with years since school when feedback loops are weak.
- **Kluger and DeNisi (meta-analysis)** — feedback is positive on average but negative in ~38% of studies. The *type* of feedback and how it's processed determines the sign.
- **Ruiz-Primo and Brookhart** — "Optimal feedback indicates the difference between the current state and the desired learning state AND helps students to take a step to improve their learning."
- **Kulik and Kulik** — applied/classroom studies usually find immediate feedback beats delayed. Lab studies sometimes show delayed feedback wins, but the likely explanation is that delayed feedback doubles as spaced exposure (a retention effect, not a feedback effect).
- **Visual-acuity study** — even very coarse outcome feedback delivered in large aggregate blocks (too aggregated to identify which responses were right or wrong) still facilitated learning. Even crude outcome feedback beats none.

## The three types (taxonomy)

Young's framing is essential — without it, you'll either chase the wrong type or try to upgrade feedback in ways that backfire.

| Type | What it tells you | Examples | When available |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Outcome feedback** | Did it work? Pass/fail, grade, applause vs. crickets. | Audience laughter or silence; app store rating; product sales; grade on a test. | Almost always. The default. |
| **Informational feedback** | *What's* wrong — but not how to fix it. | Native speaker's confused stare at a misused word; compiler error messages; a brushstroke that visibly hurts the painting. | When you can interact with the environment in real time and observe its response on a per-element basis. |
| **Corrective feedback** | What's wrong *and* how to fix it. | A coach pointing out a specific flaw and the specific correction; MIT problem sets paired with worked-answer solutions; flash-card answer reveals. | Hardest to find. Usually requires a coach, mentor, expert, or well-designed materials with worked answers. |

Corrective trumps informational trumps outcome — but only when actually available. **Don't try to "upgrade" feedback when the source can't actually provide the higher type.** Game testers asked "what don't you like" will guess (the color of the character, the music) when they were really evaluating the game holistically — forcing specificity on a holistic source returns spurious answers. Corrective feedback from a non-expert source is worse than informational feedback from an honest one.

## The four tactics

### Noise Cancellation

Every feedback stream contains a *signal* (the useful information) and *noise* (random variation you should not overreact to). One viral blog post or one harsh comment is rarely signal. Strip the noise by picking proxy metrics that correlate with the underlying skill while being less subject to luck.

Young's worked example: for blog writing, **scroll-depth analytics** (what % of readers reach the end of an article) are far less noisy than raw traffic. Traffic is driven heavily by who happens to share the piece; depth is driven by whether the writing held attention. Same skill, much higher signal-to-noise. **When to use:** any time the raw outcome metric is dominated by exogenous factors. **Contraindication:** if the proxy isn't actually correlated with the skill, you're just measuring something else.

### Hitting the Difficulty Sweet Spot

Information theory definition of information: how hard to predict the message is. If you always succeed, success carries zero information; you knew it would go well. Same for guaranteed failure. **Calibrate the challenge so success is genuinely unpredictable** — and you maximize the information yield per attempt.

People intuitively avoid constant failure but underestimate the *more pervasive* problem of being too successful, where feedback flatlines because everything works. The rule: if you're failing too often, simplify until you can start noticing what's working; if you're failing too little, raise difficulty or tighten standards. Avoid environments that always make you feel good (or bad).

### Metafeedback

Standard feedback is about your performance. Metafeedback is about whether your *method* is working — tracked through **your learning rate** itself.

Examples: chess players tracking Elo growth; LSAT studiers tracking mock-exam improvements; language learners tracking vocabulary acquired per week or errors per spoken minute. Two uses: (1) decide when to stick with your current method vs. experiment with a new one — if learning rate is trickling, you're at diminishing returns; (2) **A/B compare two study methods side-by-side**, as Young did in the MIT Challenge by splitting exam-question subtopics across approaches.

### High-Intensity, Rapid Feedback

The easiest way to improve feedback is to get a lot more of it, a lot faster than the default mode of your domain. Tristan de Montebello spoke "several times per week, jumping among different clubs" instead of the typical Toastmasters once-every-month-or-two pace. Benny Lewis exposes himself to pronunciation feedback from day one of a language, while traditional students don't speak for months.

The advantage is partly informational — more data, faster — but **the emotional component often outweighs the informational one**. Fear of feedback holds people back more than any actual feedback does; throwing yourself into a high-intensity environment desensitizes you quickly, and "knowing that your work will be evaluated is an incredible motivator to do your best." **When to use:** when the default cadence of feedback in your domain is too slow for the cycle time you need. **Contraindication:** if the high-intensity source is non-expert, you may be flooding yourself with corrective-shaped noise.

## Anchor story

Chris Rock, a sold-out HBO-special-headliner, walks into the modest brick-backed stage of the **Comedy Cellar in Greenwich Village**, unannounced, with hand-scribbled index cards of half-formed jokes. He slumps against the back wall — nothing like his usual aggressive style. "It's not going to be that good," he warns the small crowd. "Not at these prices — at these prices, I could leave right now!" He envisions the reviews: "Chris came out and he left. It was good! He didn't tell any jokes — but it was good!"

The reason for the deliberately self-deprecating preamble: at his usual venues, Rock's celebrity guarantees applause — applause that tells him nothing about whether the joke itself works. At the Comedy Cellar he engineers a feedback environment where the audience laughs at the *joke*, not the *celebrity*. He's running comedy "with the precision of an experiment." The trick he learned from his grandfather, a cab driver who preached on weekends: tiny stages are laboratories specifically because the noise floor is low. Dave Chappelle, Jon Stewart, and Amy Schumer use the Cellar the same way.

The lesson is layered. (1) Outcome feedback (laughter) is only useful when noise is stripped — celebrity is noise. (2) High-intensity, rapid feedback works only if the environment isn't gamed in your favor. (3) The right feedback often comes from *selectively ignoring* the wrong signals — Rock ignores the celebrity laugh, and de Montebello similarly ignored conflicting individual speech-coach comments and watched the *consistency* of the comments as the real signal.

## Worksheet — High-Intensity Feedback Environment Design Checklist

```
Project: __________________________
Skill: __________________________

PART A — DESIGN THE ENVIRONMENT

1. WHAT'S THE DEFAULT FEEDBACK CADENCE IN MY DOMAIN?
   (e.g., "Toastmasters speech every 4-6 weeks")
   _______________________________________________

2. WHAT'S 5-10x THAT CADENCE?
   (target rapid-feedback rhythm)
   _______________________________________________

3. WHERE CAN I GO TO GET FEEDBACK AT THAT RATE?
   List 3 venues / channels / partners:
   a. _____________________________________________
   b. _____________________________________________
   c. _____________________________________________

4. WHAT TYPE OF FEEDBACK WILL EACH VENUE PROVIDE?
   Venue a: [ ] outcome  [ ] informational  [ ] corrective
   Venue b: [ ] outcome  [ ] informational  [ ] corrective
   Venue c: [ ] outcome  [ ] informational  [ ] corrective

5. EGO-PROTECTION CHECK
   Is any venue likely to deliver mostly ego-directed feedback
   (praise for who I am, not what I did)?
   If yes, demote or replace it.

6. SOURCE-EXPERTISE CHECK (for corrective venues only)
   Does the source actually know the right answer in this domain?
   If no — accept the venue as informational at best.

PART B — NOISE-CANCELLATION PROXY-METRIC PICKER

7. RAW OUTCOME METRIC I'M CURRENTLY USING:
   _______________________________________________
   (e.g., raw blog traffic, raw audience applause, raw # of sales)

8. WHAT IS NOISY ABOUT IT?
   (e.g., depends on who shared, time of day, celebrity bias)
   _______________________________________________

9. WHAT PROXY METRIC IS CLOSER TO THE SKILL?
   (e.g., scroll-depth % instead of raw traffic;
    repeat-bookings instead of one-time bookings;
    consistency of audience reaction across N trials)
   _______________________________________________

10. CONSISTENCY-AS-SIGNAL CHECK
    Am I watching individual data points (each comment) or
    the consistency across data points (most comments say the same thing)?
    De Montebello's lesson: cross-trial consistency is the real signal.

PART C — DIFFICULTY SWEET SPOT

11. CURRENT SUCCESS RATE: ____ %
    (If 90%+: raise difficulty. If <30%: lower difficulty.
     Target: ~50% — genuinely unpredictable.)

12. FEEDBACK-DELAY POLICY
    [ ] Timer before peeking (prevents passive review)
    [ ] Immediate feedback after attempt (default)
    [ ] Delayed feedback for spaced exposure
    Default: timer, then immediate.
```

## Anti-patterns specific to this principle

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Dodging feedback to protect the ego | Fear of feedback is usually worse than the feedback. Dodging stalls learning indefinitely. |
| Praising the person ("you're smart") instead of the task | Ego-directed feedback is one of the negative-impact categories in Kluger & DeNisi. Hurts both motivation and learning. |
| Over-reacting to a single data point | One viral post, one harsh comment — rarely signal. Look for consistency across trials. |
| "Upgrading" outcome feedback into informational when the source is holistic | Game testers will invent specifics. Forcing specificity on a non-decomposable source returns spurious answers. |
| Treating corrective feedback from a non-expert as gospel | Some sources are confidently wrong. Corrective only works when the source actually knows. |
| Peeking at the answer before attempting | Short-circuits retrieval into passive review. Use a timer. |
| Letting feedback override a deliberate creative vision | Eric Barone ignored early critics whose feedback conflicted with his vision for *Stardew Valley*. Not all feedback should be acted on. |
| Sticking to an audience that flatters you | Constant success carries no information. Move to a tougher venue. |

## When to push this principle vs. when to relax it

**Push feedback harder when:**
- You're avoiding it because of fear, not because it's actually unhelpful.
- Your learning rate has stalled and you can't tell why — more feedback will surface the cause.
- The default cadence in your domain is dangerously slow (months-long performance cycles).

**Relax / filter feedback when:**
- The source can't actually decompose its judgment (holistic graders, generalist audiences) — don't force them to upgrade.
- You're acting on a deliberate creative vision the feedback can't yet see (Barone case).
- The feedback is ego-directed praise or attack with no actionable task content.
- You're being asked to react to a single noisy data point — wait for consistency across trials.
- Feedback delivered too quickly is turning your retrieval into passive review — slow it down with a timer.
