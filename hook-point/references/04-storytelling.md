# 04 — Storytelling

Once the hook catches attention, the story carries meaning. The hook is the door. The story is the room.

## The customer is the hero. The brand is the stage.

Ch. 4 — *"When we tell stories, the brand should not be positioned as the hero."*

Brownstein on Adobe's *Students Help Victims of Hurricane Harvey Restore Lost Memories*: *"If the stories in these videos weren't authentic, and/or were exploitative, people would have quickly noticed. To avoid that situation, the Adobe brand needed to position itself in the background. The heroes in the videos are the victims, the students, and the local nonprofits that helped them. Adobe does not try to steal the spotlight, but rather acts as the theater or stage where a light is shined on these issues and heroes."*

The trust trade: *"The more a brand takes a back seat to the heroic characters, the more trust and credibility they can build with their audience."*

| Brand | Hero of the story | Brand's role |
|---|---|---|
| Adobe (Hurricane Harvey) | Storm victims, student volunteers, nonprofits | The stage where photos are restored |
| Red Bull (Stratos) | Felix Baumgartner | Production company / distribution |
| Adobe (St. Jude B2B film) | St. Jude Children's Research Hospital + its patients | The technology making the work possible |
| Nike (Kaepernick) | Kaepernick and his activism | "Just Do It" frame at the end |
| Nike (Dream Crazier) | Female athletes who were called crazy | Stage for the message |

**Diagnostic for your copy:** read it back. Is the brand the noun in most sentences? Reframe.

## Red Bull Stratos — the brand-as-stage canon case

Ch. 4 — Red Bull invested *$60M+* to stage, film, and promote Felix Baumgartner's skydive from a helium balloon 24 miles up. Baumgartner reached 725 mph; broke three world records including the sound barrier at Mach 1.24. Red Bull's role: production company (Red Bull Media House) and distribution (ServusTV → other media outlets). YouTube's live stream: 340M+ site views *before* the jump; 8M+ concurrent viewers — a YouTube record at the time.

The hook is "skydiving from space." The hero is the skydiver. The brand is the stage. Not one frame is about an energy drink.

Owen Gibson of *The Guardian* on Red Bull: *"Major brands have been talking for years about becoming content producers rather than simply paying media and rights owners to advertise or slap their logos on shirts and hoardings. But none have taken it as seriously as Red Bull."*

## PCM — Process Communication Model (the canonical Kane framework)

Six personality types, six "currencies." Jeff King teaches it (Ch. 4). Used by NASA, Bill Clinton, Pixar.

| Type | Perceives world through | Currency |
|---|---|---|
| **Thinkers** | Thoughts | Logic |
| **Persisters** | Opinions | Values |
| **Harmonizers** | Emotions | Compassion |
| **Imaginers** | Inactions | Imagination |
| **Rebels** | Reactions | Humor |
| **Promoters** | Actions | Charm |

Ch. 4 — *"All of the personality types are in each of us, but we have a base personality type that we're born with that doesn't change over the course of our lives."*

The mistake: most communicators only write in *their own* base currency. A Thinker-writer fills the page with logic and loses 75% of the audience.

The fix: hit all six in a single piece.

## The PCM car ad — six currencies in one paragraph

Ch. 4, Jeff King's worked example:

> "Think of a car. This car model gets 50 miles per gallon. The car's miles per gallon are at the highest rate compared to other models in its class. We believe that this car provides more value to our customers in regard to what you're going to pay for. Bottom line — it's the best car on the market. It feels good, it looks nice, and you're going to be so comfortable driving this car. All your friends are finally going to want to hang out with you, because this car is awesome."

Breakdown:

| Sentence | Currency | Speaks to |
|---|---|---|
| "Think of a car… 50 mpg… highest rate compared to other models." | Logic | Thinkers |
| "We believe that this car provides more value to our customers…" | Values | Persisters |
| "Bottom line — it's the best car on the market." | Charm | Promoters |
| "It feels good, it looks nice, and you're going to be so comfortable…" | Feelings / Compassion | Harmonizers |
| "All your friends are finally going to want to hang out with you, because this car is awesome." | Humor | Rebels |

(Imagination isn't represented in this example — Kane's text covers the other five.)

## Dollar Shave Club — six-currency canon

Ch. 4 — *"The Dollar Shave Club created a video called DollarShaveClub.com — Our Blades Are F***ing Great, which spread the PCM language very effectively. The video went viral shortly after it was released in 2012 and currently has more than 26 million views. It helped the company launch, and led to their acquisition by Unilever for more than $1 billion."*

King's diagnosis: the storytelling worked because it used **logic, emotion, and humor** in its messaging — *"which reaches the largest percentage of the population."*

The three top vocabularies in North America: **emotions, logic, humor**. If your audience is North American, design those three before reaching for the others.

## Bill Clinton vs Hillary Clinton — the PCM lesson

Ch. 4 — Bill Clinton won the 1996 debate against Bush in part by *matching the questioner's currency*. A woman asked about the economy and said the recession was affecting her life, her friends', and her family's. Bush replied with thoughts, logic, value, opinions. The woman perceived the world through feelings; she didn't connect.

Clinton: *"He responded to her personally and shared that he felt her pain."*

King's read on the 2016 election: *"Hillary Clinton may have been the most qualified person to ever run for president of the United States, but because she only spoke in logic and values, she wasn't accessible to a significant portion of the North American population."*

## Fairy-tale three-act structure

Lupinacci (Ch. 4): *"Hypothetically, you could argue that every brand story, in regard to its proposition to the customer, could be structured like a fairy tale."*

Format:
1. Once upon a time, everything was fine…
2. …until a dragon showed up.
3. Then a hero came along and killed the dragon.
4. Everyone lived happily ever after.

Lupinacci's BMW worked example (verbatim, Ch. 4):

> "Once upon a time, everyone aspired to drive a good domestic car, right? You know, you dreamed of owning a Cadillac. And then along came a dragon called Too Much Choice. It included inexpensive and expensive Japanese cars, super expensive European cars, and way too many domestic cars. All of a sudden none of the cars available seemed satisfying. Then a knight showed up named BMW — the Ultimate Driving Machine. Everyone who ended up driving that car lived happily ever after."

The pattern: status quo → conflict (the dragon = the customer's pain) → solution (your product) → new world.

## The "You know how…?" opener

Lupinacci (Ch. 4): build agreement *before* the reveal. Each "You know how…?" forces the audience to nod yes. By the time you say the brand line, you've already had three yeses.

Lupinacci's Dasani relaunch worked example (verbatim, Ch. 4):

> "You know how there are 75,000 water brands in the market, and we're supposed to drink eight glasses of water a day? […] Well, you know how you can't figure out if one is better than the other? […] Well, you know how at the end of the day, whatever you drink or eat still needs to appeal to you? It's got to taste good; otherwise, it's medicine, right? […] Well, you should try some Dasani. […] Dasani is the water that makes your mouth water."

Test your own concepts this way. If the listener doesn't nod, the hook is off.

## Match the hook's tone to the product's tone

Ch. 4 — *"Creating something that hooks people doesn't matter if it's not the right kind of hook."*

The *Rings* anti-pattern (Ch. 4): Paramount's digital team filmed customers' reactions to *Rings*'s creepy girl crawling out of TVs at electronic stores. The video — *Rings (2017) – TV Store Prank* — hit ~10M views. But *Rings* is horror. The video produced **laughter**, not fear. The movie didn't lift ticket sales.

Diagnosis: viral ≠ effective. The hook drew the wrong emotion for the product.

| When hook tone matches product tone | When it doesn't |
|---|---|
| Stratos (awe + Red Bull = brand of extremity) | Rings TV-store prank (laughter + horror movie) |
| Cena prank (joy + a phone carrier's "good service" promise) | A self-serious B2B firm with a meme-account voice |
| Dollar Shave Club (irreverence + a disruptor brand) | A funeral-home pun account |

## Nike's "Dream Crazier"

Ch. 4 — Intercuts images of female athletes and coaches yelling and crying. Reframes "crazy": every woman called crazy was simply the first to do something. Final line: dare to be crazy.

The hero: female athletes. Nike: a logo at the end and the clothing the athletes happen to wear. The story is not about a shoe; the story is about who wears it.

## The Cena × Cricket case

Ch. 4 — *Unexpected John Cena Prank* (Shareability, Cricket Wireless, 2016): fans audition for a Cena commercial; Cena bursts through a poster of himself. 235M+ views.

The reversal: *John Cena Reacts*. Fans open fan mail thanking Cena for his "Never give up" motto. Cena gets emotional. A boy whose mother battled cancer comes through the same poster with his mother to thank Cena in person. **Most shared ad of 2017. #3 YouTube trending. 175M+ views. 2.5M shares.**

Brownstein's read: *"One of the reasons these campaigns were highly successful was because they didn't ask for anything — their only purpose was to provide value to the audience."* The brand-building did the conversion work later.

## The full-funnel activation strategy

Shareability's pattern (Ch. 4):

1. Big, viral, shareable content. (Cena prank.)
2. Engagement content — no strong CTA. (Cena reacts.)
3. Retargeted content to engaged viewers — *now* a CTA. ("Visit Cricket Wireless.")

The viewer is sold-to *after* they've fallen in love. Direct-response content riding on top of brand-building content. Brownstein's pet-products case: 800K click-throughs from two videos that weren't built as traffic drivers — because the brand-build was so strong the soft CTA at the end worked.

## "You Know How…" Hook test — apply it now

Before you ship copy, run it through:

- [ ] Is the customer the hero, or am I?
- [ ] Have I touched **emotion, logic, and humor** at minimum?
- [ ] Have I framed the dragon explicitly — the pain my reader is in?
- [ ] Can I rewrite my hook as "You know how…?" without it sounding fake?
- [ ] Does the hook's emotional register match the product's?
- [ ] If the audience laughs / cries / nods, will that emotion *route them toward purchase* or *away*?

## Common storytelling mistakes (from Ch. 4)

- Communicating in your own base PCM currency only.
- Making the brand the hero ("we built this", "our mission is").
- Picking a viral tactic that produces the wrong emotion (Rings).
- Mixing brand-build and direct-response in one artifact (Brownstein's caution).
- Treating engagement metrics (views) as success when the metric should be shares-to-views ratio or, ultimately, sale.
