# 06 — Listening

Hook Points are discovered, not declared. Ch. 6 reframes copywriting as *information-gathering* before composition.

## The core practice — listen, then write

Ch. 6 opens with Mark Cuban's habit:

> "Whenever we sit down for a meeting, I want you to take out your pad of paper and pen, and in the upper right-hand corner, write down the word *listen*."

Larry Menaw gave Cuban this advice at Tronics 2000 right out of college. Cuban still does it before every meeting.

The point is operational, not symbolic: the word **LISTEN** in the corner of the notepad is a forcing function. It makes you watch yourself — every time you're about to interrupt, your eye lands on the word.

## Listen 90%, don't interrupt, repeat back

Chris Voss (former head of FBI international hostage negotiation), via Ch. 6, on active listening:

1. Don't interrupt, disagree, or "evaluate."
2. Make brief acknowledgments — "Yes," "Uh-huh," nods.
3. Repeat back what the other person says, without being awkward.
4. Ask questions that move the discussion forward.

Kane's prescribed week-long exercise (Ch. 6):

- Listen 90% of the time.
- Stay neutral. Don't react emotionally.
- Ask thoughtful questions.
- Note how people respond when they feel heard.

## Don't pre-can the pitch

Ch. 6 — *"Don't make assumptions or go into meetings with a preconceived idea or pitch, even when you've already met with others at the company."*

The Taylor Swift sequence is the worked example. Kane met with (in order): the record label / Borchetta, Swift's father, Swift's mother, then Swift herself. *Same product*, four different Hook Points:

| Audience | Pain point | Hook Point Kane used |
|---|---|---|
| Record label / father | $100K+ spent on a flash site; 2 days to update; 90% bounce rate killing merch revenue | Cost savings + bounce-rate fix |
| Mother | Trust; protecting her daughter | Trust + ease of use |
| Swift | She loved customizing her MySpace; hated needing a developer | Creative control without code |

Kane's diagnostic line: *"You can give the best presentation in the world, but it won't secure new business if what you offer doesn't address what clients want."*

## Anti-pattern: the SiriusXM blowup

Kane's own failure (Ch. 6). The developer at SiriusXM wouldn't listen, kept cutting Kane off about social-media-driven traffic. Kane got frustrated, said *"I can't help you, then,"* and walked out. Lost the deal.

His retrospective: *"I should've taken a deep breath and worked harder to understand the developer's point of view. I could have been less reactive."*

Two lessons embedded:
- Anger is a listening failure.
- Stress is an upstream cause — meditation, sleep, and physical state determine whether you *can* listen at all.

## Ask before you assume

Kane's question template for meetings (Ch. 6):

> - What are your most important goals and objectives?
> - What obstacles are you running into while trying to achieve those goals?
> - What are the pain points that are most frustrating for your organization?
> - What are the biggest pain points you're experiencing in your specific position?

Then the closer: *"If I were able to solve your problem, would it be helpful?"* — *"It's very hard for people to say no to someone who wants to help solve a difficult problem for them."*

## Listen to dissent — Ray Dalio

Ch. 6, on Bridgewater's $150B-AUM founder: *"The smartest people who disagree with you the most [will] stress test your thinking. If they disagree, then the conversation in that disagreement will be enlightening. [It's] the quickest way to get an education."*

Apply to copy: show your hook to the person most likely to find it stupid. If they nod, you have something.

## For Dummies — listening as a brand-extension engine

Ch. 6 — John Kilcullen reads the feedback cards in every *For Dummies* book.

After *Quicken for Dummies*, readers asked for a book on managing money. Kilcullen extended from IT into personal finance. The brand grew to **2,500+ titles, 200M+ books in print** — *"Had Kilcullen not listened to his clients' feedback, the brand may not have generated around 2,500 titles."*

The Meredith Corporation cascade (Ch. 6): *Life* magazine's most popular section was *People* → they launched *People* magazine. The most popular section of *People* was *Style* → *InStyle*. The most popular section of *InStyle* was weddings → *InStyle Weddings*. The most popular section of *InStyle Weddings* was the celebrity section → *InStyle: Celebrity Weddings*.

Each new product was a hook discovered by listening.

## Discovery channels — where Hook Points live online

Ch. 6 names the operational research stack:

| Channel | What to look for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Google Trends** | Topics rising in your category | Time the hook to the wave |
| **Google News** | Recent stories you can tie to | Borrowed urgency |
| **Reddit** | Subreddits in your category; pin "wow, that's cool" posts | User-curated, less algorithmic |
| **YouTube** | Search broad keywords; mine top + obscure videos for ideas | See what already converts to attention |
| **Instagram / Facebook** | High share-to-view ratio (>1% on Facebook) | Filters out paid-media-juiced views |
| **Tubular Labs** | All-platform video analytics; sort by views, shares, time period | Cross-platform pattern recognition |

Kane's specific tactic: when working with Couric on a DJ Khaled interview, his team checked Google Trends + Facebook + Instagram to see *which Khaled topics were already shareable* — used those as Hook Points for the interview.

## Pizza Hut "Dangers of Selfie Sticks" — listening to the trend wave

Ch. 6 — Shareability's hook for Pizza Hut + Pepsi: a parody PSA about the dangers of selfie sticks. Built because:

1. Selfie sticks had just been **banned from Disneyland** — trending topic.
2. Pizza Hut was launching a **2-foot-long pizza** — needed a launch hook.
3. The hook tied them together: a "really long selfie stick" to photograph the long pizza.

Result: the **most-shared ad in the world** the month it launched.

The trend wasn't related to pizza. The team made it relatable.

## Listen for content *behavior*, not just topics

Ch. 6, Naveen Gowda: *"Your goal is not only to understand the categorical or topical needs of the audience, but also to understand what the audience needs in terms of content behavior."*

Example (Ch. 6): a woman drinks coffee without sugar (healthy lifestyle). She has seen *Goodful*'s Facebook page. She expects videos that get to the point fast and don't feel like marketing. If you serve her healthy-coffee content in a slow, salesy format, you've misread her *consumption pattern* even though you got the topic right.

Apply to your channels: *what do successful videos in this niche look like, not just what are they about?*

## Calm enough to listen — meditation as infrastructure

Kane's note in Ch. 6: meditation slows the mind, increases awareness, and keeps you present in meetings and on stage. Ten minutes a day is enough. Keith Ferrazzi's Barry Diller story: Ferrazzi was so nervous before meeting Diller he started seeing spots in his eyes; he got off the elevator, went into a phone booth, and meditated until the spots cleared. Then he had a productive meeting.

The instrumentation is plain: bad sleep, high stress, anxiety = bad listening = bad hooks. Inputs precede outputs.

## Listening checklist before any copy assignment

- [ ] Have I spent 15+ minutes with the audience in their own words (Reddit threads, comments, customer interviews)?
- [ ] What is the exact phrase a real customer used to describe the pain?
- [ ] What's trending in this category *right now* that I could borrow urgency from?
- [ ] What's the *content behavior* in this niche — not just the topics?
- [ ] Have I pre-canned a pitch? (If yes, scrap it.)
- [ ] Did I listen 90% of the meeting / 10% of the meeting?
- [ ] Did I ask: "If I were able to solve your problem, would it be helpful?"
