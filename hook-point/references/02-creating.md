# 02 — Creating a Hook Point

The load-bearing pillar of the framework. Kane's Ch. 2 is the only chapter that gives you an explicit, step-by-step procedure — everything else is context. Run this loop every time you sit down to write a hook for a LinkedIn post, X reply, video opener, cold-outreach subject line, or landing-page headline.

---

## A. What a great Hook Point looks like (Kane's checklist)

Before you draft, internalize the characteristics. Kane lists these explicitly as "guidelines to help you understand the essence of a great Hook Point" (Ch. 2). Most great hooks hit several; few hit all.

- **Few words.** "Uses as few words as possible (envision a magazine-cover headline)—that is, 'Zero to a Million Followers in 30 Days.'" (Ch. 2)
- **True to brand.** "Remains true to who you are and why you exist as a brand. If it's unrelated, it will come across as clickbait and inauthentic." (Ch. 2)
- **Subverts expectations.** "Makes people think differently and subverts expectations. For example, in a successful social video I created, the Hook Point was 'WARNING!! Safety is Dangerous.'" (Ch. 2)
- **Easy to understand, but not too easy.** "Doesn't make people think very hard—it's presented in a simple-to-understand manner. ... Doesn't make people think too little—if someone doesn't think at all about your hook, then it's probably being ignored." (Ch. 2)
- **Has curiosity.** "Has an element of curiosity that leaves audiences wanting to learn or view more—that is, 'Elon Musk Made the Cybertruck Ugly on Purpose—and It's the Smartest Thing He's Ever Done.'" (Ch. 2)
- **Original.** "Stands out with originality. If you've seen your Hook Point used somewhere else, it probably won't work." (Ch. 2)
- **Pairs relatable + unique.** "Combines a common/relatable element with something that is unique... An example is the Away Travel suitcase with phone-charging capabilities." (Ch. 2)
- **Grasped in 3 seconds.** "Can be grasped quickly. Make sure your Hook Point can be understood in three seconds max." (Ch. 2)
- **Specific.** "When choosing Hook Points, it's important to keep specificity in mind. You can't tell a story that people have heard a hundred times or they'll get bored and become numb to it." (Ch. 2)
- **Solves a pain point.** "Offers a solution to your audience's pain points." (Ch. 2)

Use these as a rubric when ranking your 50–100 candidates in Step 4.

## B. The 5-step workshop

### Step 1 — Study what works

Build a list of effective Hook Points already running in your space. *"Look for effective Hook Points used by successful brands, and put them in a list. You may be tempted to skip this step, but don't."* (Ch. 2) Even Craig Clemens — a copywriter with $1B+ in sales — started here because "his original hooks were horrible" (Ch. 2).

Sources to pull from (Kane's list, Ch. 2): book titles, social-content meme cards, headlines, concepts, launch campaigns, article headlines, TV commercials, social-media ads (use Facebook Ad Library: facebook.com/ads/library), print ads, billboards, magazine covers.

Working move: pick 20 hooks. For each, replace the brand's offer with yours. You aren't going to ship the swap — *"the purpose of this exercise is not to steal (or replicate) other brands' hooks, but to practice and spark ideas"* (Ch. 2).

### Step 2 — Learn from what doesn't work

*"Research underperforming Hook Points and diagnose why you think they don't work."* (Ch. 2) Go to social, sort by low engagement; on Amazon/Yelp/Google Shopping, find listings with the fewest reviews; pick up the magazines you'd ignore on a stand.

Kane's diagnosis list for why hooks fail (Ch. 2, verbatim): *too wordy, confusing, vague, overused, not relevant to a specific audience, outdated, created with the assumption that people are already interested in a particular topic, inauthentic, not unique enough, unappealing or use language that doesn't catch the eye.*

### Step 3 — Create your own (50–100 variations)

*"Create more ideas and variations than you think possible. If you can, create 50 to 100 potential concepts. Doing so pushes your creative limits and helps you come up with better ideas in the long term."* (Ch. 2)

Kane's drafting frame: imagine a prospect walking past a magazine stand with 30 covers. *"Which headline/Hook Point could you create to grab your prospect's attention enough to make her stop, actually buy the magazine or newspaper with your headline (as opposed to the other 30 magazines sitting right next to it), and read your article?"* (Ch. 2) Or, for a feed: *"imagine a man scrolling on his Instagram, YouTube, or Facebook feed—what's the headline or opening of a video that's going to capture his attention and make him stop for three or more seconds?"* (Ch. 2)

Drafting loop: *"Once you've completed your list, narrow it down to the three best choices. Then, repeat the process—that is, create, narrow down to three, and repeat."* (Ch. 2)

### Step 4 — Compare your Hook Points against the winners

*"Take the original Hook Points you've just created and compare them to the successful hooks from Step 1. To do so, create a combined list with all these Hook Points on the same page. Now, rank the top Hook Points from this master list."* (Ch. 2)

The fail-state is explicit: *"If your original Hook Points from Step 3 are not at the top of this new combined list and beating out the ones from Step 1, then it's time to revise your hooks until they outrank the most successful ones you've found from other brands."* (Ch. 2)

### Step 5 — Test, reiterate, repeat

*"Show them to your friends and colleagues to discover which ones attract the most attention. You can also A/B test your Hook Points on social media, with your email list, and on search-engine advertising platforms."* (Ch. 2) Even Clemens notes *"some of the concepts that are home runs are the ones you never would have expected"* (Ch. 2) — opinion is unreliable; tested signal is the only authority.

## C. 32 copy-pattern templates

Every template is grounded in a verbatim hook from the book. Use the family tag to find the right template fast.

### Forbidden / Reversal

1. **Forbidden framing** — `[Person] Swears Under Oath That Their [Product] Does Not Contain a/an [Illegal-Sounding Superlative]` — Halbert for Tova Borgnine (Ch. 1, "Tova Borgnine Swears Under Oath That Her New Perfume Does Not Contain an Illegal Sexual Stimulant"). Use when the product is unremarkable and needs a transgression frame to get read.
2. **Counterintuitive reversal** — `WARNING!! [Safe belief] is [Dangerous claim]` — Kane (Ch. 2, "WARNING!! Safety is Dangerous"). Use when your audience holds a common belief you can credibly invert.
3. **Ironic "X is a scam"** — `[Popular practice] is a scam!` — Kane (Ch. 2, "Meditation is a scam!" — and Kane's been meditating ten years; the hook *introduces* the real value). Use when you can defend the contrarian frame with substance.
4. **"X is dead; long live the X"** — `Your [common asset] Is Dead; Long Live the [same asset]` — Hook Point Ch. 10 title ("Your Hook Point Is Dead; Long Live the Hook Point"). Use to signal evolution within continuity.

### Specificity / Stat-stunner

5. **Zero-to-X** — `Zero to [specific metric] in [specific timeframe]` — Kane (Ch. 2, "Zero to a Million Followers in 30 Days"). Use when you have a real, verifiable result.
6. **Mystery + hard number** — `Why Are There [Huge Number] [Unusual Objects] on This [Place]?` — Veritasium (Ch. 3 referenced; in Ch. 2 context, "Why Are There 96 Million Black Balls on This Lake?" is the canonical example). Use for content where the number alone is the gag.
7. **"X-hour Y" compression** — `The [N]-[time unit] [aspirational activity]` — Ferriss (Ch. 2, "The 4-Hour Workweek"). Use when you can compress a long thing into a short number.
8. **Stat-stunner proof** — `The [N] fastest [category] in history have all happened in the last [short window], all by [proof source]` — Hutchinson on Nike Vaporfly (Ch. 1 frame referenced; Kane uses this Vaporfly stat as a hook example). Use when the data itself is the headline.
9. **Money-scale framing** — `$[Large Number] in [Short Timeframe]` — Hook Point Ch. 8 title ("From Hook to Scale: The Secret to $1.6 Billion in 48 Months"). Use for case-study headlines.

### Identity / Authority

10. **Title-as-authority** — `The [Domain] [Person Type]` — Michael Breus (Ch. 2, "The Sleep Doctor" — got him on Dr. Oz "more than 35 times"). Use to own a category in three to five words; Breus arrived at his by reading *The Brand Called You* and noticing "there was no term for doctors who deal with everything related to sleep" (Ch. 2).
11. **Identity-niche suffix** — `[Topic] for [Self-Deprecating Identity]` — Kilcullen, "For Dummies" (Ch. 5). Use to disarm the reader and claim a beginner's market.
12. **Persona naming for a community** — `[Maternal/Tribal Persona] and the [Outcast Community]` — Lady Gaga (Ch. 7, "Mother Monster" / "little monsters"). Use when you can give your audience a free identity to wear.
13. **Identity-authority self-intro** — `I'm a [function] strategist for [Tier 1 institution], brands, and [aspirational identity]` — Kane (Ch. 2, "I'm a digital and business strategist for Fortune 500 companies, brands, and celebrities"). Use for in-person intros where you want measured feedback; Kane keeps it *"left a bit vague on purpose—because I'm doing this in person—so I can measure the response in real time"* (Ch. 2).

### Imperative / Sensory

14. **Imperative action verb** — `Just [Action Verb]` — Nike (Ch. 2, "Just Do It"; note Kane flags this as a *tagline*, not a Hook Point — the rotating hooks live in the campaigns). Use as a brand anchor, rotate hooks around it.
15. **Extreme consequence / sensory opener** — `Your [sensory state]. Your [physical state]. You're [dire outcome]. Let's talk [product].` — Lupinacci for Timberland (Ch. 1, "Your eyes are frozen. Your skin has turned black. You're technically dead. Let's talk boots."). Use when the product earns its keep in extreme conditions.
16. **"Hot questions, hotter X" doubled adjective** — `The show with [hot X] and even hotter [Y]` — Hot Ones (Ch. 9, "The show with hot questions, and even hotter wings"). Use for show/format names that pair two qualities.

### Documentary / Mystery

17. **"Lost footage" framing** — `In [date], [people] disappeared in [place]. A year later their footage was found.` — Blair Witch (Ch. 2, verbatim). Use when realism is the entire hook.
18. **"Everything you've heard is true"** — `Everything you've heard is true.` — Blair Witch (Ch. 2). Use as confirmation when buzz already exists.
19. **Already-completed action** — `You can't [common action] because it's already [past tense]` — Geico / Martin Agency (Ch. 2, "You can't skip this Geico ad because it's already over" — Ad Age 2016 Campaign of the Year). Use when the format itself is being subverted.

### Friction-removal / Promise-compression

20. **Friction removal** — `[Service] to your door, [the friction we kill]` — Netflix (Ch. 1/Ch. 2, "No late fees" — Hastings's $40 *Apollo 13* late fee was the origin). Use when one specific frustration is universal in your category.
21. **"We're creating the [superlative] in the world"** — `Hi, [we're/I'm] [doing the superlative thing] in the world right now, and we can help you [outcome].` — Brownstein / Shareability speaking-event opener (Ch. 5). Use for in-room credibility intros.

### Promise / Problem-aware bridge

22. **Problem-aware If/Then bridge** — `If you're [seeking specific outcome with specific descriptors], then this is the most important thing you'll ever read.` — Clemens (Ch. 2). See Section D below for the full formula.
23. **"How I got" origin** — `How I got [coveted thing] in [high-status context]` — Elizabeth Banks interview clip framing (Ch. 3 referenced in process notes). Use for personal-story openers.
24. **Founder origin + place + problem** — `In [year] [founder] was visiting [specific place] and noticed [specific problem]. So they created [solution].` — Blake Mycoskie / TOMS (Ch. 2, "In 2006 he was visiting a small village in Argentina and noticed that the children there didn't have shoes."). Use when the origin story is itself the hook.

### Cause-pairing / Belief

25. **One-for-one cause pairing** — `With every [product] you buy, [brand] will [help one person in need]` — TOMS (Ch. 2, "one for one" — note Kane uses this as Exhibit A for hook fatigue: *"the original hook has been copied many times by other brands... a contributing factor to why the original hook lost steam"*). Use cautiously — copied to death.
26. **Belief + sacrifice pairing** — `Believe in something. Even if it means [extreme cost].` — Nike / Kaepernick (Ch. 5, "Believe in something. Even if it means sacrificing everything."). Use only when your brand has *demonstrated* history behind the stance (Gillette didn't, and it backfired).
27. **"Dismantling the establishment since [year]"** — `[Brand] [verb of revolt], dismantling the establishment since [founding year]` — Lupinacci's proposed Nike European-soccer line (Ch. 5, "Nike, Inc. Dismantling the establishment since 1971"). Use when the brand is a credible insurgent.

### Direct address / Open letter

28. **Open letter** — `I've watched you [specific negative action]…` — Brandon Stanton (Humans of New York) open letter to Trump (Ch. 3, "I've watched you retweet racist images…"). Use when you can address one person publicly and the audience leans in.

### Truth bomb / Surprise

29. **Three-word truth bomb** — `[Brutal three-word answer]` — Vaynerchuk (Ch. 2, "You're gonna die" — in response to "Give me three words for inspiration"). Use when the expected answer is cliché.

### Celebrity / Format

30. **Celebrity + unexpected stimulus** — `[Celebrities] [Verb] While [Unusual Activity]` — Hot Ones (Ch. 9, "Selena Gomez and Jimmy Fallon Cry While Eating Spicy Wings"). Use when you can pair a known person with a novel constraint.

### Triple-stack

31. **"Absolutely, positively" triple-stack** — `[Action] that absolutely, positively, [must happen by deadline]` — FedEx (Ch. 10, "It absolutely, positively, has to arrive overnight"). Use when reliability is the entire pitch.

### Anchor + rotation

32. **"Hold on to what matters"** — `Hold on to what [matters / resonates]` — Chatbooks (Ch. 10) — the company evolved from "Print $6 photo books" (feature hook) to this (purpose hook) when the feature framing got commoditized. Use when feature hooks have been copied.

## D. The If/Then formula (Craig Clemens)

Clemens — founder of Golden Hippo, *"more than a billion dollars in sales"* (Ch. 2) — gives one explicit formula:

> "It's a simple if/then formula: Point out your prospects' problems or needs after the if, and use your product as the solution to their problems after the then." (Ch. 2)

**Template:** `If you're [seeking specific outcome with specific descriptors], then [your specific promise].`

Kane preserves two Clemens-supplied examples verbatim (Ch. 2):

- Dating: *"If you're looking to increase the amount of dates you get with attractive, high-quality, smart women/men who have it together, then this is going to be the most important thing you'll ever read."*
- Golf: *"If you're looking to improve your golf game by five or ten strokes by the next time you set foot on the golf course, then pay close attention to the four secrets I'm going to share with you next."*

Clemens's own instruction is to use If/Then *as a starting point*, then *"A/B testing the Hook Points you come up with against other ideas that are a bit more off the wall"* (Ch. 2). It is a reliable floor, not a ceiling.

## E. Subverting expectations

Kane's most-repeated single tactic. *"Many good Hook Points get people to think differently, which is why subverting expectations with your Hook Points is a good tactic for capturing people's attention. One way to do so is to take commonly held beliefs or phrases and flip them on their heads."* (Ch. 2)

The mechanism: identify a phrase your audience says without thinking, then invert it. Kane's two examples:

- *"WARNING!! Safety is Dangerous."* (Ch. 2) — flips the conventional "play it safe."
- *"Meditation is a scam!"* (Ch. 2) — Kane meditates daily and uses the hook to introduce the real issue: *"because meditation is so popular, there's a lot of misinformation about what it is and how to do it properly."*

Gowda's caveat, in Kane's words: *"there's a delicate balance between subverting expectations and becoming a contrarian, so be conscious of this fact when coming up with hooks."* (Ch. 2) The flip must lead to substance. Yes Theory does this at format level — they wrap a familiar message ("seek discomfort") in unfamiliar adventures: *"Becoming Superman with the Ice Man, Wim Hof," "I Lived in a Luxury Airport for 4 Days. Nobody Noticed," "Letting a Coin Flip Control Our Lives for 24hrs (Dubai)."* (Ch. 2)

Use when you work in a crowded market: *"If you're a yoga teacher, meditation teacher, chef, or the like, you won't stand out if you repeat the same information that everyone else does."* (Ch. 2)

## F. Tested vs untested

Kane's discipline: opinion is unreliable. Three case studies make the point.

**Tim Ferriss + Google AdWords.** *"Ferriss was well aware of the importance of his title hook. In fact, he tested dozens of other titles in Google AdWords to find the best one. The 4-Hour Workweek beat out This Sucks, How to Live Like a Druglord and The Wild Gooserace (are you surprised?)."* (Ch. 2) The hook he kept is the brand he kept.

**Tesla Cybertruck.** Public opinion was polarized at launch — but *"generating more than 250,000 pre-orders of the Cybertruck right after it was announced is remarkable."* (Ch. 2) Negative comments are signal too: *"When people leave these negative comments on my posts, they're actually just helping me gain more visibility... as long as the positive outweighs the negative, you're in a good spot."* (Ch. 2)

**Michael Breus, "The Sleep Doctor."** Backlash for 5–10 years from colleagues calling him a "charlatan" and "sellout." Same colleagues, later: *"Hey, how do you get so much media attention?"* (Ch. 2) Strong response — even mixed — beats no response.

The testing channels Kane names (Ch. 2): A/B on social, on your email list, on search-engine advertising platforms, with friends and colleagues. Clemens's added warning: *"some of the concepts that are home runs are the ones you never would have expected. This is why it's essential to continually A/B test your ideas."* (Ch. 2)

## G. Where drafting goes wrong

Pulled from `_notes/05-mistakes.md`. The most common failure modes when sitting down to draft:

- **Drafting for yourself, not your audience.** *"One of the biggest mistakes people make when coming up with Hook Points—or any content, for that matter—is creating them for themselves instead of for their audience."* (Ch. 2)
- **Skipping Step 1.** *"You may be tempted to skip this step, but don't."* (Ch. 2) Without a benchmark, you can't tell if your hook is strong.
- **Copying competitors with less finesse.** Blockbuster copied Netflix and lost the market (Ch. 1 / Ch. 2 reference).
- **Hooks that don't survive the magazine-stand test.** If it doesn't beat the 30 other covers next to it, revise (Ch. 2 drafting frame).
- **Hooks that take more than 3 seconds to understand.** *"Make sure your Hook Point can be understood in three seconds max."* (Ch. 2)
- **Hooks that take *no* thought.** *"If someone doesn't think at all about your hook, then it's probably being ignored."* (Ch. 2)
- **Forcing humor.** *"Don't force it to be funny. Make sure you're confident in your ability to create effective Hook Points before tackling humor, or hire a great comedic talent to help you."* (Ch. 2)
- **Confusing hook with tagline or USP.** *"'Just Do It' is Nike's tagline, and their USP is their shoes. Today, their strongest Hook Points... are the athletes they sponsor."* (Ch. 2)
- **Selling in the hook.** *"People love to buy, but hate to be sold to."* (Ch. 2) The hook gets attention; it does not close.
- **First-draft hooks.** *"You won't typically find the perfect Hook Point on the first attempt (or even the first few attempts)."* (Ch. 2) If you didn't generate 50–100, you haven't drafted yet.

## Working procedure (drop-in)

1. Write the audience pain or desired outcome in one sentence.
2. Pull 20 winning hooks from your space (Step 1). Pull 10 losers (Step 2).
3. Draft 50–100 variants using the templates in Section C, the If/Then formula (Section D), and one explicit subversion attempt (Section E).
4. Combine your set with the winners on one page. Rank. Revise until yours beat theirs (Step 4).
5. Pick three. A/B test against your list, ads, or socials. Replace opinion with signal (Step 5).
6. Track when the winner stops landing. Repeat.
