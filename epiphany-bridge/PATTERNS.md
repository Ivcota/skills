# Epiphany Bridge — Patterns & Examples

Phase-by-phase generation patterns, calibrating examples, and the canonical potato gun exemplar threaded across all five phases. Skill loads this when proposing candidate phrasings so the output stays book-faithful instead of drifting to generic LLM voice.

The running example is **Russell Brunson's potato gun → funnels story** (Brunson, *Expert Secrets*, Secret #9) — the exact illustration Brunson uses to teach the framework — so each phase's exemplar threads cleanly into the next.

---

## Desire Archetypes *(book, Phase 1)*

Every external desire collapses into one of four:

| Archetype | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Win** | Get / earn / build something new | "Make money online", "land 10 clients", "lose 30 lbs" |
| **Retrieve** | Get back something lost | "Get my health back", "win back my marriage", "rebuild after the layoff" |
| **Escape** | Get out of a current condition | "Quit my 9-5", "leave the city", "get out of debt" |
| **Stop** | End an ongoing pain | "Stop the panic attacks", "stop fighting with my kids", "stop dreading Mondays" |

When generating Phase 1 candidates, tag the desire — it sets the emotional register of the whole opening.

---

## Internal Struggle Prompts *(book, Phase 1)*

External desire is the surface ask. Internal desire is the identity question underneath. If the user gives only the surface, ask one of these:

- "If you'd hit the external goal but nothing inside changed — would that have been enough?"
- "What would having the [external thing] have meant about you?"
- "Who were you afraid you were becoming if it didn't work out?"
- "What did you secretly want to prove? And to whom?"
- "What were you tired of feeling, not just tired of doing?"

Common internal desires (Brunson uses these openly): wanting to be a provider, wanting to be respected, wanting to give a spouse a specific gift (Brunson's: his wife retiring to be a stay-at-home mom), wanting to stop being the disappointment, wanting to be the kind of person who finishes what they start.

**Anti-pattern:** "I wanted to be the best version of myself" — too abstract. Push for the specific identity claim.

---

## Old Vehicle Patterns *(book, Phase 1)*

A list of 2-3 failed attempts. They don't need to be dramatic; they need to be **specific enough to feel real** and **familiar enough that the listener has tried them too**.

- For "make money online" → eBay flipping, dropshipping, MLM, computer parts websites, freelance gigs that didn't scale
- For "lose weight" → keto, calorie counting, Peloton, that one trainer, Whole30, the gym membership that lapsed
- For "find a partner" → the apps, the setups, the friend-of-a-friend, the "just put myself out there" phase
- For "grow a business" → the agency, hiring the consultant, the rebrand, the new logo, the SEO push

The wall-hit moment matters more than the failure count. End the old-vehicle beat on the specific frustration that made trying again feel pointless.

---

## Villain Archetypes *(book, Phase 2)*

Villains are almost never literal people. They're false beliefs, broken systems, or industry incentives. Generate villains in these categories:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **False belief** | "You need 10,000 hours before you can charge", "real artists don't make money", "you have to suffer to succeed" |
| **Industry norm / lie** | "The diet industry says calories in / calories out", "schools teach you to be employees", "VCs say you need a co-founder" |
| **System / gatekeeper** | "Google changing ad costs overnight", "the algorithm", "insurance companies", "the publishing gatekeepers" |
| **Cultural script** | "Hustle culture", "the corporate ladder lie", "the starving artist trope" |

**Anti-pattern villains:** "myself", "fear", "self-doubt", "imposter syndrome" — listeners can't side against these. If the user names one of these, redirect: "What belief did you have that turned out to be wrong? Let's name *that* — that's the villain."

The villain is what you'll be "throwing stones at" for the rest of the story.

---

## Stakes Phrasings *(book, Phase 2 — "what if")*

Stakes are concrete worst-case scenarios, not feelings.

- Bad: "I would have been devastated."
- Good: "I would have had to quit wrestling mid-season, get a 9-5, and watch my wife keep working two jobs through the pregnancy we were already planning."

Pattern: "If this failed, the next thing I would have *had to do* is ___, and the thing I would *never have gotten* is ___."

---

## Guide Types *(book, Phase 3)*

The guide can be:

- A specific person (named, with 1-line context: "Mike Filsaime, who ran a similar business to mine")
- A book or podcast or sermon
- A chance overheard comment
- An inspiration / divine impression (Brunson is open about using this)
- A pattern noticed in the wild

Always give the guide a one-line identity tag so the listener trusts the source.

---

## Epiphany Phrasing *(book, Phase 3 — the core craft)*

The job of the epiphany beat is to make the listener have the same aha you had. This means:

1. **Concrete mechanism, not abstraction.** "He told me about funnels" is abstraction. "He said it works like McDonald's — after the Big Mac, they ask if you want fries. He'd added an upsell page; 1 in 3 buyers took it; he could now outspend everyone on ads" is mechanism.
2. **Borrow a metaphor the listener already knows.** Brunson's potato gun epiphany uses McDonald's — universally familiar, instantly transmits the mechanism.
3. **Show your own moment of getting it.** "Confused, I asked him how. Then he gave me the big aha." The listener's brain syncs with the narrator's brain in the same beat.

**Test:** if someone read only Phase 3 in isolation, would they have the shift? If no, rewrite.

---

## Framework Naming Patterns *(book, Phase 4)*

Brunson always names frameworks. Naming makes a framework feel real, ownable, and re-usable.

Common containers:
- **The [N]-Step [Outcome] Framework** ("The 5-Step Funnel Framework")
- **The [Memorable Name] Method** ("The Epiphany Bridge Method")
- **The [Outcome] [Container]** ("The Perfect Webinar Script")
- **The [Acronym]** ("The MAGIC formula", "The DCF protocol")
- **The [Metaphor]** ("The Value Ladder", "The Dream 100")

Keep step count to 3-7. Fewer feels lightweight; more feels like a course outline.

---

## Case Study Tagging *(book, Phase 4 — audience-matched sub-stories)*

Capture each case study with these fields so the user can swap them by audience:

```
Name: [person + context — "Brandon and Kaelin Poulin, LadyBoss founders"]
Audience tag: [who this case study lands for — "weight-loss coaches", "info-product builders", "course creators"]
Result: [one-line concrete outcome — "took LadyBoss from idea to over a million women served"]
```

Brunson's rule: "If I were speaking to a dentist, I wouldn't share Kaelin Poulin's results, but I would tell Anissa Holmes's story." Always carry at least 2 case studies tagged by audience.

---

## Transformation Contrast *(book, Phase 5)*

Achievement and transformation are different beats and must land in that order.

- **Achievement = external close.** Mirrors Phase 1's external desire. "I wanted to make money to support my wife" → "We now generate millions in sales each month."
- **Transformation = internal close.** Mirrors Phase 1's internal desire. "I wanted my wife to retire and become a stay-at-home mom" → "She retired. We have five kids. She's home with them."

Pattern: "I set out to ___. I got that. But what actually changed was ___ — I became someone who ___."

**The transformation line is the one the listener remembers.** Spend more craft on it than on the achievement line.

---

## Length Calibration

| Use case | Word target | What gets compressed |
|---|---|---|
| Ad / email / FB Live opener | 200-400 | Trim old vehicles to 1, compress framework to 1 sentence, drop multiple case studies |
| Podcast intro / sales video / speaking opener | 500-900 | One old vehicle, one case study, full transformation beat |
| Webinar Story segment / keynote | 1000-1800 | Full 14 questions answered, 2-3 case studies, vivid scenes |
| Long-form (interview, book chapter) | Unbounded | Fully expand every beat, multi-scene |

The 30-Second cut ships regardless of use case.

## The Canonical Potato Gun Exemplar *(book — Brunson's own worked example)*

This is the story Brunson uses across pp. 175-190 to teach the framework. Use it as a calibrating example when proposing candidate phrasings — never as a template the user copies.

### Phase 1 — Backstory

- **Setting + character:** In college, newly engaged to Collette. Student-athlete wrestler — NCAA rules blocked a full-time job.
- **External desire (Win):** Make money online to support himself and his wife.
- **Internal desire:** Wanted Collette to retire from her two jobs and become a stay-at-home mom so they could start a family. He wanted to be the one who gave her that gift.
- **Old vehicles:** Selling stuff on eBay. Setting up websites to sell computer parts. Each experiment lost more money than it made and pushed them deeper into debt.

### Phase 2 — Journey / Conflict

- **The call:** Saw people selling "how to" info products online and seeming to do really well. Decided to try making one, despite not thinking he had a marketable skill. Filmed himself and a friend building a potato gun, turned it into a DVD.
- **Villain:** Google. He'd built a working system — $10/day in ads, one $37 DVD sold per day. Then Google changed advertising costs overnight; ad spend ballooned past $50 to sell the same $37 product.
- **Stakes:** Quit wrestling and get a full-time job — or Collette continues working two jobs to support him. Both options felt horrible. He felt completely hopeless.

### Phase 3 — New Opportunity

- **Guide:** Mike Filsaime — someone he'd met online running a similar info-product business. Russell had assumed Mike's business was also dead.
- **Epiphany:** "Business is better than ever." Mike had added one step to his website. Like McDonald's: after the Big Mac, they ask about fries and a Coke. After someone bought Mike's product, an upsell page offered another product at a discount. 1 in 3 buyers took it. Mike was now making more per customer — so he could afford to keep paying Google for traffic.
- **New opportunity:** Russell found a company selling potato gun kits, struck a fulfillment deal, added the kit as an upsell to the DVD. 1 in 3 DVD buyers bought the kit. The funnel was profitable enough to pay for Google ads and keep a hefty profit. Realization: **the secret wasn't what he was selling, but how he was selling it.**

### Phase 4 — Framework

- **Strategy:** Tested funnels across markets — weight loss, dating, couponing, network marketing, iPhone apps, supplements. Built different frameworks for different business types: lead-gen funnels, physical-product funnels, service funnels.
- **Your results:** One funnel generated 1.5 million leads in six weeks. Funnels took brand-new startups to eight figures in less than a year. A charity funnel generated $2 million+ in sales.
- **Others' results:** Brandon & Kaelin Poulin → LadyBoss → over a million women served on weight-loss journeys. Dr. Anissa Holmes → hundreds of new dental clients per month, replicated across countless other dentists. *(Note: Brunson swaps which case he tells based on audience.)*

### Phase 5 — Achievement and Transformation

- **Achievement:** ClickFunnels' funnels generate 1,000+ new leads a day and millions in monthly sales. Russell's company funnels print money.
- **Transformation:** Collette retired. She became a stay-at-home mom. They had five kids. And hundreds of other entrepreneurs Russell trained have freed themselves the same way — using funnels to launch their companies.

### The 30-Second Compression (Brunson's own)

```
Backstory:        Wanted to make money to support my wife.
Journey:          Started selling potato gun DVDs but got shut down by Google.
New Opportunity:  Mike Filsaime told me about an upsell, and I discovered funnels.
Framework:        Built funnel frameworks to grow my companies.
Achievement:      Made a ton of money, and my wife retired so she could have kids and be a stay-at-home mom.
```

Notice: Phase 5 in the 30-second version **fuses achievement and transformation into one line.** Both loops still close. That's the compression skill.
