# Partnership

**Core concept:** Partnership is your ability to structure and maintain strategic relationships that benefit everyone — the fifth and final P, and the "money step" where multiplier effects kick in. Instead of two parties arguing over how to slice a pie, partners build a whole new bakery together. Networking is no longer about finding clients one transaction at a time; it is about finding leverage — other people's lists, distribution, brand, capital, and products — so your reach compounds without proportional cost (S1, Ch 7 "Partnership", line 2068; "Go networking for partnerships… Not clients", line 2120).

**Why it works:** Once Pitch, Publish, Product, and Profile are in place, you become genuinely attractive to partners who already hold the missing pieces — a list, a distribution channel, a brand, a balance sheet, an adjacent product. The first four P's earn you the right to be in the room; Partnership is what turns the room into deals. Solid partnerships consistently produce outcomes better than planned — global expansion, investment at target valuation, equity in growth companies — because each side multiplies the other (S1, Ch 7 "Expect magic", line 2178: "you're constantly surprised by how things turn out better than you expected").

**Key insights:**
- "KPIs don't go out looking for clients, they go looking for partnerships." Go hunting for leverage — lists, distribution, brand, capital, products — not transactions (S1, Ch 7 "Go networking for partnerships… Not clients", line 2120).
- Three-calls rule: "Whenever you have an idea, no matter how crazy, make at least three calls to see if it's doable." Three calls reliably surprise you (S1, Ch 7 "Getting started with partnerships", line 2106).
- Reframe the question. Don't ask "How do I make it happen?" — ask "Who do I need to talk to?" (S1, Ch 7 "Getting started with partnerships", line 2102).
- Use the language of opportunities, not requests. Adults don't respond to needy framing; they respond to "you win, I win, the customer wins" (S1, Ch 8 "Requests vs. Opportunities", line 2395).
- Spirit of partnership first — fake intent kills deals. "You genuinely have to care about the wants and desires of the person you're doing the deal with." Even Larry Page calls Google employees "team" for this reason (S1, Ch 7 "The spirit of partnership", line 2032).
- A network isn't business cards. "A network is a group of people who share opportunities" — people who will take your call and trust you have something valuable. Build it by habitually spotting opportunities for others (S1, Ch 8 "It's not just what you know", line 2422).
- Expect magic. Solid partnerships consistently deliver outcomes better than planned (S1, Ch 7 "Expect magic", line 2178).

## Sub-models

### Five partnership / deal types

The five archetypes to start with — in roughly increasing complexity (S1, Ch 7 "What kind of deals can you start with?", line 2068):

1. **Affiliate partnerships.** Commission-paying tracking-link networks. "Anyone who arrives at the site through your link and buys, you get [X%] commission." Worked example: a t-shirt affiliate sale logs the partner and pays "a commission of twenty percent" (S1, Ch 7 "Affiliate partnerships", line 2074).
2. **Co-promotion.** Two similar-sized lists mail each other's offers — database-for-database. The cleanest deal when two parties have roughly equivalent reach in adjacent niches (S1, Ch 7 "What kind of deals", line 2068).
3. **Product creation partnerships.** Team up to bundle a better outcome — e.g. personal trainer + image consultant + photographer = a "Complete Makeover" package none could sell alone (S1, Ch 7 "What kind of deals", line 2068).
4. **Packaging up.** Bundle your products with adjacent businesses' existing products. The classic example is the "Movie-Meal Deal": a cinema ticket bundled with a nearby restaurant's meal, sold as one offer (S1, Ch 7 "What kind of deals", line 2068).
5. **Free-bundle group.** Multiple businesses combine free offers into one irresistible basket — each participant gets exposure to every other participant's audience at zero marginal cost (S1, Ch 7 "What kind of deals", line 2068).

### Three partnership-management tools

Once a deal exists, three tools sustain it (S1, Ch 7 "The tools", line 2046):

1. **Negotiations.** Win/win, professional, never manipulative. Ethical caveat: "I do not believe you should use negotiation tactics to create unfair agreements; they simply won't last… Good negotiations never involve manipulation" (S1, Ch 7 "Negotiations", line 2050).
2. **Written agreements.** MOU, contract, email confirmation, employment contract — whatever fits the deal. Ethical caveat: "Regardless of the way you choose to document the agreement, do it for the best interests of everyone involved, not so you can enforce a carefully concealed clause" (S1, Ch 7 "Written agreements", line 2054).
3. **Systems.** Affiliate-tracking software, time-tracking, crowd-funded shareholding tools — the operational backbone that makes the deal scale beyond a handshake (S1, Ch 7 "The tools", line 2046).

### Six rituals for meeting a potential partner

The choreography of a partnership meeting — verbatim sequence (S1, Ch 7 "Meeting a potential partner", line 2151):

1. **Set the scene.** Meet on neutral, pleasant ground — restaurant, hotel, club, lounge. The setting itself signals professionalism and equality before a word is spoken.
2. **Bring a gift.** Thoughtful, not expensive — a book, an article, something specific to them. Shows you were already thinking of them before the meeting.
3. **Make friends first.** Don't talk business until you genuinely like each other. People do deals with people they like, not with strangers running through an agenda.
4. **Do business last.** The deal itself should take just twenty to thirty minutes. If the friendship is real and both pitches are clean, the deal is fast.
5. **Pick up the bill.** Especially if you instigated the meeting. Small cost, large signal.
6. **Follow through.** Act within fifteen minutes on every off-hand promise. The promise you keep within the hour is worth ten promises kept next week.

### Five partnership-scouting questions

Replace the worker-bee question "Can I make a sale here?" with five KPI questions when you meet someone (S1, Ch 7 "Go networking for partnerships… Not clients", line 2128):

1. Do they have a **list** of potential clients?
2. Are they interested in **co-producing a product**?
3. Have they got a **product that would be attractive to your contact list**?
4. Would this person be a **valuable addition to the team**?
5. Is there a **beneficial contact** you can put them in touch with?

If the answer to all five is no, this person isn't a partner — they may be a client, a friend, or simply not the right room.

### Three calls rule

"Whenever you have an idea, no matter how crazy, make at least three calls to see if it's doable" (S1, Ch 7 "Getting started with partnerships", line 2106).

Why three: one call is anecdote, two is coincidence, three forces you to actually test the idea against the market. Three calls almost always surprise you — either the idea is more doable than you thought, or one of the three points you at the real opportunity. Worked example: Kevin Harrington "picked up the phone and called 50 Cent" — got him on TV, product sold over $20M in its first few months (S1, Ch 7 "Getting started", line 2114).

## Ethical boundary

The Partnership chapter is the strongest ethical cluster in the book. Five rules:

1. **Don't fake the spirit of partnership.** "You can't fake it when it comes to a successful partnership; you genuinely have to care about the wants and desires of the person you're doing the deal with" (S1, Ch 7 "The spirit of partnership", line 2032). Without genuine care, every tool below — negotiations, agreements, systems — becomes a manipulation lever, and the deal collapses on first contact with friction.

2. **Don't squeeze every drop out of a relationship.** "Don't try to squeeze every drop out of a relationship… being a good partner ensures you are a Key Person of Influence for the long run" (S1, Ch 7 "The spirit of partnership", line 2040). Harrington's thirty-year reputation as "fair and professional" — built in an industry "known for speed, hustle and sometimes cut-throat behavior" — was the asset that made him "usually the first person people would call when a hot deal came along" (S1, Ch 6 intro, line 1877). Allow others their triumphs; celebrate the wins of others even when it's not yet your turn.

3. **Never use negotiation tactics to create unfair agreements; never manipulate.** "I do not believe you should use negotiation tactics to create unfair agreements; they simply won't last… Good negotiations never involve manipulation" (S1, Ch 7 "Negotiations", line 2050). Good negotiation revolves around seeing the full picture from both sides and requires both parties to make sacrifices for the good of the partnership long-term.

4. **Document agreements for the best interests of everyone — not to enforce a concealed clause.** "Regardless of the way you choose to document the agreement, do it for the best interests of everyone involved, not so you can enforce a carefully concealed clause" (S1, Ch 7 "Written agreements", line 2054). The intent behind the paperwork matters as much as the paperwork itself.

5. **Turn down deals that aren't true long-term partnerships.** Darren Finkelstein turned down a major opportunity because "it didn't feel like a true partnership" beyond a fast transaction. Stated principle: **"If it's not good long term, it's not good short term"** (S1, Case Studies — Darren Finkelstein, line 2689). The discipline of saying no to short-term-only deals is what preserves capacity for the partnerships that compound.

## Common Partnership mistakes

- **Networking for clients instead of partnerships.** Why it fails: "Going out to a networking event looking for a client is like trying to get to China on foot. You want to find who already flies there in a jet." Single-sale hunting wastes the room. Fix: walk in with the five scouting questions — list, co-produce, attractive product, team addition, beneficial contact (S1, Ch 7 "Go networking for partnerships… Not clients", line 2120).

- **Going it alone / trying to create the "original mistake".** Why it fails: "I am always surprised when someone tries to do something on their own; I'm convinced that some people want to create an 'original mistake'." Most people, left alone, become "distracted, bored, discouraged, uninspired" and won't complete the KPI outcomes. There's no such thing as a self-made millionaire — success is always a team sport. Fix: get mentors, coaches, peer groups; surround yourself with people who already have the result you want (S1, Ch 8 "You can't do it alone", line 2311).

- **Using the language of requests instead of opportunities.** Why it fails: "Unless you are a child asking your parents for something small, the language of requests does not work very often." Requests turn your problems into someone else's problems. Fix: speak the language of opportunity — frame every ask so the other side wins by helping you win. "I need more sales" becomes "Would you like to follow up on hot leads and get ten percent?" (S1, Ch 8 "Requests vs. Opportunities", line 2406).

- **Faking the spirit of partnership / squeezing the relationship.** Why it fails: tools without genuine care produce agreements that "simply won't last." Fix: see the deal from the other side of the table; work as hard for them as you want them to work for you, or more so (S1, Ch 7 "The spirit of partnership", line 2032).

- **Skipping ahead to Partnership before the first four P's are in place.** Why it fails: "You can't leap into a lucrative partnership with a poor pitch, no credibility, a broken business model, and being invisible." Almost having a book = zero books on Amazon; almost having a product = nothing to sell but your time. Fix: complete Pitch, Publish, Product, Profile to a high standard before initiating partner conversations (S1, Ch 2 "The order is important / Then the results come", line 869).
