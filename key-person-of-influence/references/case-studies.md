# Case Studies — Key Person of Influence

Worked examples from *Key Person of Influence* (Harrington & Priestley, 2015), grouped by which of the 5 P's each one illustrates most clearly. Every case is drawn from the book and ends with a verbatim citation.

Use these as patterns to imitate — not just as proof points. When you can map your own situation onto one of these stories, you've found the move you should be making next.

---

## Direct-Response TV ecosystems (Kevin Harrington's work)

These cases mostly illustrate the compounding effect of a sharp Pitch, a Product engineered around a single insight, and Partnerships that lock distribution.

### Ginsu Knife — Kevin Harrington & Arnold Morris

Kevin spotted a product-plus-pitchman combination at a trade fair (Curiosity Overload in action), then cut a deal to run the world's first 30-minute infomercial in dead-air slots from midnight to 6 a.m. The pitch and the slot were both unconventional; the combination sold three million units in under five years.

- **P's illustrated:** Pitch + Partnership
- **Author lesson:** A great pitch in an unwanted time-slot beats a weak pitch in prime-time — distribution gets unlocked the moment the pitch is right.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 1 — Curiosity Overload", line: 248, quote: "Ginsu Knife… midnight to six a.m. and ended up selling three million units… in under five years."}

### Jack LaLanne Power Juicer — Kevin Harrington & Jack LaLanne

Rather than invent a product and look for a face, Kevin worked backwards from LaLanne's existing fresh-juice routine and personal story. The product was wrapped around the man's mountain of value, not bolted onto him. It crossed $1B in global sales.

- **P's illustrated:** Pitch + Product
- **Author lesson:** "You are already standing on a mountain of value" — build the product around the person's existing story, don't invent a story to fit a product.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 1 — Already a unique set of skills", line: 583, quote: "Jack Lalanne… 'Power Juicer,' that product has exceeded one billion dollars in sales globally"}

### Snuggie — Kevin Harrington & the Snuggie team

The Snuggie team built three sequential pitches (one for investors, one for on-air, one for retailers) and used the line "we'll give you the original, but if you sell other brands, we can take our brand out of your store" to lock retail shelves against knockoffs. Over 20M units sold.

- **P's illustrated:** Pitch + Partnership
- **Author lesson:** A pitch isn't one speech — it's a different version for each audience in the value chain, each one engineered to advance the deal.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 3 'Pitch' — Snuggie example", line: 952, quote: "By pitching the deal well to investors, then on TV and then to the retailers, they've sold more than twenty million units"}

### Wolfman Jack — *151 #1 Hits*

The original infomercial just played music with a phone number on screen — and flopped. Kevin rewrote the in-between segments as a benefit-stacked pitch: feeling, curation, savings, fun as the party host. Same product, same songs, new pitch. "Immediately sales went through the roof."

- **P's illustrated:** Pitch
- **Author lesson:** Articulating the payoff is what turns a product people might want into a product people buy now. Without it you have a song with a phone number.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 3 'Pitch' — Articulate the payoff", line: 1220, quote: "Between the songs, he articulated the benefits… Immediately sales went through the roof."}

### Great Wok of China — Kevin Harrington & Wally Nash

A single physical-product insight — each wok hammered by hand 6,000 times — was sold as a bundle (wok + recipe book + utensils) at $40. 45 million units shipped, roughly $10M in profit. The "idea" was communicated through the object itself.

- **P's illustrated:** Product
- **Author lesson:** A physical product can be intellectual property — when the insight (6,000 hammers) lives inside the object, the product is the pitch.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 5 'Product' — Physical products", line: 1647, quote: "hand hammered 6000 times… sold with a recipe book… sold forty-five million of them."}

### City-Kitty — Kevin Harrington

A training kit to teach cats to use a regular toilet. The product was so hyper-niche and unusual that networks ran free segments on it. Over $1M in profit in under twelve months.

- **P's illustrated:** Product
- **Author lesson:** Niche products earn media for free. The narrower and weirder the specific problem, the larger the free amplification.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 5 'Product' — Niche products are higher value", line: 1738, quote: "City-Kitty… over one million dollars in profit in under twelve months!"}

### 50 Cent music system — Kevin Harrington

Kevin used the three-calls habit — he just picked up the phone and called 50 Cent. They put him on TV. The product made over $20M in its first few months.

- **P's illustrated:** Partnership
- **Author lesson:** Don't ask "how do I make it happen?" — ask "who do I need to talk to?" Then make the call. KPIs answer KPIs.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 7 'Partnership' — Getting started", line: 2114, quote: "called 50 Cent… product made sales of over twenty million dollars in its first few months"}

## Big corporate JV partnerships

### QGR — Quantum + Guthy-Renker JV

Quantum and Guthy-Renker each had a pile of product deals that weren't right for either brand individually. Instead of letting those deals die, they spun out a new joint-venture company (QGR) to run them. QGR quickly made tens of millions in profit on inventory both parents would have abandoned.

- **P's illustrated:** Partnership
- **Author lesson:** Two parties stop arguing over slicing a pie and instead build a bakery — JVs convert "wrong-fit" deals into pure upside.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 7 'Partnership'", line: 2018, quote: "Quantum and Guthy Renker… spun out a new company, QGR… tens of millions of dollars profit"}

### Cannes → Rupert Murdoch — Kevin Harrington

At Cannes, Kevin observed that Hollywood relies on international revenue much more than domestic. He "hijacked the habit" — applied the same playbook to infomercials in Europe — and ended up doing a distribution deal with Rupert Murdoch. Company revenue jumped from $100M to over $500M.

- **P's illustrated:** Partnership (with Profile lifting him into the rooms)
- **Author lesson:** Observe other industries for strategies; the move that's tired in one industry is breakthrough in the next.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 8 — Hijack your habits", line: 2285, quote: "deal with Rupert Murdoch and his company jumped from 100 million dollars in sales to over 500 million dollars"}

## Authorship leading to opportunities

### *Act Now!* → Shark Tank — Kevin Harrington

Kevin's first book *Act Now!* generated radio interviews. Reality-TV producer Mark Burnett heard one of those interviews, ordered the book, and invited Kevin to be an original Shark. That single book led to 100+ deals filmed on television.

- **P's illustrated:** Publish + Profile
- **Author lesson:** The book doesn't have to sell millions; it just has to land in the right hand. Opportunities flow to authors who are already KPIs.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 4 'Publish'", line: 1384, quote: "found his way onto the show Shark Tank because of his first book, Act Now!"}

## Coaching / Speaking

### Daniel Priestley — London launch

Daniel arrived in London with no network. Through a single friend, he hosted a ~$2,500 dinner party for around 40 entrepreneurs and pitched the KPI idea over the meal. The dinner generated several great meetings that turned into partnerships. Launch-week result: 800 attendees and $160k in sales — with zero paid ads.

- **P's illustrated:** Partnership (built on a sharp Pitch)
- **Author lesson:** Networking for partnerships, not clients — one well-hosted room of the right people beats a year of cold outreach.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 7 'Partnership'", line: 2026, quote: "over 800 people attended our events and we made over $160,000 in sales"}

### Lazo Freeman — Radical 12-week transformations

Lazo repositioned from "personal trainer" to "radical 12-week body transformations with highly successful people who do amazing things in their work but very ordinary things naked." He added a book (*Six Pack Chicks*), a DVD, and a steady Facebook stream of before/after proof. Price rose from ~30% above industry average to $15,000 per transformation, with a waiting list.

- **P's illustrated:** Pitch + Profile (across all five over time)
- **Author lesson:** A provocative, specific, emotionally-charged micro-niche pitch lets you price 10x the category — and a waiting list does your selling for you.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Case Studies — Lazo Freeman", line: 2603, quote: "Today Lazo charges $15,000 for a Radical 12-Week Transformation, and he has a waiting list!"}

## Professional services

### Mike Symes — financial marketing

Mike sharpened his pitch ("ignite their brands, illuminate their points of difference, spread like wildfire"), wrote *Find Your Firebrand*, built a brand-assessment tool as a productized service, and became chairman of an industry body. Within six months, the CEO of a UK bank was asking him for coffee.

- **P's illustrated:** All five — Pitch + Publish + Product + Profile + Partnership
- **Author lesson:** When the five P's compound, you stop pitching for business and start being pitched to.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Case Studies — Mike Symes", line: 2619, quote: "He went from being great at pitching for new business to having people pitch to him to become a client."}

### IP lawyer (KPI client)

A solo IP lawyer worked with the KPI Program to create several informational products and write a book. The combination converted her firm from a local practice into one where hundreds of inbound prospects globally asked her firm to advise on their business — through a productized service.

- **P's illustrated:** Publish + Product
- **Author lesson:** Even regulated, "you-have-to-be-here" service professions can productize their method and scale globally through information products plus a productized service.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 5 'Product' — Implementation, not ideas", line: 1752, quote: "hundreds of people want her firm to advise on their business. She's created a productized service"}

## Aviation

### Paul Fowler — Oxfordshire Flying Club / "Spitfire Project"

Paul identified the Spitfire micro-niche within general aviation, phoned the editor of *Pilot* magazine, and pitched an article — the editor gave him a double-page spread on the spot, before he'd even finished pitching. He then built a "Zero to Hero" productized flight-school program with audio/video flight briefings, and landed BBC and partnership deals.

- **P's illustrated:** Pitch + Publish + Product + Profile + Partnership
- **Author lesson:** A pitch sharpened around a true micro-niche disarms gatekeepers — the editor doesn't wait for the end of your sentence when the niche is right.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Case Studies — Paul Fowler", line: 2639, quote: "double-page spread dedicated to the Spitfire project on the spot, even before he had finished his pitch"}

## E-commerce / Physical product

### Jane Malyon — The English Cream Tea Company

Jane wrote *Play Nicely*, then pivoted to a chilled-delivery high-tea gift box. To build profile, she set an audacious Guinness World Record goal for the largest cream-tea gathering — and won it. BBC, newspaper, magazine and conference coverage followed. Within two years she was fielding offers to license the brand.

- **P's illustrated:** Publish + Product + Profile
- **Author lesson:** An audacious, photogenic profile event creates years of inbound — go for one big credibility moment instead of constant small posts.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Case Studies — Jane Malyon", line: 2665, quote: "set the world record for the largest gathering in one place, all enjoying English cream tea… featured on the BBC"}

### eBay couple (anonymous)

A couple built a $400k/year eBay business, then packaged "how we did it" as an information kit. That single info kit went on to generate $1.2M — three times their underlying business.

- **P's illustrated:** Product
- **Author lesson:** Your operational know-how is often more valuable to peers than your underlying business — productize the method.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 5 'Product' — An information kit", line: 1712, quote: "That information kit generates over 1.2 million!"}

### John Cassidy — *7 Mistakes People Make with Their Headshot Photos*

A photographer who turned his domain knowledge into a short before/after picture book. The book itself became his most effective business-development tool — he uses it to win clients rather than to "sell books."

- **P's illustrated:** Publish
- **Author lesson:** A picture book is a valid format for visual industries — and pictures of customer transformations are themselves the sales argument.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 4 'Publish' — Picture book", line: 1450, quote: "7 Mistakes People Make with Their Headshot Photos… series of before and after photos"}

## KPI Program alumni — title-as-positioning

### Shireen Smith, Chris Davies, Marianne Page

Three alumni whose book titles each broadcast the niche in the title itself:
- *Legally Branded* — Shireen Smith
- *Winning Client Trust* — Chris Davies
- *Process to Profit* — Marianne Page

A reader who hears any of these titles instantly knows what the author is a KPI in — no further explanation needed.

- **P's illustrated:** Publish
- **Author lesson:** Many more people will hear your title than read your book. Pick a title that names the micro-niche it positions you in.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 4 'Publish' — Choosing the title", line: 1500, quote: "Legally Branded… Winning Client Trust… Process to Profit… perfect for getting across the message that the author is a Key Person of Influence"}

### Anchor titles — *Six Pack Chicks*, *Naked Divorce*, *Honey Let's Buy a Boat*

Three thought-leadership titles that each broadcast a hyper-specific niche outcome the moment you hear them — Lazo Freeman's body-transformation book for ambitious women, Adèle Théron's 21-day program for people going through divorce, and Darren Finkelstein's complete guide to buying and owning a powerboat.

- **P's illustrated:** Publish
- **Author lesson:** The title formula is "concrete promise / subtitle that names the audience". Cryptic titles forfeit the positioning.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 4 'Publish' — Thought leadership", line: 1424, quote: "Honey Let's Buy A Boat… Six Pack Chicks… Naked Divorce – The 21 Day Program For Your New Life."}

### "The Posture Doctor" (KPI client)

A chiropractic doctor who specialises in correcting poor posture and built a YouTube channel around it. In the first year, over one million people watched her videos — and the inbound opportunities followed.

- **P's illustrated:** Profile (anchored on a niche-specialist handle)
- **Author lesson:** "The [Niche] Doctor / Guy / Coach" is a single-noun brand that names the micro-niche. Use it to make yourself unmistakably the KPI on Google.
- **Citation:** {source: S1, location: "Ch 6 'Profile'", line: 1899, quote: "in the first year over one million people watched her videos."}

## Darren Finkelstein — Partnership ethics

Darren ran a boat dealership and repositioned from "I sell boats" to "I'm in the boat-ownership business." He wrote *Honey, Let's Buy a Boat* in 200 days, added a dozen wrap-around services (servicing, refuelling, washing, valet, owner education) under one ownership umbrella, and earned media coverage and Google page-one presence.

The decisive case study, though, is what he turned down: a major opportunity that didn't feel like a true partnership — just a fast transaction. His stated principle: "If it's not good long term, it's not good short term."

- **P's illustrated:** Pitch + Publish + Product + Profile + Partnership (with a Partnership-ethics lesson)
- **Author lesson:** Turn down deals — even big ones — that aren't real partnerships. Long-term reputation is the asset that compounds; one bad short-term deal eats years of it.
- **Citations:**
  - {source: S1, location: "Case Studies — Darren Finkelstein", line: 2677, quote: "You're not in the boat sales business you're in the boat ownership business."}
  - {source: S1, location: "Case Studies — Darren Finkelstein", line: 2689, quote: "Darren even turned down a major opportunity because it didn't feel like a true partnership… 'If it's not good long term, it's not good short term'"}
