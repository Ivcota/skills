# Pitch

**Core concept:** Pitch is the first of the five P's and the foundation everything else stands on: your ability to communicate your value and uniqueness through the spoken word — most concretely, the answer you give to "What do you do?" The authors define a perfect pitch as one powerful enough that a single answer "would mean they want to tell everyone about you. They would want to phone their most prized contacts and introduce you" (Ch 3, line 932). Until that bar is met, the rest of the method has nothing to amplify.

**Why it works:** A great pitch turns every interaction into a potential deal — listeners either act immediately or refer you, because they are clear, credible, and emotionally engaged. The mechanism is leverage: every downstream P (book, product, profile, partnership) is built on top of the pitch, so "a poor pitch will yield a boring book, products that are impossible-to-sell, websites that don't keep people reading and fruitless partnerships" (Ch 3, line 1327). Fix the pitch, and everything else compounds; skip it, and amplification just makes the weak message louder.

**Key insights:**
- The pitch must contain your "why" — your Big Game. "Your pitch must contain your why. It's your compass" (Ch 3, line 1000); Bill Gates pitched "a PC on every desk," Oprah pitched "a daily dose of inspiration with women all over the world."
- Use a sniper rifle, not a shotgun. "You need to become a laser-sharp marksman focusing on a very specific micro-niche" (Ch 3, line 1080) — the cast-your-net-wide approach is dead online.
- Beware the polite response. "A polite comment is one of the worst responses you can get… It is polite responses that keep you looking good, but going nowhere" (Ch 3, line 964) — a strong negative reaction beats a benign one.
- Ground the pitch in reality through a Customer, Technology, or Industry insight (Ch 3, line 1188) — pitches without lived insight sound made up.
- Articulate three payoffs, not features: "You get to earn more money with less struggle. You get more recognition within the industry you love. You get to attract more of the right opportunities that energize you" (Ch 3, line 1233).
- Embrace critics. "If someone says to you, 'That won't work,' don't be offended, get curious" (Ch 3, line 1311) — objections become the next iteration of the pitch.
- If your pitch sounds too rehearsed, you haven't rehearsed it enough. "If your pitch sounds too rehearsed, you haven't rehearsed it enough" (Ch 3, line 1299) — you should be able to talk for three minutes or three hours from the heart.

## Sub-models

### Big Game (six components)

Every great pitch is built around a "Big Game" worth playing — the why behind the work. The authors define six things a Big Game must contain:

- It must be fun
- It must have rules
- There must be players
- There must be a prize
- There must be a way to win
- There must be a way to lose

"It must be fun … It must have rules … There must be players … There must be a prize … There must be a way to win … There must be a way to lose" (Ch 3, line 1029). Without a way to lose there are no stakes; without rules there is no game. The Big Game is what you'd love to play for the next three years, win or lose — and it lives inside the pitch as your compass.

### Two types of problems worth solving

A pitch must center on a real problem. The book names exactly two kinds:

- Something people or businesses are already doing/buying which you can provide better, cheaper, more conveniently or with more emotional benefit
- An unsolved problem people or businesses have or will have soon (as a consequence of technological, economic, political or social change), which you can predict and solve

"Something people or businesses are already doing/buying which you can provide better … An unsolved problem people or businesses have or will have soon" (Ch 3, line 1166). If your pitch doesn't map cleanly to one of these two, it's not yet a pitch.

### Three insights every grounded pitch is born from

The pitch must come from somewhere real. The authors identify three legitimate origin insights:

- A **Customer Insight** — you were a customer who hit a wall and set out to right the wrongs
- A **Technology Insight** — you spotted a new application for a technology you know well
- An **Industry Insight** — you know your niche so well you can see how it should be done better

"A Customer Insight … A Technology Insight … An Industry Insight" (Ch 3, line 1188). Pitches that aren't grounded in one of these three feel manufactured and don't survive contact with real audiences.

### Seven-step presentation pitch structure

When you have more than a sentence — at an event, on a podcast, in a webinar — use the seven-step arc:

1. **Position yourself as clear and credible** — open by anchoring why the listener should pay attention
2. **Articulate the problem and how you noticed it** — name the pain and your origin point
3. **Extrapolate on the impact of the problem** — show the cost of leaving it unsolved
4. **Solve it** — your method/product/approach
5. **Prove it** — case studies, results, third-party validation
6. **Ask for what you want** — the explicit next step
7. **Leave people uplifted** — close on inspiration, not transaction

"Position yourself as clear and credible … Articulate the problem … Extrapolate … Solve it … Prove it … Ask for what you want … Leave people uplifted" (Ch 3, line 1269).

### Three core payoffs (Kevin's rule)

Every pitch must spell out three payoffs in the listener's life, in this order:

- **More money** — with less struggle
- **More recognition** — in the industry you love
- **More of the right opportunities** — that energize you

"Kevin recommends you have three core benefits … You get to earn more money with less struggle. You get more recognition … You get to attract more of the right opportunities" (Ch 3, line 1226). If a pitch lists features without naming all three payoffs, it under-delivers.

## Ethical boundary

**Don't take on a micro-niche you don't personally identify with.**

"If you are not a vegetarian, don't make it part of your niche just because you think it makes business sense. You will either get found out as a hypocritical meat-eater" (Ch 3, line 1076).

The reasoning: a pitch built around a niche you don't actually live will either expose you as a hypocrite or trap you inside a business you grow to resent. The niche has to be one you'd enjoy connecting with and one whose people would enjoy connecting with you — otherwise the pitch becomes performance, and the rest of the five P's compound a lie.

## Common Pitch mistakes (deep dive)

**1. Leading with credentials or a generic job title**
Saying "I'm a management consultant" or "I help businesses grow" is boring and general, leaves over 80% of potential on the table in every interaction, and earns only a polite response. None of those answers open the floodgates of opportunity. Fix: construct a social pitch that lights people up — laser-focused on a micro-niche, naming a specific problem with a clear payoff, honed to be powerful in under two minutes. "All ten of those answers will get a polite response… None of them will open up the floodgates of opportunity" (Ch 3, line 1121).

**2. Settling for a polite response**
"Sounds interesting" is a meaningless non-response. Polite responses keep you looking good but going nowhere — you can go nowhere for years on polite reactions. Fix: aim for an emotionally-charged response. They should love it or hate it, ideally take immediate action; a strong negative reaction is better than a polite benign one. "A polite comment is one of the worst responses you can get… It is polite responses that keep you looking good, but going nowhere" (Ch 3, line 964).

**3. Going into a pitch unprepared or cold**
You only get one chance to make a first impression. If your pitch sounds too rehearsed, you haven't rehearsed it enough; most pitches Kevin hears are far from perfect, and cold attempts fail. Fix: practice in front of a camera, mirror, spouse, kids; get mentors with fresh perspective; write out the presentation pitch, learn it, and own it so it comes from the heart rather than memory. "Don't approach this moment ill-prepared… Practice. Don't ever go into a pitch situation cold" (Ch 3, line 946).

**4. Fighting critics or getting defensive about your pitch**
Typical first-time entrepreneurs want people to love their ideas and hate having flaws pointed out — but a critical eye is exactly what sharpens the next iteration. Fix: when someone says "That won't work," get curious, ask why, write down the answers, then build the objection's resolution into the pitch. "If someone says to you, 'That won't work,' don't be offended, get curious" (Ch 3, line 1311).

**5. Being a generalist / trying to please everyone**
When you try to catch everyone in your net, you catch no one. The internet doesn't help generalists stand out; broadness creates no density or gravity to attract anyone. You cannot be a KPI at "all sorts of things." Fix: claim a micro-niche, pick something specific you'll become known for, and turn away anything that isn't quite right — sniper rifle, not shotgun. "You can't be a Key Person of Influence at 'all sorts of things.' You have to pick something that you are going to become known for" (Ch 1, line 508).

**6. Pitching from theory instead of a real insight**
A pitch invented at a whiteboard — without a Customer, Technology, or Industry insight underneath — sounds manufactured and doesn't survive contact with real audiences. Fix: ground every pitch in one of the three insight origins; if none of them are true for you in this niche, you haven't earned the pitch yet. "A Customer Insight … A Technology Insight … An Industry Insight" (Ch 3, line 1188).
