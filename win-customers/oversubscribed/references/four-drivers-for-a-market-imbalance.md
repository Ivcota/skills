# The Four Drivers for a Market Imbalance

**Core concept:** Become oversubscribed by creating more buyers than sellers through one of four positions: innovation, relationships, convenience, or price. Innovation gives buyers something new that only one supplier offers; relationships make buyers ignore alternatives; convenience removes the effort of finding alternatives; and price uses a structural efficiency to undercut other suppliers while remaining profitable. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1503-1519]]

**Why it works:** Each driver changes the buyer-to-seller balance in a different way. A distinctive innovation restricts supply, an owned relationship reduces shopping around, a frictionless offer makes alternatives feel costly, and an efficiency advantage permits a lower profitable price. These positions demand different resources, so the business must choose one main position and may use another as a secondary rather than trying to dominate all four. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1508-1528]]

## The four positions

| Driver | How it creates the imbalance | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Innovation** | Create something “new and shiny” that nobody else sells, leaving one supplier and a niche of buyers who want it. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1508-1510]] |
| **Relationships** | Build such a powerful relationship that buyers ignore other sellers. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1511-1513]] |
| **Convenience** | Offer the most frictionless expression of what buyers want, in the right place and at the right time, so they resist spending more time, money, and energy on alternatives. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1514-1516]] |
| **Price** | Invest in an asset or efficiency that lets the business charge less than other suppliers can while still making a profit. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1517-1519]] |

## Key insights

- **Choose one main market position.** Established markets often contain major players occupying each of the four positions, with one driver dominant and another secondary. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1521-1528]]
- **Do not combine positions whose economics contradict each other.** High innovation conflicts with maximum convenience because innovation takes time to scale; ultra-low price conflicts with relationship leadership because relationships cost money to build. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1526-1528]]
- **Innovation does not have to be an epic breakthrough.** It may be a subtle repackaging, an import from another market, or a familiar offer with a new feature. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #1 Innovation,” [raw:1555-1564]]
- **Innovation has three forms.** Product innovation changes what is offered, systems innovation changes how it is delivered, and brand innovation makes an ordinary product more desirable through packaging and positioning. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #1 Innovation,” [raw:1577-1591]]
- **Relationships can be strengthened three ways.** Become more influential, become better known, or put lasting deals in place so customers stop shopping around. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #2 Relationship,” [raw:1643-1658]]
- **Convenience means reducing market friction.** The source defines friction as the time, energy, effort, and know-how required to buy; it is reduced through better distribution, better market information, or automation. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #3 Convenience,” [raw:1678-1686; raw:1699-1713]]
- **A price position comes from lower costs, not thinner margins.** Invest in useful assets, refine away costs that add no customer value, or systemize with technology instead of people. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #4 Price,” [raw:1733-1761]]

## Driver 1 — Innovation

Innovation creates more buyers than sellers by introducing something the market has not seen before and now wants. The new offer can serve a niche or a local market; it does not need to become a mass-market blockbuster. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #1 Innovation,” [raw:1552-1561]]

The author separates innovation into three forms: [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #1 Innovation,” [raw:1577-1591]]

- **Product innovation:** Create a new product or modify an existing product in a new way; *Star Wars* is the source’s example of a new galaxy of characters and products. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #1 Innovation,” [raw:1582-1585]]
- **Systems innovation:** Deliver an existing product through a faster or more reliable system; Facebook is used as an example of a new system for tracking friends and serving advertisers. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #1 Innovation,” [raw:1586-1588]]
- **Brand innovation:** Make something ordinary more desirable through new market packaging; Ralph Lauren’s high-fashion branding of the polo shirt is the source’s example. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #1 Innovation,” [raw:1589-1591]]

Subtle repackaging can be powerful: Judy Craymer turned ABBA music into *Mamma Mia!*, and Priestley’s team combined elements of a Silicon Valley growth accelerator with training and development to create the Key Person of Influence programme. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #1 Innovation,” [raw:1565-1576]]

## Driver 2 — Relationships

Relationship leadership means owning the buyer relationship strongly enough that customers become loyal or stop shopping around. The source illustrates this with an accountant whose clients no longer actively compare alternatives. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #2 Relationship,” [raw:1612-1620]]

The author gives three routes: [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #2 Relationship,” [raw:1643-1658]]

- **Become more influential:** Improve the ability to enrol others in your ideas and projects. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #2 Relationship,” [raw:1649-1652]]
- **Become better known:** Expand personal or business appeal through media, events, publicity, and other brand-building activity. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #2 Relationship,” [raw:1653-1655]]
- **Get deals in place:** Use lasting agreements because customers tend to stop shopping around once the deal is done. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #2 Relationship,” [raw:1656-1658]]

Contracts can remove competitors from the buyer’s attention: after Priestley signed a 24-month phone contract, the competing plans and advertisements effectively disappeared because the carrier owned the relationship. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #2 Relationship,” [raw:1621-1634]]

Influencers can also own the relationship with a market because buyers follow their advice instead of researching every option; the source points to Roger Federer, food critics, and financial advisers. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #2 Relationship,” [raw:1635-1642]]

## Driver 3 — Convenience

Convenience wins by removing market friction: the time, energy, effort, and know-how needed to buy. When purchasing becomes simpler and faster, buyers choose the easier supplier rather than investing effort in alternatives. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #3 Convenience,” [raw:1678-1689]]

The author identifies three routes: [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #3 Convenience,” [raw:1699-1713]]

- **Better distribution:** Make the offer available with less buyer energy, effort, or time. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #3 Convenience,” [raw:1704-1706]]
- **Better market information:** Reduce how much buyers must research before choosing. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #3 Convenience,” [raw:1707-1709]]
- **Automation:** Use machinery or online or offline systems to make things happen faster or automatically. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #3 Convenience,” [raw:1710-1713]]

Amazon is the source’s example: the book itself is the same whether bought in a store or online, but Amazon’s ease and simplicity made it the default option. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #3 Convenience,” [raw:1690-1692]]

Convenience advantages can be short-lived. Once information or distribution problems are solved, Internet-enabled competitors can quickly enter and account for the trend. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #3 Convenience,” [raw:1693-1698]]

## Driver 4 — Price

Price leadership means lowering production costs enough to charge less than competitors while still earning a profit. It is an efficiency position, not an instruction to sacrifice margin. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #4 Price,” [raw:1733-1735]]

The author gives three routes: [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #4 Price,” [raw:1748-1761]]

- **Invest:** Own assets that lower costs or create a barrier to entry, such as premises or equipment that would otherwise be expensive to hire. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #4 Price,” [raw:1753-1755]]
- **Refine:** Remove costs that do not increase value for the paying customer, preserving margin while lowering price. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #4 Price,” [raw:1756-1758]]
- **Systemize:** Replace avoidable overhead with systems and technology where appropriate. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #4 Price,” [raw:1759-1761]]

Small service businesses may hold a price advantage by operating from a spare room, using inexpensive software, piggybacking on market trends, and keeping overhead low. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #4 Price,” [raw:1736-1739]]

Asset ownership can create a larger barrier: the source describes Andrew Lloyd Webber using profits from successful shows to buy major London theatres, reducing the cost of running his own productions while other producers must use his property. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, “Driver #4 Price,” [raw:1745-1747]]

## Choosing the primary driver

Use the operating model—not a wish to claim every advantage—to choose the position. The source says the four positions have opposites and directs the business to focus on one main position. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 3, [raw:1521-1528]]

| If the business is built around… | Lead with… | Avoid pretending that… | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creating a product, delivery system, or brand treatment the market has not seen | **Innovation** | A new offer can immediately be the most scaled and frictionless option | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1562-1591; raw:1526-1528]] |
| Influence, familiarity, loyalty, or lasting agreements | **Relationships** | Relationship depth can be built without the media, events, publicity, deal-making, and other resources it requires | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1643-1658; raw:1526-1528]] |
| Distribution, information, or automation that removes buyer effort | **Convenience** | A friction advantage will remain protected after other sellers enter | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1693-1713]] |
| Assets, refinement, or systems that structurally reduce cost | **Price** | Being cheapest is viable when the lower price destroys profit | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1733-1761]] |

## Product applications

| Context | Application | Example | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stage entertainment — innovation** | Repackage familiar intellectual material into a new product buyers have not experienced in that form. | Judy Craymer turned ABBA music into the stage show *Mamma Mia!*. | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1565-1571]] |
| **Professional services — relationships** | Deepen familiarity until clients no longer routinely compare alternatives. | The source asks whether people have considered replacing their accountant, then argues that most have not because the accountant owns the relationship. | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1612-1620]] |
| **Retail — convenience** | Make an unchanged product significantly easier and simpler to buy. | Amazon made books easier to purchase than traditional bookstores, becoming the default despite the product itself being the same. | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1690-1692]] |
| **Small service business — price** | Use a low-overhead operating model to charge less while retaining a worthwhile income. | The source describes businesses using a spare room, inexpensive software, and low costs instead of a costly city office and brand. | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1736-1739]] |
| **Theatre ownership — price** | Invest in a scarce asset that lowers internal costs and creates a barrier for competitors. | Andrew Lloyd Webber bought major London theatres, allowing his own shows to use owned venues while other productions must pay to use the property. | [*Oversubscribed*, [raw:1745-1747]] |

## Copy patterns

- **Position choice:** “Our main market position is [innovation / relationships / convenience / price], with [compatible driver] as a secondary.” [Source pattern: “the big brands focus on dominating one of these four market imbalances and choose one of the others as a secondary,” *Oversubscribed*, [raw:1521-1523]]
- **Innovation:** “Create [something new] that [the market] hasn’t seen before and now wants.” [Source pattern: “create something new that the market hasn’t seen before and they now want,” *Oversubscribed*, [raw:1552-1554]]
- **Relationships:** “Own the relationship by [becoming more influential / becoming better known / putting lasting deals in place].” [Source pattern: “there are three ways to do this: Become more influential… Become better known… Get deals in place,” *Oversubscribed*, [raw:1643-1658]]
- **Convenience:** “Reduce the [time / energy / effort / know-how] required to buy through [better distribution / better information / automation].” [Source pattern: “Market friction refers to the time, energy, effort and know-how required to buy something” and “Convenience or less friction occurs in three ways,” *Oversubscribed*, [raw:1678-1686; raw:1699-1713]]
- **Price:** “Bring down the cost of [production or delivery] so we can offer [the product] cheaper than other suppliers and still make a profit.” [Source pattern: “bring down the cost of production so that you can offer something cheaper than everyone else but still make a profit,” *Oversubscribed*, [raw:1733-1735]]
- **Price improvement:** “[Invest in an efficiency], [refine away non-value costs], and [systemize repeatable work] to keep overheads low and margins healthy.” [Source pattern: “Invest… Refine… Systemize,” *Oversubscribed*, [raw:1748-1761]]
