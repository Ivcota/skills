# Case Studies and Worked Examples

## Events and education

### Workshop demand before ticket release
- **Application:** Create visible demand before release, state genuine capacity, then announce a fixed release time.
- **Example:** A two-day workshop priced at $795 asked for interest first. More than 175 people responded for 120 total tickets, and the event nearly sold out on the first day, four months in advance.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Buying Environments Create Buyers” [raw:1786-1801]. “Within 48 hours, over 175 people had posted on the Facebook thread that they were interested and willing to pay the price.”

### Glastonbury pre-registration
- **Application:** Use pre-registration, explicit rules, visible excess interest, and a narrow buying window.
- **Example:** Glastonbury has attendees pre-register and declare their intended ticket quantity, then discloses 350,000 registrations for 120,000 tickets before release.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “The Power of Signalling” [raw:3795-3826]. “They openly share that over 350,000 tickets are pre-registered, but that they'll only issue 120,000.”

### Monty Python staged release
- **Application:** Release capacity in stages rather than exposing it all at once.
- **Example:** Monty Python first announced one O2 show, sold it out in seconds, then released nine additional dates under the banner of outstanding demand.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Staged Release” [raw:4492-4533]. “A staged release is where you only release quantities of your capacity over several stages.”

### Trade-show queue and visible participation
- **Application:** Create a line and branded participation at a crowded trade show so transparency works for the seller rather than against it.
- **Example:** After a main-stage talk, attendees queued at the booth to complete a five-question survey in exchange for a book and samples carried in visible branded bags.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Transparency” [raw:4026-4028]. “All day there was a line-up of people filling in the forms at the booth and it attracted even more people who hadn't seen my talk.”

## Professional services and coaching

### JP De Villiers narrows his fitness market
- **Application:** Build an offer for a few clients whose constraints make the provider unusually valuable.
- **Example:** Fitness trainer JP De Villiers moved from £45 hourly work and burnout to eight wealthy, frequently travelling clients paying up to £40,000 per year, alongside oversubscribed retreats.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “You Don't Need Everyone” [raw:1325-1365]. “He doesn't need everyone to be a client; he needs eight people who want a dedicated level of service that most trainers are too busy to deliver.”

### Guy Rigby caps accounting intake
- **Application:** Make capacity and quality limits explicit even when that forces prospects to wait.
- **Example:** Guy Rigby declined new accounting clients until the next quarter because his team capped quarterly intake to protect service quality.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Campaign Planning” [raw:3067-3075]. “His team limited the number of new clients each quarter so that they never risk the quality of their service.”

### The selective coach
- **Application:** Interview prospective clients, state operating rules, and demonstrate willingness to walk away from a poor fit.
- **Example:** A referred coach returned after six weeks, interviewed Priestley, stated his rules and holidays, and won a $30,000 coaching engagement.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “It's OK to Fail” [raw:2140-2163]. “He then proceeded to interview me as a potential client and give me his list of rules for working with him.”

### A proposal that needed a smaller first step
- **Application:** Offer a low-risk first step instead of demanding a large initial commitment.
- **Example:** A consultant's six-figure first proposal stalled despite strong fit; Priestley concludes that a smaller first step could have unlocked later spending.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “It's Easier to Climb Small Stairs than Big Walls” [raw:2601-2607]. “Had she created a low-risk first step for us to climb we would have spent a fortune by now.”

### Campaign arithmetic for a consultant
- **Application:** Turn a finite annual client goal into campaign arithmetic.
- **Example:** A consultant seeking twelve $30,000 projects from franchise or multi-location owners plans three campaigns, each intended to sign four clients.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Create a Campaign Timeline” [raw:3681-3686]. “Three campaigns a year, each designed to sign on four new clients.”

## Investment and fundraising

### Matthew Michalewicz's expressions of interest
- **Application:** Collect non-binding expressions of interest before asking for funds, then disclose oversubscription while converting commitments.
- **Example:** Matthew Michalewicz gathered more than $4 million of indications for a $3 million raise, then called investors and filled the round before reaching the whole list.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “It's Easier to Climb Small Stairs than Big Walls” [raw:2579-2596]. “Because he created an easy first step, he was able to get soft commitments that he then firmed up after being sure he was oversubscribed.”

## Health and fitness

### Repackaging a health offer around wants
- **Application:** Package the same underlying delivery around what people want while retaining what they need.
- **Example:** A health business failed when it explained required habits, then took off after changing its language and packaging toward desired outcomes without changing the membership, food plan, or habits.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “People Don't Buy What They Need; They Buy What They Want” [raw:1980-1987]. “What didn't change was the actual product itself... because they were packaged in alignment to a want rather than a need, they started to sell.”

### Weight-loss implementation ladder
- **Application:** Build an offer ladder from information through done-for-you delivery.
- **Example:** For weight loss, the book maps a $20 book, $500 gym membership, $3,000 personal training, and $15,000 liposuction to increasing implementation responsibility.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Give Away Ideas, Charge for Implementation” [raw:2520-2558]. “The less energy required from the client, the more valuable the product.”

## Consumer products and retail

### Bremont's story ecosystem
- **Application:** Use a coherent story ecosystem to help a young brand compete with established incumbents.
- **Example:** Bremont connects its watches to pilots, military authorization, aircraft materials, expeditions, and the founders' family history; many watches sell out during release week.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Tell Your Stories” [raw:5081-5118]. “Before this company releases a watch they first start telling its story.”

### Nikki Beach turns buyers into celebrities
- **Application:** Make customers visible and aspirational instead of visibly pushing the product.
- **Example:** Nikki Beach carries champagne through the club with sparklers, turning the buyer into a celebrity and causing others to want the same product.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Turn Your Clients Into Celebrities” [raw:1891-1904]. “They have turned their clients into celebrities and made their product something to aspire to.”

### Preserve the winning formula
- **Application:** Preserve the winning formula while innovating around its edges.
- **Example:** BlackBerry abandoned its secure, compatible enterprise position to chase Apple's cool image; Porsche, LEGO, and *Les Misérables* are presented as preserving their core while evolving elsewhere.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Innovate but Don't Mess with a Winning Formula” [raw:2638-2670]. “If you know your winning formula, innovate at the edges and keep focused on the core.”

### A genuine one-day book promotion
- **Application:** Use genuine, brief price events rather than perpetual fake discounts.
- **Example:** A one-day Amazon reduction moved *Entrepreneur Revolution* from about number 2,000 to the top 150 books; Priestley contrasts this with deals that remain available and are not real reductions.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Price Rises and Time Limits” [raw:4588-4615]. “There's only one occasion when a price and time promotion works when it's a genuine deal for a very short period of time.”

### Star Wars and Beatles special editions
- **Application:** Create special editions through demographic focus, features, packaging, bundles, or production limits.
- **Example:** The Star Wars and Beatles catalogues repeatedly use box sets, added content, remastering, special packaging, and limited runs.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Special Editions” [raw:4551-4570]. “Box sets, added content, bundles, special merchandise and even special packaging lead to the creation of a special edition.”

## Hospitality

### Granger & Co protects delivery standards
- **Application:** Protect delivery standards even when customers must wait.
- **Example:** Granger & Co lets a line form while keeping service relaxed, food preparation careful, and seated guests unrushed.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “It's OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2319-2334]. “They seem perfectly OK to let people wait outside while they deliver their magic inside.”

### The one-pizza restaurant at Lake Como
- **Application:** Play the opposite of category norms while executing one central promise exceptionally well.
- **Example:** A Lake Como restaurant has no lake view, plastic chairs, average service, and only one menu item, but stays full because its garden-grown pizza is remarkable.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “It's OK to Buck the Trend” [raw:2357-2381]. “Above all, they get one thing right—the pizza.”

### Chanel queue versus abundant discount stock
- **Application:** Make visible selectivity part of the buying environment.
- **Example:** Galeries Lafayette requires customers to queue before entering its Chanel area, while a nearby discount seller visibly pushes abundant stock and remains empty.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Buying Environments Create Buyers” [raw:1832-1864]. “People don't buy what others want to sell; they buy what other people want to buy.”

## Technology and SaaS

### Twitter's fail whale
- **Application:** Let a real capacity failure honestly signal demand rather than hiding it.
- **Example:** Twitter's 2009 “fail whale” communicated that the service could not cope with demand, creating mystique rather than ending interest.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “It's OK to Fail” [raw:2110-2136]. “The platform was in such high demand that it almost couldn't handle everyone who wanted to use it.”

### Beta-user campaign timeline
- **Application:** Plan a beta campaign around a defined user cohort and a later expansion date.
- **Example:** A startup seeking 250 beta users schedules the beta launch in month one, observes behavior for six months, and schedules expansion in month nine.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Create a Campaign Timeline” [raw:3693-3695]. “They schedule a beta launch campaign in month one and another campaign in month nine when they plan to expand the user base.”

### Long-term technology partner
- **Application:** Build campaign and delivery capability with a close technology partner.
- **Example:** Priestley's company uses a long-term technology partner that can assemble promotional landing pages in days rather than weeks.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “From Now On, You're Also an IT Business” [raw:4988-4995]. “When we have a new idea, we can execute it in days not weeks.”

## Technology and product launches

### Apple's first iPhone launch
- **Application:** Make people wait for information, the release date, and access while maintaining product quality and terms.
- **Example:** Apple's first iPhone launch built anticipation through clues, pre-orders, and queues, then sold at premium pricing despite alternatives.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “It's OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2276-2314]. “A large part of this power came because Steve Jobs thought it was OK to make people wait.”

## Property and information products

### Give away property information, charge for implementation
- **Application:** Give commodity information away and charge for implementation that creates the outcome.
- **Example:** A property-information seller struggled with refunds for a £39 book; a contrasting company gives books, videos, and reports away, then earns from portfolio building, renovation, and rental.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Getting a Grip on Reality” [raw:3379-3425]. “It then works to build their clients a property portfolio, renovate their properties and rent them out for a top price.”

## Media and personal brand

### Famous for a few
- **Application:** Become known to a focused audience through a consistent message, content, an offer ecosystem, continuity, and collaboration.
- **Example:** Priestley defines being “famous for a few” as recognition, familiarity, liking, and trust among roughly 5,000–10,000 people through repeated exposure.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Famous for a Few” [raw:1410-1477]. “You just need to be famous for a few thousand people.”

### Reddington's employee publishing practice
- **Application:** Build company visibility by publishing the ideas of many employees under their own names.
- **Example:** Reddington asks every employee to blog monthly, pitch the company's vision, and build connections, creating a company full of visible personalities.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Build a Remarkably Trusted Personal Brand” [raw:2828-2838]. “Every employee is requested to blog each month and share their ideas under their own name.”

### Aussie Home Loans' founder promise
- **Application:** Use a founder's face and direct promise to compete against faceless incumbents.
- **Example:** Aussie Home Loans founder John Simmonds appeared directly in advertisements promising, “At Aussie, we'll save you,” differentiating the company from major banks.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Role 1: The Key Person of Influence” [raw:5784-5794]. “No one in Australia had ever seen the CEO of a bank making promises to them.”

## Brand and social impact

### Campaigns built around a larger philosophy
- **Application:** Campaign around a larger philosophy or transformation rather than the product category alone.
- **Example:** Chipotle's “cultivating a better world,” Nike's failure message, and Apple's “Think Different” communicate causes bigger than burritos, apparel, or computer specifications.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Create a Campaign Theme” [raw:3589-3629]. “Oversubscribed businesses more often talk about something bigger than what they do.”

### Charity: Water's donate-your-birthday campaign
- **Application:** Make contribution desirable, branded, and participatory.
- **Example:** Charity: Water's “donate your birthday” campaign reframed clean-water fundraising as a simple, appealing action and raised more than $20 million annually in the account cited by the book.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “People Don't Buy What They Need; They Buy What They Want” [raw:1991-1999]. “He created an ultra-cool campaign around the concept of donating your birthday.”

## B2B sales and marketing

### Seven hours or eleven touches
- **Application:** Accumulate relationship time through scalable media before the sales conversation.
- **Example:** Books, blogs, tweets, videos, podcasts, reports, questionnaires, case studies, and events are used to build the proposed seven hours or eleven touches of trust.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “The 7-Hour Rule” [raw:4159-4248]. “You should have 7 hours or 11 touches of relationship-building content at your disposal in your business at any given time.”

### Products-for-prospects
- **Application:** Create products-for-prospects that educate, entertain, and reveal buyer intent before the core offer.
- **Example:** BMW track days and test drives, or Citibank reports, talks, and software, are presented as lower-risk precursors to cars and mortgages.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Products-For-Prospects” [raw:4266-4288]. “Your business will sell more of its core offering if you produce more products-for-prospects too.”

### Release-readiness thresholds
- **Application:** Measure signal strength against capacity before release.
- **Example:** The book's worked thresholds are five times capacity for deposits or other strong signals, ten times for educated or entertained prospects, and one hundred times for soft interest such as downloads.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Oversubscribe Your Capacity” [raw:4334-4356]. “Five times capacity for strongly signalled interest... Ten times capacity for educated or entertained customers... One hundred times capacity for soft interest.”

### Campaigns paired with disciplined sales calls
- **Application:** Follow campaigns with rehearsed, documented sales conversations rather than open-ended chat.
- **Example:** Priestley's first company paired monthly buzz-generating campaigns with Glen Carlson making more than 50 daily calls, following up, scripting, and rehearsing.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Role 2: Head of Sales and Marketing” [raw:5826-5842]. “He made 50+ calls a day, he followed up with people when he said he would, developed scripts that told our story perfectly and then rehearsed them and delivered them.”

### Five reusable sales tools
- **Application:** Equip salespeople with reusable assets rather than relying on improvisation.
- **Example:** The prescribed toolkit is a script, printed brochure, simple value diagram, sign-up form, and term sheet.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “The Sales Tools” [raw:5883-5912]. “You need to develop these five sales tools for your sales person.”

## Customer experience and operations

### The Remarkable Audit
- **Application:** Audit every touchpoint, score it from 1–10, then improve one or two items monthly.
- **Example:** The audit covers websites, brochures, people, products, premises, advertisements, staff handbooks, uniforms, and packaging for customers, staff, and suppliers.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4847-4867]. “Identify one or two things a month to improve and invest in them.”

### Penn & Teller's unexpected extra
- **Application:** Create “up energy” with an unexpected extra rather than merely satisfying the stated promise.
- **Example:** Penn & Teller delivered the expected theatre show, then unexpectedly performed street magic and posed for photographs outside.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4893-4951]. “They had to leave people with more than they expected.”

### Four operational levers from customer feedback
- **Application:** Use Net Promoter Score feedback to improve product, delivery systems, target market, and expectations.
- **Example:** The head of operations is assigned four levers: improve the product, improve delivery, change the target market, or change expectations.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Role 3: Head of Operations” [raw:5913-5976]. “There are four areas to focus on here.”

## Campaign analytics

### Newspaper advertisement tracking
- **Application:** Attach codes to campaign assets, measure response, and reserve deep analysis for the post-campaign review.
- **Example:** Priestley's newspaper campaign tracked booking codes and found that odd pages beat even pages, two-color advertisements beat black-and-white ones, and an upside-down advertisement failed.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “The Truth Is in the Results” [raw:5197-5233]. “Each ad had a booking code next to the phone number and we had our team collect the codes from every person who responded.”

## Campaign operations and team

### Debrief, celebrate, and rest
- **Application:** Debrief immediately with all participants, then celebrate and schedule downtime.
- **Example:** The team circles up after an event for five questions. After three successful but exhausting events caused three resignations, Priestley instituted a party and a no-work rest period.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “The Debrief” and “Time to Party and Rest” [raw:5251-5303]. “At the end of a campaign, regardless of the result, take time to party as a team and recognize the effort that went into the campaign.”

## Team design

### Four accountable CDE roles
- **Application:** Start a campaign team with four accountable roles rather than waiting for growth before recruiting.
- **Example:** The CDE team comprises a Key Person of Influence, a sales and marketing lead, an operations and product lead, and a finance, logistics, and reporting lead.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “The CDE Team” [raw:5527-5549]. “This will require a CDE Team of four people taking on these key roles.”

### Borrowing industry influence at age 22
- **Application:** If the founder lacks influence, attach a credible industry figure to the project.
- **Example:** At age 22, Priestley paid a retired industry elder a percentage of every sale to serve as the Key Person of Influence and open doors.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Role 1: The Key Person of Influence” [raw:5716-5723]. “Simply having his name attached to our business was enough to open the doors we needed opened.”

## Small business planning

### Lifestyle scale before performance scale
- **Application:** Distinguish lifestyle from performance and prove the method at a smaller capacity first.
- **Example:** The source recommends two to four campaigns yearly to become oversubscribed at lifestyle scale before expanding capacity and campaign frequency.
- **Source:** Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, “Lifestyle First, Then Performance” [raw:5473-5495]. “Run two to four campaigns a year, focus on getting oversubscribed on your capacity and earning good money without working crazy hours.”
