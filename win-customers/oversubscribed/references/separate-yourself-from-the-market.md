# Separate Yourself from the Market

**Core concept:** Separate from both the economy and the generic industry by building a market of your own—one in which demand attaches to your business, products, personality, and philosophy. When customers see you as separate from the industry, you become subject to your own forces of demand and supply and can become oversubscribed on your own terms. (Principle 2, opening and “How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1210-1215], [raw:1290-1302])

**Why it works:** Industry oversupply matters less when buyers do not treat you as interchangeable with the industry. A relatively small group of loyal buyers can create more demand than you can handle, and the source says even a small lifestyle business may need only a few thousand loyal customers rather than mass awareness. (Principle 2, opening, “How to Create Your Own Market,” and “You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1213-1215], [raw:1303-1306], [raw:1390-1392])

The aim is not to cater to what the broad market thinks. It is to find the people who care about what you do, place a high value on the result, can afford it, and have needs you understand well enough to surprise and delight them. Over time, that market can become resistant to a competitor’s cheap offer or “cheesy ploy” because its loyalty is attached to you. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1381-1392])

## Key insights

- **Create a market of your own.** Linking the business to industry trends keeps it competing on price; separating from the industry lets income follow the demand inside the market you built. (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1285-1296])

- **Build loyalty around more than the product.** Priestley says to cultivate a tribe loyal to the business, its products, its personality, and its philosophy, then make those people part of something special. (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1297-1302])

- **You do not need everyone.** Oversubscription requires more people than you can handle, not universal appeal; the source says a small, dedicated fan base can be enough. (“How to Create Your Own Market” and “You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1303-1306], [raw:1390-1392])

- **Design for a narrow, high-value fit.** JP De Villiers moved away from accommodating every caller, identified a niche, tailored his offering, and focused on a small number of travelling wealthy men who valued his philosophy and unusually dedicated service. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1325-1362])

- **Find your market instead of obeying the market.** The right people care about the work, highly value the result, can afford it, and have unmet needs and wants the business understands deeply. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1381-1389])

- **Become famous for a few.** Fame here means that people who have never met you nevertheless feel they know, like, and trust you; they recognize you, consume your work, buy your products, and follow what you do. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1410-1426])

- **Build focused fame with five ingredients.** Use a consistent and repetitive message, publish content, offer a commercial ecosystem, preserve continuity, and collaborate selectively with others who reach a similar group. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1445-1477])

## The five ingredients for being famous for a few

- **Consistent and repetitive message.** State what you do in clear, credible terms and do not radically change the description for each audience. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1452-1461])

- **Content.** Give people articles, blogs, books, reports, videos, podcasts, or updates through which they can repeatedly encounter your ideas. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1462-1464])

- **Commercial ecosystem.** Once people know, like, and trust you, give them ways to buy at smaller, middle, and larger levels rather than relying on one transaction. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1465-1469])

- **Continuity.** Maintain a visible history that people can discover and a way for them to stay updated on what happens next. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1470-1474])

- **Collaboration.** As opportunities arise with people known by a similar group, use a framework for deciding which products, events, and projects deserve a yes or a no. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1475-1477])

## What to protect

- **Fit over volume.** JP’s model protects a small core of suitable clients by questioning callers and recommending another trainer or a later start when appropriate. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1325-1337])

- **A tailored promise.** The offer should remain built around the chosen market’s constraints and desired result rather than drifting back toward generic category service. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1353-1362])

- **Knowledge of the audience.** The source expects the business to understand the market’s unmet needs and wants well enough to bring useful insights and delight. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1381-1389])

- **Message continuity.** Repeated exposure works only when the business keeps showing up with the same clear and credible account of what it does. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1452-1461])

| Context | Application | Source example |
|---|---|---|
| **Actors and talent** | Become a draw in your own right so buyers evaluate access to you rather than the oversupplied labor category. | The source contrasts hundreds of thousands of Screen Actors Guild members with a small group of actors whose presence draws audiences and allows them to command fees unrelated to the general acting market. (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1260-1272]) |
| **Premium consumer brands** | Build demand around the named brand so category pricing has less influence over what buyers will pay. | Moët & Chandon is described as having a market unaffected by other sparkling-wine prices, while Tory Burch built a special relationship with a new type of buyer during a recession. (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1275-1281]) |
| **Investment and philosophy-led businesses** | Give buyers a philosophy they can deliberately choose and follow rather than asking them to treat the offer as generic. | Berkshire Hathaway is presented as having a dedicated following of people who buy into Warren Buffett’s philosophy rather than treating its shares like ordinary alternatives. (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1282-1284]) |
| **Professional services and coaching** | Identify the few clients for whom a dedicated service solves a distinctive constraint, tailor the offer to them, and refer or defer poor fits. | JP De Villiers built around eight key clients who travelled constantly, valued his fitness philosophy, and wanted a level of service most trainers were too busy to deliver; other callers could be referred elsewhere or told he might be available later. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1325-1362]) |
| **Events and retreats** | Give the wider audience a related offer without diluting the tightly limited core service. | JP served people outside his eight-client core through twice-yearly, 10-day fitness retreats for groups of 20, and the retreats were also oversubscribed. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1363-1365]) |
| **Media and personal brand** | Become repeatedly visible to a focused audience through interviews, videos, books, events, articles, reports, podcasts, and updates rather than pursuing mass celebrity. | Priestley describes being known to thousands rather than millions and says focused fame comes from a clear repeated message, content, offers, continuity, and collaboration. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1410-1477]) |

## Copy patterns

- **Tribe statement:** “We are building a group of people who are loyal to our [business], [products], [personality], and [philosophy]—people who want to be part of something special.” (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1297-1302])

- **Distinctive-fit statement:** “We help [specific market] achieve [valued result] despite [distinctive constraint].” This follows the source’s description of clients wanting JP to get them fit despite gruelling schedules and strong personalities. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1360-1362])

- **Market-definition statement:** “Our market is made up of people who [care deeply about the work], [place high value on the result], [can afford it], and have [unmet needs or wants] we understand.” (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1381-1389])

- **Capacity statement:** “We do not need everyone; we need more of the right people than we can handle.” (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1390-1392])

- **Focused-fame statement:** “We want to be famous for a few: people who have [read our work], [watched our material], [bought our products], and [follow what we do].” (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1416-1426])

- **Consistent-message statement:** “We [clear, credible description of what we do]. We will show up and describe it consistently rather than changing the message for every audience.” (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1452-1461])

## Practical sequence

1. **Stop anchoring to the generic industry.** Identify where following industry trends or market rates keeps the business competing on price. (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1285-1296])
2. **Name the tribe.** Define the people who could become loyal to the business, products, personality, and philosophy. (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1297-1302])
3. **Choose a narrow fit.** Focus on people who care, value the result, can pay, and have unmet needs the business understands. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1381-1389])
4. **Tailor the offering.** Shape the service around the chosen market’s specific constraints rather than trying to accommodate every possible buyer. (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1329-1362])
5. **Raise the right profile.** Publish, speak, and appear where the chosen audience can repeatedly encounter the same clear and credible message. (“You Don’t Need Everyone” and “Famous for a Few,” [raw:1353-1359], [raw:1452-1464])
6. **Build ways to engage.** Create a commercial ecosystem through which different members of the audience can buy at different levels. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1465-1469])
7. **Maintain continuity and collaborate selectively.** Let people see the history of the work, follow what happens next, and accept only collaborations that fit a clear yes-or-no framework. (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1470-1477])

## Diagnostic questions

- Are buyers choosing **you**, or are they comparing you mainly with the industry and its prevailing prices? (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1285-1296])
- Is there a defined group loyal to the business, products, personality, and philosophy? (“How to Create Your Own Market,” [raw:1297-1302])
- Can you describe the narrow group that cares deeply, values the result, can afford it, and has needs you understand? (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1381-1389])
- Does the offer solve a constraint that makes you unusually valuable to that group? (“You Don’t Need Everyone,” [raw:1334-1362])
- Would people who have never met you still feel they know, like, and trust you? (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1419-1426])
- Are you pursuing mass fame when being recognized, known, liked, and trusted by a focused few thousand would be enough? (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1410-1426])
- Are the message, content, commercial ecosystem, continuity, and collaborations working together to keep you known by the chosen audience? (“Famous for a Few,” [raw:1445-1477])

**Ethical boundary (book-wide):** Use this principle only for an offering that genuinely serves people. Priestley says the method is not for people seeking a gimmick, a fast sale, or a swift win over unsuspecting buyers; it assumes that the business cares about customers and intends to remain for the long haul. (Introduction, [raw:895-900])
