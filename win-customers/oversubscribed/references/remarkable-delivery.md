# Remarkable Delivery

## Core concept

Remarkable delivery is the most important phase of the Campaign Driven Enterprise process. Promotion creates expectation; the business must now deliver an end-to-end experience that is genuinely worthy of positive conversation. The old advice to “sell the sizzle” no longer works when the steak is poor. *(Source: Daniel Priestley, **Oversubscribed**, Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” [raw:4794-4800; raw:4814-4829].)*

The test is not whether one feature is impressive. Customers should want to share the story, recommend the business, pass on its materials, post about its service, or talk about how its people treated them; staff, suppliers, competitors, and the press should also encounter something worth remarking on. “The whole lot has to be positively remarkable” to remain oversubscribed for the long term. *(Source: Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” and “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4821-4829; raw:4847-4853].)*

## Why it works

A campaign builds energy before release, and that energy raises expectations. If delivery fails to meet them, dissatisfied customers can spread negative stories; if delivery is remarkable, clients become the marketing team and brilliance becomes the long-term lead-generation strategy. *(Source: Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” [raw:4801-4820].)*

The mechanism is emotional, not merely functional. Delight, pleasant surprise, magic, and unexpected care create “up energy”; predictable satisfaction creates “sideways energy”; disappointment, anger, and resentment create “down energy.” Priestley argues that only uplift reliably makes people talk about a business in a positive way. *(Source: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4911-4932].)*

Unexpected goodness creates that uplift. A promise that is fulfilled exactly as expected may satisfy, but a restrained promise followed by better-than-expected delivery can create a story. Priestley’s instruction is to talk about only 70% of what the business intends to do and “keep some magic up your sleeve.” *(Source: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4935-4951].)*

## Key insights

### Positively Remarkable Delivery

- **The steak must be good.** No amount of promotional expense can carry poor performance; the product and experience must deserve the energy built around them. *(Source: Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” [raw:4794-4813].)*

- **Remarkable means worthy of being talked about.** The practical test is whether people voluntarily share, recommend, link to, pass on, photograph, post about, or publicly praise what the business delivered. *(Source: Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” [raw:4814-4826].)*

- **Make the whole business remarkable.** Staff treatment, brochures, service, products, and the experience around them all contribute to whether the business stays oversubscribed. *(Source: Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” [raw:4821-4829].)*

### The Remarkable Audit

- **Audit every touchpoint.** Priestley names websites, brochures, people, products, premises, advertisements, staff handbooks, uniforms, and packaging, and extends the audit beyond customers to the team and suppliers. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4847-4853].)*

- **Score each touchpoint from 1–10.** The first audit may be disheartening, but its purpose is to expose where the organization stands and where a touchpoint can be raised toward 10/10 for being positively remarkable. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4854-4863].)*

- **Improve one or two items each month.** Identify one or two touchpoints and invest in improving them. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4864-4867].)*

- **Put investment into delivery.** Priestley’s rule is that the best product wins: keep making the product better, then surround it with remarkable touchpoints. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4864-4870].)*

- **Do not retreat from the difficulty.** The remaining work and complexity are part of the value-creation challenge; Priestley urges the reader to “lean into the discomfort” rather than treat it as evidence that the method is wrong. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4871-4875].)*

### Energy Up, Down or Sideways

- **Up energy is unexpected positive emotion.** It comes from delight, love, spontaneous joy, pleasant surprise, magic, and unexpected care. *(Source: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4918-4920].)*

- **Down energy is an unannounced negative experience.** Dissatisfaction, disappointment, anger, annoyance, outrage, and resentment arise when the negative experience was not communicated in advance. *(Source: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4921-4923].)*

- **Sideways energy is expected satisfaction.** A job done as expected creates an exchange, but usually not a story people feel compelled to tell. *(Source: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4924-4932].)*

- **Keep real goodness in reserve.** Do something great that people did not see coming; announcing every extra in advance converts the surprise into an ordinary expectation. *(Source: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4935-4948].)*

- **Restrain the promise, not the delivery.** Priestley advises talking about only 70% of what the business intends to do, dialing the promise back a little, and delivering better than expected. *(Source: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4949-4951].)*

### From Now On, You’re Also an IT Business

- **Every business must use technology to support remarkable delivery.** Priestley applies this to bakeries, hair salons, consulting services, and training companies, not only to software companies. *(Source: Phase 4, “From Now On, You’re Also an IT Business” [raw:4969-4977].)*

- **Systemize the experience.** The business needs systems to track client interactions, manage product delivery, and set follow-up reminders and notifications; without automation, remarkable delivery does not scale. *(Source: Phase 4, “From Now On, You’re Also an IT Business” [raw:4978-4980].)*

- **Build or buy the capability.** At least one team member must understand the technology, or the business should work closely with a supplier who can help it collect and use data, build web pages, and execute ideas quickly. *(Source: Phase 4, “From Now On, You’re Also an IT Business” [raw:4978-4980; raw:4988-4995].)*

- **Keep delivery systems current.** Priestley treats a stale brochure site and the absence of useful online media as warning signs that the business has stopped innovating. *(Source: Phase 4, “From Now On, You’re Also an IT Business” [raw:4996-5004].)*

## Remarkable Audit

1. **List every touchpoint.** Include the website, brochures, people, products, premises, advertisements, staff handbook, uniforms, packaging, and any other point where someone encounters the business. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4847-4853].)*

2. **Include every stakeholder group.** Audit the experience of customers, team members, and suppliers rather than looking only at the buyer journey. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4847-4853].)*

3. **Ask whether each touchpoint is positively remarkable.** Judge whether it is worthy of voluntary positive conversation, not merely whether it functions. *(Source: Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” and “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4814-4829; raw:4847-4853].)*

4. **Score each touchpoint from 1–10.** Record the present score honestly even when the result exposes a large amount of unfinished work. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4854-4863].)*

5. **Choose one or two monthly improvements.** Invest in those items, complete the work, then continue raising the remaining touchpoints toward 10/10. *(Source: Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4858-4867].)*

6. **Protect unexpected goodness.** Deliver the promised outcome, then add a genuine positive moment that was not sold in advance. *(Source: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4935-4951].)*

7. **Systemize repeatability.** Track interactions, delivery, follow-up, and useful data so remarkable delivery can scale without becoming inconsistent. *(Source: Phase 4, “From Now On, You’re Also an IT Business” [raw:4978-4980].)*

## Copy patterns

- **Touchpoint audit:** “Is this [website / brochure / person / product / premise / advertisement / handbook / uniform / package] positively remarkable?” *(Source pattern: “Is this touchpoint positively remarkable?” Phase 4, “The Remarkable Audit” [raw:4847-4853].)*

- **Conversation test:** “Will people speak positively about [the business] after they have purchased? Will they share [the story]? Will they recommend [the business] to their friends?” *(Source pattern: Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” [raw:4821-4823].)*

- **Shareability test:** “Is [the brochure] worth passing on? Is [the service] worth posting about? Would someone publish a story about how [the team] treated them?” *(Source pattern: Priestley’s brochure, service, and staff-treatment questions in Phase 4, “Positively Remarkable Delivery” [raw:4821-4823].)*

- **Unexpected-goodness brief:** “Do something great that people didn’t see coming: [unannounced positive moment].” *(Source pattern: “Do something great that people didn’t see coming.” Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4935-4937].)*

- **Restrained promise:** “When you sell, talk about only 70% of what you intend to do; keep [genuine unexpected goodness] as a surprise.” *(Source pattern: Phase 4, “Energy Up, Down or Sideways” [raw:4943-4951].)*

- **Delivery-system brief:** “Use [system] to track [client interactions], manage [product delivery], and set [follow-up reminders and notifications].” *(Source pattern: Phase 4, “From Now On, You’re Also an IT Business” [raw:4978-4980].)*

## Ethical boundary

Restrained expectations are not permission to make “preposterous” claims. Priestley explicitly assigns operations the job of policing expectations: marketing and sales must set the right expectations, customers must receive what they wanted, and then the business may add unexpected goodness. If a salesperson exaggerates to win the sale, operations should “nip it in the bud.” *(Source: **Oversubscribed**, “Role 3: Head of Operations” [raw:5934-5940; raw:5974-5976].)*
