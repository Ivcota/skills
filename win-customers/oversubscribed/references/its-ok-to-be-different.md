# It’s OK to Be Different

**Core concept:** If you do what others do, you will get what others get. An oversubscribed business separates itself through a clear philosophy, honest limits, high standards, a willingness to say no, patience about delivery, and the confidence to play a different game from the rest of its industry. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Be Different” [raw:2020-2022; raw:2052-2061; raw:2129-2139; raw:2224-2233; raw:2329-2339; raw:2379-2389].]

**Why it works:** Difference polarizes the market. People who agree with the philosophy become more loyal, while people who do not agree move away; that gives the business a market of its own instead of leaving it bland, interchangeable, and forced to compete on price. Genuine limits can also signal real demand, selective refusal preserves room for better-fit clients, waiting protects the quality that created the demand, and a well-executed opposite is memorable enough to talk about. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “The Power of Philosophy” [raw:2045-2058; raw:2082-2092], “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2124-2139], “It’s OK to Say No” [raw:2224-2257], “It’s OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2315-2339], and “It’s OK to Buck the Trend” [raw:2370-2389].]

## Key insights

1. **A philosophy is made from strongly held beliefs and opinions.** It is your take on the work and the world around it; it does not need to appeal to everyone. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “The Power of Philosophy” [raw:2059-2061].]

2. **Polarization is a consequence of being clear, not a reason to go bland.** Pete Evans attracts loyal fans and detractors because he stays true to his philosophy, then focuses his recipes, books, products, and conversations on the people who agree. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “The Power of Philosophy” [raw:2040-2051].]

3. **A clear philosophy creates a market of your own.** Put the philosophy into the world and see who shows up; without one, the business looks like every other commodity and returns to price competition. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “The Power of Philosophy” [raw:2070-2072; raw:2082-2092].]

4. **Be open about genuine failure to cope with demand.** When fully booked or constrained by staff, supplies, systems, or creativity, be polite, cheerful, and honest rather than pretending to be superhuman. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2129-2139; raw:2164-2169].]

5. **High standards require the ability to say no.** Apply fit standards not only to customers, but also to discounts, rule changes, applicants, suppliers, and investors; saying no to the wrong fit creates space for the right one. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Say No” [raw:2199-2213; raw:2224-2257].]

6. **Making people wait can protect the magic.** A business should not crowd, rush, or overbook its delivery merely to squeeze in another sale; doing the work properly takes time, and the wait signals that the result is worth waiting for. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2315-2339].]

7. **Buck the trend by executing the opposite exceptionally well.** Identify the expected industry norm, consider its opposite, and make that opposite work—from fixed pricing instead of hourly billing to bundles instead of components. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Buck the Trend” [raw:2379-2389].]

## Apply in source order

### 1. State the philosophy

**Ask:** What are your strongly held beliefs about the work? What do you stand for, what do you stand against, and what change do you want to see? [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “The Power of Philosophy” [raw:2085-2089].]

**Act:** Express those beliefs clearly and accessibly, then focus recipes, products, content, and conversations on the people who agree rather than the people who do not. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “The Power of Philosophy” [raw:2045-2051; raw:2082-2092].]

### 2. Expose the real constraint

**Ask:** Is the bottleneck the delivery system, the number of senior staff, supplies, space, or your own creativity? [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2134-2136; raw:2164-2166].]

**Act:** When the business is full, say so politely and cheerfully, name the constraint, explain that it is being fixed, and invite people to try again. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2129-2139].]

### 3. Decide what receives a no

**Ask:** Which customers, discounts, rule changes, applicants, suppliers, or investors fail the standards required for the business to work properly? [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Say No” [raw:2199-2213; raw:2224-2230].]

**Act:** Interview for fit and politely decline relationships that would demand excessive time and energy or make a remarkable result difficult to deliver. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Say No” [raw:2231-2233].]

### 4. Protect the time quality requires

**Ask:** Where would crowding, rushing, or adding another customer cause the magic to disappear? [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2322-2334].]

**Act:** Hold the standard even when a queue forms; do not rush preparation, pressure current customers to leave, or take on work that cannot be delivered properly. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2322-2339].]

### 5. Play the opposite game well

**Ask:** What are the expected norms in the industry, what would the opposite be, and how could that opposite be done really well? [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Buck the Trend” [raw:2382-2384].]

**Act:** Consider a sourced inversion such as fixed pricing instead of hourly billing, bundles instead of components, disruptive new thinking instead of heritage, or month-by-month billing instead of a long contract. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Buck the Trend” [raw:2387-2389].]

| Context | Application | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Food and media** | State a recognizable philosophy, accept that it will divide opinion, and keep creating for the people who agree. | Pete Evans ties his public comments, recipes, books, products, and online conversations to his beliefs about food rather than focusing on detractors. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “The Power of Philosophy” [raw:2040-2051].] |
| **Technology platforms** | Let a genuine capacity failure reveal demand instead of hiding it behind a claim of limitless service. | Twitter’s 2009 “fail whale” said it was unable to cope with current demand; Priestley says the failure gave the service mystique and signalled that it was oversubscribed. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2110-2128].] |
| **Professional services** | Disclose real downtime, return refreshed, interview prospective clients, and set rules that allow the work to be delivered properly. | A referred business coach took six weeks off, later interviewed Priestley, stated his working rules and holidays, and won a $30,000 coaching engagement without appearing desperate for the money. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2140-2163].] |
| **Client selection** | Screen for fit and decline work when the relationship would consume excessive energy or make a remarkable result difficult. | Priestley’s business interviews people before they become clients and politely turns away those who are not a good fit. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Say No” [raw:2224-2233].] |
| **Technology product launches** | Build anticipation, make people wait for information and release, and bring the product to market only when it is ready on the company’s terms. | The first iPhone launch used clues, a later release, pre-orders, and queues; Priestley attributes part of its power to Steve Jobs being willing to make people wait. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2276-2314].] |
| **Hospitality capacity** | Keep service relaxed and food preparation careful even when customers must wait outside. | Granger & Co stays full without overcrowding, does not rush seated guests, and lets a line form while it protects the experience inside. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2319-2339].] |
| **Hospitality positioning** | Reject category conventions, concentrate on one remarkable strength, and let that difference become the story people repeat. | The cited Lake Como restaurant gives up the lake view, polished fit-out, broad menu, and elaborate service, but stays full by opening its garden and getting one thing right: the pizza. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Buck the Trend” [raw:2357-2381].] |

## Copy patterns

The brackets below generalize structures that appear in the source; they do not add new claims or contexts.

- **Philosophy diagnostic:** “What’s your philosophy? What are your strongly held beliefs about [what you do]? What do you stand for? What do you stand against? What is the change you want to see in the world?” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “The Power of Philosophy” [raw:2085-2089].]

- **Honest capacity message:** “We’d love to work with you, but right now [honest capacity bottleneck]. There’s a problem caused by too much demand. We’re working to fix it; please try again soon.” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2129-2136].]

- **Bottleneck diagnostic:** “Where are the bottlenecks in your business? Is it [the delivery system], [the number of senior staff], or [your own creativity]?” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2164-2169].]

- **Fit refusal:** “I think you’d be happier going elsewhere.” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Say No” [raw:2247-2257].]

- **Standards-before-volume message:** “Doing [the work] right takes time. We will not sacrifice [our standard] to squeeze another [customer] through the door.” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Make People Wait” [raw:2329-2339].]

- **Opposite-of-the-norm prompt:** “If everyone in [industry] does [expected norm], we will do [well-executed opposite].” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Buck the Trend” [raw:2382-2389].]

## Ethical boundary

**Difference is not a gimmick or a fabricated inability to cope.** Capacity limits should be real: be polite, cheerful, and honest about being full, name the actual bottleneck, explain that it is being addressed, and avoid overselling the capacity the business can properly deliver. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2129-2139; raw:2164-2169].]

The coach example earns respect because his limits and willingness to walk away were genuine—“It wasn’t a gimmick”—and his terms were designed to work for both parties. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Fail” [raw:2148-2163].]

Selectivity should also protect fit and delivery: the source’s stated reason for turning prospects away is that some would require too much work or would make a remarkable result difficult to deliver. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Say No” [raw:2224-2233].]

The source explicitly accepts that saying no may cost something in the short term and says being fussy makes the business better and stronger in the long term. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 5, “It’s OK to Say No” [raw:2243-2246].]
