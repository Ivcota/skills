# Demand and Supply Set the Price

**Core concept:** The market forces of demand and supply determine the price and profit a business can make, but a business can create its own market forces. The goal is to make demand for the offer greater than the capacity to deliver it, so willing buyers outnumber the available supply. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Demand and Supply Set the Price” `[raw:969-971; raw:1123-1125]`]

**Why it works:** It only takes two committed buyers to push up the price of one scarce thing. The wider market does not set the decisive price when a small group values the offer enough to compete for limited supply; the price keeps rising until one bidder gives in. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1036-1042; raw:1043-1046; raw:1047-1049]`]

When supply expands until everyone who wants the offer can get it, prices and margins fall. When demand and supply are balanced, the business earns normal wages but not profit; when supply exceeds demand, it makes losses. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” and “Profits, Losses or Wages?” `[raw:1144-1149; raw:1171-1173; raw:1176-1178; raw:1179-1181; raw:1182-1184]`]

## Key insights

- **Two determined buyers can be enough.** At an auction, two people who desire one scarce thing can bid the price upward even when almost everyone else declines to participate. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1014-1018; raw:1019-1021; raw:1024-1031; raw:1032-1035; raw:1036-1042]`]

- **The majority is not the market that matters most.** Focusing on what the whole marketplace will pay produces an average price; the better question is which small group values the offer highly. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1047-1049]`]

- **The job is not to please everyone.** Find the people who cannot live without the offer, learn what they want, and determine where to find them. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1062-1066]`]

- **Profit is tolerated only when demand is higher than supply.** Marketing, lead generation, management, and team-building tactics do not mean much if demand does not exceed the business’s capacity to deliver. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1115-1119; raw:1120-1122; raw:1123-1125; raw:1126-1128]`]

- **Some willing people must miss out.** Trying to sell to every willing buyer removes the condition that makes the offer oversubscribed. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1133-1137; raw:1138-1143]`]

- **Keep missed-out buyers interested while serving accepted buyers remarkably well.** The balance is to preserve excess demand without losing the people who missed out entirely, while delivering remarkable value to those who got through. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1144-1149]`]

- **The same product can produce profit, wages, or losses.** Profitability changes when the relationship between demand and supply changes, even if the product itself stays the same. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Profits, Losses or Wages?” `[raw:1171-1173; raw:1176-1178; raw:1179-1181; raw:1182-1184; raw:1186-1188]`]

## The demand–supply outcomes

| Position | Demand–supply relationship | Business result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oversubscribed** | Demand outstrips supply. | Profit is tolerated on top of normal wages. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Profits, Losses or Wages?” `[raw:1176-1178]`] |
| **Balanced** | Demand and supply are relatively even. | Normal wages are tolerated, but not profit. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Profits, Losses or Wages?” `[raw:1179-1181]`] |
| **Undersubscribed** | Excess supply is available above demand. | The business makes losses. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Profits, Losses or Wages?” `[raw:1182-1184]`] |

## Application sequence

1. **Start with the demand–supply relationship.** Treat the balance of buyers and available supply as the force that ultimately sets the price. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1105-1112; raw:1115-1119]`]

2. **Anchor the comparison to delivery capacity.** The source frames the profit condition as demand exceeding the business’s capacity to deliver. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1123-1125]`]

3. **Look past the majority.** Stop centring the analysis on what the entire marketplace will pay and find the small group that values the offer. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1047-1049]`]

4. **Identify the source of highest value.** Gary Vaynerchuk’s value in the auction was not merely an hour of consultation but the possibility of a serious high-level introduction through his network. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:992-999; raw:1055-1057]`]

5. **Serve the high-value few better than alternatives can.** The source directs attention toward the people who find the offer highly valuable and calls for serving them better than anyone else. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1058-1061]`]

6. **Allow demand to remain above supply.** Do not automatically expand availability until every willing buyer can obtain the offer. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1133-1137; raw:1138-1143; raw:1144-1149]`]

7. **Protect both sides of the imbalance.** Keep people who missed out interested while giving remarkable value to the people who got through. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1144-1149]`]

8. **Recheck the outcome.** Determine whether the present relationship produces profit, normal wages, or losses, because the result changes as demand and supply change. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Profits, Losses or Wages?” `[raw:1171-1173; raw:1176-1178; raw:1179-1181; raw:1182-1184; raw:1186-1188]`]

## Decision questions

- **Who values the offer enough to compete for limited supply?** [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1036-1042; raw:1043-1046; raw:1047-1049]`]

- **Who are the people who cannot live without the offer?** [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1062-1066]`]

- **What do those people want?** [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1062-1066]`]

- **Where can those people be found?** [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1062-1066]`]

- **Will some willing buyers miss out at the present capacity?** [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1133-1137; raw:1138-1143; raw:1144-1149; raw:1150-1153]`]

## Product applications

| Context | Application | Source-backed example | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expert time and professional services** | Offer finite expert capacity and focus on the few buyers who value the expert’s highest-leverage contribution. | Gary Vaynerchuk auctioned a one-hour consultation; two remaining bidders drove the price to 4,000 each, producing 8,000 for two hours. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:989-991; raw:1014-1018; raw:1024-1031; raw:1032-1035; raw:1055-1057; raw:1058-1061]`] |
| **Company acquisition** | Recognize that one other determined buyer can create an oversubscribed market for a single asset. | Facebook’s purchase price for WhatsApp was pushed upward because Google also wanted the company, although the wider market would have assigned a lower number. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1043-1046]`] |
| **Hiring** | Create more suitable applicants for a role than available jobs, so some people who want the position must miss out. | The author states that hiring top talent requires being oversubscribed for top talent. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1129-1132]`] |
| **Publicity** | Create more demand for the story than the business can grant access to, so some outlets do not receive it. | The author applies oversubscription to impactful publicity by requiring more interested outlets than available access. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1129-1132]`] |
| **Products** | Keep the number of buyers above the quantity supply allows rather than expanding supply to every willing buyer. | The author states that products need more buyers than supply can allow for, which means some people miss out. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1129-1132; raw:1133-1137]`] |
| **Luxury goods** | Accept that unmet demand can increase how coveted the product becomes. | Ferrari does not try to satisfy everyone who wants one; people missing out is presented as part of what makes the automobile coveted. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1138-1143]`] |
| **Medical services** | Watch the ratio of buyers to providers rather than assuming the service itself guarantees high profit. | California plastic surgeons made exceptional money when buyers vastly outnumbered surgeons, then returned toward normal wages after more surgeons entered and demand and supply moved back toward balance. | [*Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Profits, Losses or Wages?” `[raw:1189-1191; raw:1192-1194]`] |

## Copy patterns

- **Find the few:** “Your job isn’t to please everyone. Your job is to find those people who can’t live without [you/your offer].” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1062-1066]`]

- **Ask the market-defining questions:** “Who are those people? What is it they want? And where do you find them?” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1062-1066]`]

- **State the scarce-capacity equation:** “If [two people] want [your time/offer] and only [one person] can get it, the price rises until one of them gives in.” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “The Story of the Two Bidders” `[raw:1062-1066]`]

- **Name the profit condition:** “Demand for [the offer] must always be greater than [our/your] ability to supply it.” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1123-1125]`]

- **Apply the principle to a goal:** “If you want [top talent/impactful publicity/product sales], you need to be oversubscribed for [applicants/outlets/buyers]. That means some [people/outlets/buyers] need to miss out.” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1129-1132]`]

- **Explain the cost of excess supply:** “If supply is too great and everyone who wants [the offer] can get it, prices will fall and so will margins.” [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Principle 1, “Some People Miss Out” `[raw:1144-1149]`]

## Ethical boundary

Use this principle only for a quality business with an offering that genuinely serves people. It is not a gimmick, a fast-sale device, or a way to pull a swift win over unsuspecting buyers; it assumes care for customers and commitment to the business for the long haul. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Introduction `[raw:895-897; raw:898-900]`]

Do not replace selection with pressure. When someone is unsure, the author’s practice is not to argue or hard-sell them, but to let them decline while others who already want the offer are waiting. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Introduction `[raw:877-881]`]

Do not present scarcity as a joke, gimmick, or ploy. In the author’s event example, the oversubscription was genuine because the business had sold beyond the venue’s capacity. [Source: *Oversubscribed*, Introduction `[raw:860-866; raw:867-870; raw:871-873; raw:874-876; raw:895-897]`]
