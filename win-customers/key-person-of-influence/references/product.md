# Product

**Core concept:** Product, the third of the five P's, is your ability to scale your value through an elegant product and services **ecosystem** — turning your skill set (your intellectual property) into an asset that doesn't sleep. Harrington and Priestley are explicit that a Key Person of Influence does not sell a single thing; they orchestrate a mix of free idea-spreaders, mid-priced offerings and high-value implementation products so that "products and services don't make money, product and service ecosystems do." (S1, Ch 5 "Product ecosystems", line 1609.)

**Why it works:** Two macro shifts make the ecosystem model the only sane play. First, the value of information has collapsed — "information is so readily available that its value has fallen considerably" — while the value of implementation has risen, because "people are time poor and drowning in options. They want trusted suppliers who can get things done for them" (S1, Ch 5 "Implementation, not ideas", line 1746). Second, if you are your only product and you're paid for your time, "there will always be a limitation on what you can earn… you can only work a third of the day and be in one place at a time" (S1, Ch 5 "Products don't sleep", line 1661). An ecosystem solves both problems: you give the ideas away to get known, then charge premium prices for the productized implementation that customers no longer have time to do themselves.

**Key insights:**
- Products don't sleep — they sell 24/7 globally, and decouple income from personal time. "If you are your only product, being paid for your time, this presents a big problem" (S1, Ch 5 "Products don't sleep", line 1661).
- Share your secrets. "Don't keep secrets. Share your best ideas with everyone… The more you share, the more their value goes through the roof" (S1, Ch 5 "Here's a secret: share your secrets", line 1726).
- Implementation, not ideas. "The real money comes from offering an implementation solution" (S1, Ch 5 "Implementation, not ideas", line 1748).
- Niche products are higher value. "People spend a lot of time and money producing lame-duck products that lack value because they are too general" (S1, Ch 5 "Niche products are higher value", line 1737).
- Build to scale globally from day one — "it's actually cheaper to think globally than locally" (S1, Ch 5 "Built to scale", line 1833).
- Productize services. "A service can be packaged up like a product. You can give your service a name, it can have a method, it can have a brochure, it can be delivered by other trained professionals" (S1, Ch 5 "Productized services", line 1638).
- Make a product for your competitors. "There's also a lot of intellectual property that you take for granted that your 'competitors' or counterparts… would be interested in" (S1, Ch 5 "Make a product for your competitors", line 1758).

## Sub-models

### Two types of products you must develop

Every KPI ecosystem contains two tiers — built in this order:

1. **Products that spread ideas and allow you to get known by more people.** These are free or near-free. Their job is to put your thinking in front of strangers and qualify warm prospects.
2. **Products that deliver high value and make significant profits.** These are the implementation products that warm prospects pay premium prices for.

"Products that spread ideas and allow you to get known by more people. Products that deliver high value and make significant profits." (S1, Ch 5 "Creating your product", line 1692.)

Skipping tier (1) and trying to sell only tier (2) is why most experts can't fill their high-end programs — nobody knows them well enough to trust the implementation product yet.

### Free / idea-spreading product types

The book names three formats for tier-one products:

- **A free audio podcast.** Subscribed audio puts you in someone's headphones weekly and trains them on your worldview.
- **An information kit.** Workbooks and training resources, delivered online or offline. One KPI couple turned their $400k/yr eBay business into an information kit "on how they set up their eBay business, sourced products and marketed them. That information kit generates over 1.2 million!" (S1, Ch 5 "An information kit", line 1712.)
- **A webinar series.** Live or recorded sessions, often featuring interviews with top industry experts. This is the book's "KPI by association" play: by interviewing established KPIs on your webinar, you borrow their authority and your name starts appearing next to theirs in search results and audience minds.

"A free audio podcast… An information kit… A webinar series." (S1, Ch 5 "Start with products that spread ideas", line 1700.)

### Three product modalities

Whatever the tier, products show up in one of three forms:

- **Digital products** — downloads, online tools, courses, apps. Near-zero marginal cost, high perceived value, deliverable globally tonight.
- **Productized services** — your service given a name, a method, a brochure, trained deliverers and a fixed scope, so it stops behaving like time-for-money work. The book's anchor example is a plastic surgeon who turned a one-off operation into a productized "smile transformation" with packaged consultation, surgery, recovery and follow-up. The IP lawyer case is similar: "We helped her to create several informational products and to write a book. As a result, hundreds of people want her firm to advise on their business. She's created a productized service" (S1, Ch 5 "Implementation, not ideas", line 1752).
- **Physical products** — a unique tangible item where the idea is communicated through the object itself. Kevin's "Great Wok of China" is the canonical example: "Each wok was hand hammered 6000 times… sold with a recipe book and utensils for forty dollars per unit and Kevin's company sold forty-five million of them" (S1, Ch 5 "Physical products", line 1647).

"Digital products… Productized services… Physical products." (S1, Ch 5 "Digital products / Productized services / Physical products", line 1632.)

### Kevin's 10-question intangible-qualities checklist

This is the master diagnostic for any product, regardless of tier or modality. Run every candidate product through all ten before launch:

1. Is the product unique in its marketplace?
2. Does it have enough of a market who want it?
3. Does it really solve a problem?
4. Is there a powerful offer with a competitive price?
5. Can you easily explain how it works?
6. Is there a magical transformation or demo?
7. Is it multifunctional?
8. Are there credible testimonials?
9. Are there independently proven results?
10. Can you answer the questions the buyer is thinking?

(S1, Ch 5 "Ten intangible qualities of a successful product", line 1779.)

A "no" on any one of these is a launch blocker, not a polish item. Kevin earned the list by selling billions of dollars of direct-response TV; if a product can't survive all ten questions on the studio floor, it doesn't go on air.

## Product ecosystem worked example

The two clearest worked examples in the book are the **speaker offer stack** and **Kevin's DRTV ecosystem**.

**Speaker offer stack** (used by Daniel and the KPI Program speakers): the speaker walks on stage, gives away the best ideas for 45 minutes — that's the free idea-spreader. Then: "If you like what you just heard and you want to know a little more, I have a special offer for my online training program" (S1, Ch 5 "Products expand your income opportunities", line 1655). The online training is tier-one paid. From training graduates, a small percentage move into the productized service (coaching, accelerator, accreditation). The talk is the top of the ecosystem; the productized service is the floor where the bulk of revenue lands. Daniel's own London launch is built on this logic: a $2,500 KPI dinner party for ~40 entrepreneurs generated, in the opening week, "over 800 people attended our events and we made over $160,000 in sales" (S1, Ch 7 "Partnership", line 2026).

**Kevin's DRTV ecosystem**: a single physical product (Ginsu Knife, Power Juicer, Great Wok, Snuggie, etc.) is the visible artefact, but each sits inside a layered ecosystem. The Ginsu Knife sold "three million units of the product in under five years" (S1, Ch 1 "Curiosity Overload", line 248) because the infomercial itself was the idea-spreader, the knife was the implementation product, and downstream catalogues, retail placements and follow-on items captured the rest of the value. Jack LaLanne's "Power Juicer… has exceeded one billion dollars in sales globally" (S1, Ch 1 "Already a unique set of skills", line 583) because LaLanne's existing book, TV show and brand were the free top-of-funnel; the juicer was the high-value implementation. The Snuggie sold "more than twenty million units" because the team "pitched the deal well to investors, then on TV and then to the retailers" (S1, Ch 3 "Snuggie example", line 952) — three different pitches feeding one ecosystem. "City-Kitty" — a training product for teaching cats to use a regular toilet — "made Kevin over one million dollars in profit in under twelve months" because the niche was so specific the networks gave it free publicity (S1, Ch 5 "Niche products are higher value", line 1738).

In every case the rule holds: products and services don't make money — ecosystems do.

## Ethical boundary

The Product chapter's hard guardrail is question 8 and 9 of Kevin's checklist made explicit: **back claims with credible testimonials and independently proven results — not actor portrayals.** Kevin's words, from someone who has run more direct-response TV ads than almost anyone alive: "An actual customer promo is ten times better than any actor portrayal. Real people offer real results, and professional endorsements from industry associations, doctors, and third-party clinical studies build product credibility that survives scrutiny" (S1, Ch 5 "Ten intangible qualities of a successful product", line 1793).

This matters because the product ecosystem amplifies whatever it carries. If the testimonials are fake, the niche is broad enough that no individual customer can verify them, and the ecosystem is scaled globally on day one — the damage scales too. Kevin's thirty-year reputation as "fair and professional" in an industry "known for speed, hustle and sometimes cut-throat behavior" (S1, Ch 6 intro, line 1877) is itself the asset that made the next deal possible.

Cross-reference: the broader ethic of Product is **give first / share your secrets** — "The more you share, the more their value goes through the roof" (S1, Ch 5 "Here's a secret: share your secrets", line 1726). The book frames hoarding information as both ineffective (information has fallen in value) and unethical (it treats customers as marks rather than as people you're trying to help implement something). The ecosystem is built on generosity at the top of the funnel and proof-backed claims at the bottom.

## Common Product mistakes

**1. Trading time for money / being your only product**
- *Why it fails:* "If you are your only product, being paid for your time, this presents a big problem… There will always be a limitation on what you can earn" because you can only work a third of the day and be in one place at a time (S1, Ch 5 "Products don't sleep", line 1661).
- *Fix:* Productize. Build a product ecosystem mixing free idea-spreaders with high-value implementation products. Even pure services businesses can productize — give the service a name, method, brochure and trained deliverers.

**2. Selling information instead of implementation**
- *Why it fails:* "Information is so readily available that its value has fallen considerably… The real money comes from offering an implementation solution" (S1, Ch 5 "Implementation, not ideas", line 1746). Up until the 2000s people paid hundreds for CDs and reports; today that information sits one search away for free.
- *Fix:* Give ideas away freely (podcasts, articles, webinars). Charge premium for the productized service that implements those ideas for a buyer who is time-poor and drowning in options.

**3. Building a generic / "one-size-fits-all" product**
- *Why it fails:* "People spend a lot of time and money producing lame-duck products that lack value because they are too general" (S1, Ch 5 "Niche products are higher value", line 1737). "How to be a good parent" doesn't move. A mass-produced product dumbs itself down to satisfy everyone and ends up exciting no one.
- *Fix:* Hone the micro-niche. Name a real, specific problem a named group of people has — "How to be an amazing single parent while working your high-pressure city job" (S1, line 1736) — and build the product against that specific pain.

**4. Skipping the free idea-spreader tier**
- *Why it fails:* Trying to sell a high-value implementation product to people who've never encountered your ideas means every sale starts cold. The book's whole "two types of products you must develop" model assumes the free tier feeds the paid tier (S1, Ch 5 "Creating your product", line 1692).
- *Fix:* Ship at least one of the three free formats — podcast, info-kit, or webinar series — before you launch a premium product. Interviewing top KPIs on your webinar (KPI by association) accelerates this.

**5. Launching without running the 10-question checklist**
- *Why it fails:* Skipping any of the ten intangible qualities — unique, big-enough market, real problem, powerful offer, easy explanation, magical demo, multifunctional, credible testimonials, independently proven, answers buyer questions — leaves a hole the market will find (S1, Ch 5 "Ten intangible qualities of a successful product", line 1779).
- *Fix:* Run every candidate product through all ten questions before launch. Treat any "no" or "not sure" as a launch blocker, not a polish item.
