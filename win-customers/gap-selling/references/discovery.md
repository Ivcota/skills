# Gap Selling Discovery

Use this reference when the user wants a call plan, discovery questions, qualification, gap diagnosis, or a problem map.

Source refs: *Gap Selling*, Ch. 2-4, Ch. 8-10; see [`sources.md`](sources.md).

## Nine Truthbombs

Keenan's worldview for the sale:

1. **No problem, no sale.** Start with a problem, not a product.
2. **Every sale has a gap.** The gap is the distance between current and future state.
3. **All sales are about change.** The buyer is buying change, not stuff.
4. **Customers resist change.** Status quo has emotional gravity.
5. **Sales are emotional.** Risk, uncertainty, control, pride, and fear shape the decision.
6. **Customers change when worth the cost.** The future state must outweigh money, time, effort, and risk.
7. **Asking why gets yes.** Why reveals intrinsic motivation.
8. **Sales happen when future state is better.** Make the desired outcome concrete.
9. **No one cares about you.** The buyer cares what you can do for their world.

## Current State

Current state is where the buyer is now: their perceived reality, business environment, problems, and emotions. Gather five elements:

| Element | What to capture | Example questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Facts** | Literal, physical business facts relevant to what you sell. | “Walk me through how this process works today.” “How many people/tools/steps are involved?” |
| **Problems** | Clear, measurable issues caused by the current environment. | “Where does that break down?” “What is not working?” |
| **Impact** | Business consequences of the problem. | “What does that cost?” “How does that affect revenue, time, risk, customers, or team performance?” |
| **Root cause** | Why the problem exists. | “Why do you think this happens?” “What in the process creates that?” |
| **Emotional state** | How the problem makes buyers feel and what change threatens. | “How is this affecting you/the team?” “What worries you about changing it?” |

Completion bar: no current-state summary is complete until each element is specific or marked unknown.

## Future State

Future state is where the buyer wants to be after the problem is solved. Capture:

- The measurable outcome.
- The business impact of that outcome.
- The emotional payoff.
- The strategic reason the outcome matters.

Core questions:

- “What are you trying to accomplish?”
- “What would solving this mean for the organization, your team, and you?”
- “If this problem disappeared, what would be different?”
- “How much more/less/faster/better would you want this to be?”

Completion bar: vague futures like “better efficiency” must be converted into quantities, consequences, and buyer language.

## Problem Identification Chart (PIC)

Build the PIC before discovery or prospecting. It prevents vague calls and keeps the seller inside problems they can solve.

| Problem | Impact to organization | What I know / root cause |
|---|---|---|
| Problem your offer can solve | Business consequences if it exists | Why it happens / evidence / likely causes |

Use the PIC to:

- Plan discovery questions.
- Identify ICPs for prospecting.
- Map demo features to buyer problems.
- Prepare likely objections and root causes.

Completion bar: a PIC row is not usable unless the problem is buyer-recognizable, the impact is business-relevant, and the root cause points toward something you can credibly address.

## Gap Discovery Process

### Question types

- **Probing questions** get specific details: facts, quantities, definitions.
- **Process questions** ask how work actually happens.
- **Provoking questions** challenge the buyer to see the current state differently.
- **Validating questions** repeat back what you heard so the buyer can confirm or correct it.

Command-statement starters reduce interrogation tone:

- “Tell me a little bit about…”
- “Help me understand…”
- “Please describe…”
- “Walk me through…”
- “Share your process for…”

### Step 1: Discover the facts

Learn the literal environment relevant to your solution. Avoid random company trivia; stay inside the part of the business your product can affect.

Output format:

```text
Facts known:
- [fact]
Facts missing:
- [question to ask]
```

### Step 2: Discover the problems

Use the facts to expose measurable problems. Keep asking until problems are concrete.

Bad: “We need to grow faster.”
Good: “We are growing 22%; we need 25%; the 3-point gap risks missing the board plan.”

Prompt:

- “Define that for me. What does [vague phrase] mean in numbers?”
- “Where does that process fail?”
- “What happens when it fails?”

### Step 3: Discover the impact

Impact creates urgency and value. Distinguish technical problems from business problems:

- **Technical problem:** system/process limitation.
- **Business problem:** revenue, cost, risk, customer, team, or strategic consequence.

Prompt:

- “Tell me how this issue is affecting you.”
- “Describe the impact it is having on the department.”
- “What are the consequences every time this occurs?”

Completion bar: every meaningful problem has at least one business impact.

### Step 4: Discover the root cause

Do not prescribe until you know why the problem exists.

Prompt:

- “Why do you think this problem is happening?”
- “How do your current processes affect this?”
- “What would happen if you could change [root-cause candidate]?”

Completion bar: the root cause explains why the problem persists and connects to a credible solution path.

### Step 5: Validate continuously

Use validation before changing topics:

- “What I hear you saying is…”
- “Am I understanding you correctly?”
- “Did I get this right?”

Completion bar: the buyer has confirmed the seller's understanding in buyer language.

### Step 6: Define future state and intrinsic motivation

After current-state diagnosis, ask what future the buyer wants and why it matters.

Prompt:

- “How would you like this to work?”
- “What would this represent in revenue/time/cost/risk?”
- “Tell me a bit about what is driving this change.”

## Intrinsic Motivation

Intrinsic motivation is the deeper reason the buyer wants the outcome. Two buyers can want the same surface outcome for different reasons; the sale should be customized to the deeper why.

Three-layer future state:

| Layer | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical future** | Tool/process improvement | Cleaner reporting, faster workflow, better integration |
| **Business future** | Operating result | More orders, lower churn, faster market response |
| **Core future** | Strategic/personal reason | Beat competitor, hit board target, protect reputation, sell company |

Key prompt: “Tell me a bit about what is driving this change.”

Completion bar: the reason behind the desired outcome is explicit enough to shape messaging, demo, objections, and next yes.

## Qualification Test

An opportunity is qualified when the answer is yes to all four:

1. Does the prospect have a problem you can fix?
2. Does the prospect agree they have the problem?
3. Does the prospect want to fix the problem?
4. Will the prospect go on a journey with you to fix it?

If any answer is no, do not pitch. Either ask more discovery, widen the gap, or qualify out.

## CRM Challenge

A good discovery produces notes so specific that another person could read them without company names and you could identify the deal.

CRM fields:

- Current-state facts.
- Problems.
- Business impact.
- Root cause.
- Emotional state.
- Future-state outcome.
- Intrinsic motivation.
- Gap value.
- Decision criteria.
- Buying process.
- Next yes.

Completion bar: no note should be generic enough to fit another account.
