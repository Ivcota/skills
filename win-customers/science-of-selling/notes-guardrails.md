mistakes:
  - mistake: "Relying on natural sales talent instead of training and continual improvement."
    why_fails: "Natural ability is insufficient in a crowded market, and a fixed mindset causes salespeople to treat failure as identity rather than feedback."
    fix: "Adopt a growth mindset and continually strengthen sales skills through training and practice."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/09-chapter-1.md", section: "Natural Ability Is No Longer Enough", quote: "If you have a fixed mindset, you should address it because it will hinder your ability to achieve the level of success you desire. The good news is that your mindset is your mindset, and you can change it. By choosing to embrace the new, empowering belief that your sales skills are like a muscle that needs to be continually strengthened, you will inspire the work ethic necessary to achieve high levels of sales performance."}

  - mistake: "Basing sales behavior on trial-and-error, expert opinion, or other conjecture instead of how buyers make decisions."
    why_fails: "Trial-and-error is slow and risky; expert advice often rests on weak evidence and can contradict decision science."
    fix: "Use scientific findings about how buyers’ brains formulate buying decisions as the standard for sales behavior."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/09-chapter-1.md", section: "Why Selling Should Be Focused on Buying", quote: "Trial and error is precarious for two reasons. First, because of the dynamic nature of selling, testing one sales strategy in an effort to validate causation is not an easy task. This is something that even scientists struggle with, and most salespeople and business leaders are unfamiliar with how to carry out this complex process productively. Second, continual testing with trial and error takes time, and revenue generation (sales) is hardly the place to gamble on experimentation."}

  - mistake: "Forcing buyers to conform to the seller’s sales model instead of aligning selling to buying."
    why_fails: "If sellers do not know how buyers construct decisions, they cannot know whether a behavior supports or violates that process."
    fix: "Make the sales process mirror the buyer’s decision-making process."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/09-chapter-1.md", section: "Why Selling Should Be Focused on Buying", quote: "The way most salespeople are taught to sell is grounded in selling, not buying. Salespeople are shown sales activities and behaviors and then taught to make their buyers conform to their model of selling. It’s the exact opposite of how it should be."}

  - mistake: "Leaning mostly on rapport, likability, or relationship selling without a strong central message."
    why_fails: "Peripheral influence can persuade briefly, but it fades and leaves buyers vulnerable to competitors or cancellations."
    fix: "Use both routes of influence: heuristics/relationship cues plus a buyer-committed central message."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/10-chapter-2.md", section: "The Shortfall of the Peripheral Route", quote: "But if it is the main method of influence you use, remember that it exerts a strong but temporary sway on your buyers. Once the memory of their interaction with you fades, so will their commitment to you, your company, and the product or service. As a result, these customers are highly susceptible to being stolen by competitors or to canceling their orders."}

  - mistake: "Starting with the product before establishing Why Change."
    why_fails: "Status quo bias makes buyers prefer no change; without a compelling problem, the product feels unnecessary or irrelevant."
    fix: "Find, understand, and make felt the buyer’s problem before advancing."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "Why #1: Why Change?", quote: "Ultimately, if you don’t give potential customers a compelling reason to change, they won’t. This is why the status quo bias is your most formidable competitor."}

  - mistake: "Trying to create urgency through pressure."
    why_fails: "Pressure triggers reactance; buyers feel their freedom is restricted and may reject both seller and message."
    fix: "Create urgency while explicitly preserving the buyer’s sense of choice."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "Why #2: Why Now?", quote: "To be successful in selling, you must be able to build urgency, but not cause buyers to feel that you are pressuring them to buy. In fact, countless sales have been lost when salespeople try to create urgency, but instead trigger reactance."}

  - mistake: "Failing to understand the buyer’s problems deeply enough, then feature dumping."
    why_fails: "Feature dumps are unfocused, boring, and give buyers extra reasons to conclude the solution is not a fit."
    fix: "Diagnose the cause and scope of the buyer’s problem, then connect the solution to that problem."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "2. Understand Problems", quote: "Feature dumps: I dislike them and so do buyers, but without an ample grasp of the problems your buyers face you will fall into this inept selling practice. Feature dumping is when salespeople blindly list the various features and benefits of their product or service in the hope that one may pique their potential customer’s curiosity. This does more harm than good, because even if one feature or benefit does kindle interest, the salesperson has also given the buyer numerous other reasons why his product or service is not a good fit."}

  - mistake: "Defining competitive advantage around what the seller thinks is important."
    why_fails: "Buyer-irrelevant advantages are not persuasive; different buyers value different things."
    fix: "Define distinct value as unique value the buyer actually desires and competitors cannot match."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "Rule #1: Distinct Value Must Matter to Buyers", quote: "A common mistake salespeople often make when forming their competitive advantage is basing it on what they think is important about their company, product, or service. However, what you think potential customers should care about, they will often consider irrelevant."}

  - mistake: "Using one-size-fits-all feature-benefit statements."
    why_fails: "They assume every buyer benefits the same way and ignore the buyer’s unique situation."
    fix: "Use Primary Buying Motivator Statements that link value to the buyer’s stated motivators."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/16-chapter-7.md", section: "How to Demonstrate Value", quote: "The foundational flaw of the feature-benefit statement is that it assumes that every buyer will benefit the same way from a certain feature. Yet each prospective customer will benefit from your product or service in different ways. And this is what makes these one-size-fits-all assertions so detrimental to the success of the sale—they treat all potential customers the same, ignoring their unique perspectives and situations."}

  - mistake: "Waiting until the end of the sale to handle objections."
    why_fails: "Late objections have often been festering, corrupting the buyer’s perspective, and sales time now works against you."
    fix: "Neutralize objections earlier by gaining commitments to the Six Whys; if an objection appears late, identify and overcome it quickly."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/16-chapter-7.md", section: "Rule #1: Identify the Objection(s) Quickly", quote: "There is no doubt that the most unproductive place to handle objections is at the end of the sale. This is because although the objection may only have been revealed at the end of the sale, it has usually been festering for some time, corrupting the buyer’s perspective."}

  - mistake: "Treating closing as one final commitment instead of a series of strategic commitments."
    why_fails: "A single end-of-sale ask forces the brain into an unnatural leap and creates pressure and anxiety."
    fix: "Ask for small commitments throughout the sale, especially commitments to the Six Whys."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/17-chapter-8.md", section: "Closing with Strategic Commitments", quote: "Ignoring these necessary commitments during the sale and only focusing on the final one at its conclusion forces the brain to take an unnatural mental leap that produces negative feelings of pressure and anxiety. Yet this is exactly what traditional closing strategies have done."}

  - mistake: "Mistaking buyer comprehension or nodding for commitment."
    why_fails: "Understanding an idea does not mean the buyer will act on it; without commitments the seller cannot see why the sale is dying."
    fix: "Use commitment trial closes to obtain active, verbal commitments."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/17-chapter-8.md", section: "Commitment Trial Closes", quote: "When salespeople fail to obtain strategic commitments throughout the sales process, they will often mistake their buyers’ comprehension of an idea with commitment to it. Now, there’s no doubt that comprehension matters in selling; if people don’t understand what you are communicating you will always struggle to influence them. However, comprehension is not enough."}

  - mistake: "Overloading buyers with too many options or too much presentation content."
    why_fails: "The brain becomes overwhelmed, confused, and less able to decide."
    fix: "Show only the information and options buyers need to confidently make a positive decision."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/18-chapter-9.md", section: "Strategy #1: Less Really Is More", quote: "Likewise, many salespeople frequently hinder the effectiveness of their sales presentations by engulfing buyers in a plethora of options. They mistakenly assume that more options will help buyers make better choices. But as we’ve seen, too much information obstructs the brain’s capacity to make a decision."}

  - mistake: "Using low anchors or letting competitors/buyers set the anchor."
    why_fails: "Anchors bias how later value and price information is perceived."
    fix: "Deliberately introduce credible, favorable anchors before price or negotiation."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/18-chapter-9.md", section: "Strategy #2: Anchoring", quote: "In your sales presentations you should be very deliberate about the anchors you introduce. I have witnessed salespeople share stories or statements that convey low anchors that actually devalue their product or service and undermine the sale."}

  - mistake: "Cluttering sales stories with memorable but distracting details."
    why_fails: "Distracting details pull attention away from the point and reduce the story’s influence."
    fix: "Keep stories lean and remove anything that distracts from the core message."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/18-chapter-9.md", section: "1. Remove All Unnecessary or Distracting Details", quote: "One of the most common mistakes that I see salespeople make when sharing stories is cluttering them with unnecessary details that distract the brain from the point of the story."}

  - mistake: "Using text-heavy slides and reading them to buyers."
    why_fails: "Text-heavy presentation is boring, hard to follow, and makes the presenter unnecessary."
    fix: "Use pictures and minimal text so buyers can picture and retain the idea."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/18-chapter-9.md", section: "Strategy #4: The Picture Superiority Effect", quote: "I can still recall one presentation I witnessed where the presenter used PowerPoint slides that contained nothing but text, a lot of text. He then proceeded to read the slides to the audience. Not only was he disengaged from the audience, but the presentation was boring and hard to follow."}

  - mistake: "Ignoring the buyer’s emotional state and nonverbal cues."
    why_fails: "Negative emotional states can skew perception, inhibit value recognition, and halt the sale."
    fix: "Monitor nonverbal behavior, identify emotional state, and shift it before advancing."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/12-chapter-4.md", section: "Identifying Buyers’ Emotional States", quote: "The most common mistake I’ve witnessed salespeople make when attempting to gain an awareness of their buyers’ emotional states is focusing only on their buyers’ words and ignoring their nonverbal signals."}

  - mistake: "Hiring salespeople by gut feel, incomplete interviews, or flawed personality tests."
    why_fails: "These practices create costly mis-hires and poor sales performance."
    fix: "Evaluate science-linked qualities such as intrinsic motivation, buyer perspective-taking, integrity, growth mindset, and communication skill."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/20-chapter-10.md", section: "Shift #3: Sales Hiring Practices Will Improve", quote: "Many of the problems surrounding this process are rooted in the way sales leaders make hiring decisions. Often they rely on gut feelings, incomplete interviewing processes, or easily manipulated and inherently flawed personality tests."}

boundaries:
  - section: "Likability bias / building rapport"
    rule: "Do not use shallow, disingenuous, or manipulative likability tactics."
    reasoning: "Hoffeld says these tactics fail, erode integrity, and make the seller appear phony."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/10-chapter-2.md", section: "Heuristic #3: Likability Bias", quote: "What you should not engage in are any shallow, disingenuous, or manipulative techniques. These tricks don’t work; they erode your integrity and will make you come off as phony."}

  - section: "Why Now / creating urgency"
    rule: "Create urgency ethically without becoming an overbearing hustler or making buyers feel pressured."
    reasoning: "Pressure triggers reactance and can cause buyers to reject the seller and message."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "Why #2: Why Now?", quote: "I’m not saying you should become an overbearing hustler. No one likes that kind of person, and that approach often backfires. Besides, there are numerous scientifically proven ways to ethically create urgency throughout the sale, many of which I’ll teach throughout the book."}

  - section: "Why Now / reactance reduction"
    rule: "Preserve the buyer’s sense of free choice when nudging action."
    reasoning: "People want control in financial decisions; choice-preserving language reduces reactance."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "Why #2: Why Now?", quote: "As the panhandler scenario suggests, when making financial decisions, people want to feel that they are in control. They don’t want to feel pressured to act one way instead of another. Yet they often need a nudge in the right direction."}

  - section: "Why Change / making problems hurt"
    rule: "Do not literally hurt buyers; make the negative impact of existing problems felt and understood."
    reasoning: "The author frames pain as awareness of detrimental outcomes, not inflicting harm."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "3. Make Problems Hurt", quote: "I’m not saying literally hurt. But rather, it’s crucial to make buyers feel the negative impact their issues are producing."}

  - section: "Emotional cognition / voice inflection"
    rule: "Do not exaggerate emotional delivery or behave abnormally when trying to project positive emotion."
    reasoning: "Positive emotion should enhance credibility, not distract from it."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/12-chapter-4.md", section: "1. Use Productive Voice Inflections", quote: "Though they are an effective way to transfer emotion, don’t misuse them. I have seen salespeople who, in an effort to express positive emotions, behave in ways that appear exaggerated and abnormal. Remember, the productive transference of positive emotions will never distract from your credibility, it will enhance it."}

  - section: "Emotional cognition / strong beliefs"
    rule: "Only express beliefs you genuinely hold; do not fake conviction."
    reasoning: "Buyers can detect feigned belief, which damages trust and emotional connection."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/12-chapter-4.md", section: "2. Verbally Convey Strong Beliefs", quote: "Conveying beliefs that inspire positive emotions must always be a genuine, outward expression of what you actually believe. You can’t fake it. People can tell when someone is feigning a belief."}

  - section: "Hawthorne effect / calling attention to emotions"
    rule: "Call out negative emotional states with concern and compassion, not accusation."
    reasoning: "Without compassion, the buyer may become combative and focus on the seller rather than the emotion."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/12-chapter-4.md", section: "Strategy #2: Leverage the Hawthorne Effect", quote: "One final point about the Hawthorne effect: it is important that it is implemented with a concerned tone. If you fail to exude compassion, the person may respond in a combative manner, which will direct her attention toward you and not her emotional state."}

  - section: "Value creation / buyer relationship"
    rule: "Define value with the buyer, not for the buyer."
    reasoning: "Value is buyer-defined; seller-only value creation ignores what matters to the buyer."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/16-chapter-7.md", section: "The Science of Value Creation", quote: "Value is always defined by buyers. This is why value creation is not something you do yourself, but rather something that is done with your buyers."}

  - section: "Influence tools / sales intent"
    rule: "Use the strategies with and for buyers, not as tactics done to buyers."
    reasoning: "The author explicitly frames science-based selling as aligned assistance, not manipulation."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/16-chapter-7.md", section: "Why Use These Strategies and Insights", quote: "As a result, these strategies should not be thought of as something you do to others, but something you do with and for them."}

  - section: "Why Spend the Money / dominant buying motives"
    rule: "Do not tell buyers their dominant buying motive; guide them to state their own reasons."
    reasoning: "Buyer-owned reasons are used to nudge the decision; seller-imposed motives are disallowed."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "How to Use Dominant Buying Motives to Earn More Sales", quote: "Remember: never tell buyers their dominant buying motive. Instead, guide them into stating their reasons (desire for gain or fear of loss) for purchasing and then use their reasons to help nudge them into a positive buying decision."}

  - section: "Why Spend the Money / fear of loss"
    rule: "If you evoke fear of loss, always show how the buyer can alleviate it through the product or service."
    reasoning: "Fear without a clear relief path frustrates buyers and can cause them to ignore the message."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/11-chapter-3.md", section: "2. Fear of Loss", quote: "As a result, when you evoke the fear of loss, you must always reveal how buyers can escape that fear through the benefits they will receive from your company, product, or service."}

  - section: "Buying requirements / process"
    rule: "Do not treat an opportunity as qualified until the decision process, decision makers, influencers, buy-in path, criteria, time frame, and finances are known or explicitly marked unknown."
    reasoning: "Buying requirements reveal how the decision will be made and how the seller should position the message and next commitment."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/15-chapter-6.md", section: "Primary Buying Motivator #3: Buying Requirements", quote: "This buying motivator unveils who is involved in the buying decision, how that decision will be made, and the specific criteria that will be used to judge your company, product, or service."}

  - section: "Buying requirements / information confirmation"
    rule: "After identifying decision criteria, repeat them in the buyer's words and ask for confirmation before using them to position value or close."
    reasoning: "Confirmation catches misunderstanding and helps the buyer take ownership of the criteria."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/15-chapter-6.md", section: "Confirm the Decision Criteria", quote: "After you’ve identified the decision criteria... you should obtain buy-in."}

  - section: "Hiring / integrity"
    rule: "Do not hire or tolerate salespeople who will do or say anything to earn the sale."
    reasoning: "Hoffeld names this as a liability and bad for business; long-term success requires trustworthiness and honesty."
    citation: {source: "The Science of Selling", file: "source-extract/20-chapter-10.md", section: "3. Top Performers Possess Integrity", quote: "Those who experience long-term success in sales are trustworthy, honest, dependable, and honorable. In today’s transparent business climate, having salespeople who will do or say anything to earn the sale is just too big a liability and is simply bad for business."}
