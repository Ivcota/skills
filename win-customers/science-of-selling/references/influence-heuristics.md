# Influence Heuristics

Use this when adding persuasion support to a sales message, pitch, demo, or buying process. The core distinction from Chapter 2 is **central route vs. peripheral route**. The notes quote: "The goal of every sales process should be to guide buyers to commit to the salesperson's message (the central route) while using heuristics (the peripheral route) to effectively convey that message."

## Central vs. peripheral rule

- **Central route:** buyer thoughtfully commits to the message. This is durable and less vulnerable to competitors.
- **Peripheral route:** buyer uses mental shortcuts. Helpful, but short-lived if it replaces the central message.

Never use heuristics as decoration. Tie each one to a specific Why.

## Heuristic map

| Heuristic | What it does | Use it when | Pattern | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Single-option aversion** | One option feels riskier than a choice set. | Buyer hesitates because the offer feels all-or-nothing. | Present 2–3 valid packages or next steps. | Do not create fake options. |
| **Asymmetric dominance** | An inferior decoy makes the dominated option easier to choose. | Buyer is stuck comparing dissimilar options. | Add a clearly inferior-but-real option near your preferred option. | Decoy must be real, honest, and not deceptive. |
| **Likability** | Rapport increases receptivity. | Trust is low or relationship is cold. | Be genuinely interested; connect on relevant commonality. | No fake rapport. Guardrails cite: avoid "shallow, disingenuous, or manipulative techniques." |
| **Social proof** | Reduces perceived risk. | Buyer worries whether this is safe or accepted. | "Teams like yours use this to [outcome]." | Proof must match the buyer's situation. |
| **Reciprocity** | Valuable help creates receptiveness. | Opening a prospecting conversation or restoring goodwill. | Give a useful audit/report/idea before asking. | Gift must be useful, not a bribe. |
| **Labeling** | People act consistently with positive standards. | Asking for a behavior aligned with buyer identity. | "You're the kind of team that validates before scaling. Would it make sense to...?" | Label must be earned and respectful. |
| **Option framing** | Makes comparison easier and reduces overwhelm. | Buyer is confused by choices. | Frame choices by outcome, risk, or fit. | Do not hide material tradeoffs. |

## Asymmetric dominance example

The primary-source notes for Chapter 2 say the asymmetric dominance effect works by introducing "a third option that is inferior to one of the others" so "the lesser alternative makes the option that it is dominated by look more appealing." Use this only when all options are legitimate and the comparison helps the buyer decide.

## Social proof pattern

Hoffeld's notes cite: "When you utilize social proof successfully, you convey to potential customers that buying your product or service is the safe thing to do."

Use:

> "A [similar company/person] had the same concern. They chose [solution] because [buyer-relevant reason], and the result was [specific outcome]."

## Reciprocity pattern

From the application notes: "We normally sell this report for [$value], but I would like to give it to you at no cost, because rather than just tell you [claim], I'd like to show you so you can see for yourself."

Use reciprocity to create value before requesting time, not to guilt the buyer.

## Labeling patterns

- **Expectation label:** "A lot of teams in your position compare [A] and [B]. What usually helps is [comparison/action]."
- **Positive label:** "You clearly care about making this decision carefully. Could we pressure-test the two risks together?"

## Heuristic audit

For each persuasive element, ask:

1. Which Why does this support?
2. Is the central message already clear?
3. Is the heuristic truthful and buyer-benefiting?
4. Would the buyer still trust us if we explained why we framed it this way?

If any answer fails, cut it.
