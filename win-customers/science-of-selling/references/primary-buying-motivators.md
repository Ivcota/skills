# Why People Buy: Primary Buying Motivators

Use this when preparing discovery, listening during calls, qualifying an opportunity, or turning buyer language into a value message. Chapter 6's core warning: do not guess why buyers would buy from the seller's point of view. Find the buyer's **primary buying motivators**: the conditions that must be fulfilled for the buyer to both **desire** and **be able to** purchase.

The extraction notes cite Chapter 6: "The key to effective listening is not to listen more, but to know what you should be listening for." Hoffeld identifies three primary buying motivators: **buyer problems**, **dominant buying motives**, and **buying requirements**.

## The three motivators

| Motivator | What it answers | What to capture | Questions | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Buyer problems** | Why would the buyer change? | Meaningful problem, cause, scope, damage, and cost of inaction. | "What prompted you to look at this?" "Where is this breaking down?" "What happens if it continues?" | Problem statement in buyer words. |
| **2. Dominant buying motives** | Why does the buyer personally care enough to prioritize this? | Desire for gain and fear of loss, stated by the buyer. | "If this worked, what would it let you do?" "If you wait, what could go wrong?" | Gain/loss language the buyer personally owns. |
| **3. Buying requirements** | How will the buyer judge, approve, and fund the purchase? | Decision process, people, buy-in path, criteria, timeline, and finances. | "How is a decision like this made?" "Who else is involved?" "What criteria will you use?" | Qualification map and close path. |

## Motivator 1: buyer problems

Buyer problems are the chief reason people buy: they believe the purchase will solve a meaningful problem. Do not stop at "pain." Build a complete problem map.

### Three-step problem discovery

1. **Identify the issue.** Use a relevant insight/statistic or a first-level question to help the buyer name the issue.
   - "Many clients come to us because of [problem A] or [problem B]. Which is more relevant here?"
   - "How is your organization addressing [known industry concern]?"
2. **Find cause and scope.** Use second-level questions to make the problem concrete.
   - "What is causing that?"
   - "How often does it happen?"
   - "Which teams/customers/processes are affected?"
3. **Surface damage and consequences.** Use second- and third-level questions to reveal why the problem matters.
   - "What happens when that continues?"
   - "If this is not corrected, what does it cost the team?"
   - "What would it mean for you if that trend continued?"

## Motivator 2: dominant buying motives

Dominant buying motives are the emotional reasons buyers purchase. They help buyers prioritize this purchase over other possible uses of time, money, and attention.

Find them with **third-level questions** after the buyer has already described the fact and explained its meaning. The two motive types are:

- **Desire for gain:** the positive outcome the buyer wants.
- **Fear of loss:** the negative consequence the buyer wants to avoid.

### Third-level motive questions

- "If this issue is resolved, how would that positively affect you and your team?"
- "If you did decide to move forward, what would be the advantage?"
- "If this issue is not corrected, how would it affect [metric/team/you]?"
- "If nothing changes, what would that mean for you and the other stakeholders?"

### Guardrail

Never tell buyers their dominant buying motive. Guide them to state their own gain/loss reasons, then reuse their words.

## Motivator 3: buying requirements

Buying requirements are the specific factors that influence the buying decision and reveal how to position the seller, message, and next step. Capture two components: **decision process** and **decision criteria**.

### Buying Requirements Gate

Treat buying requirements as an explicit gate in the sales workflow. It is **not one of the Six Whys**; it is the discovery checkpoint that tells you how to answer the later Whys.

**Where it goes:** after the buyer has at least tentatively accepted **Why Change** and **Why Now**, and before you present the solution.

**Gate question:**

> "If you were going to solve this, what would a good solution need to include for you to feel confident moving forward?"

Then clarify:

- "How would you judge the options?"
- "Who else would be involved?"
- "What would make this a yes?"
- "What would make this a no?"
- "What timeline matters?"
- "What would the money, budget, or ROI need to look like?"

**Pass condition:** the buyer confirms the decision criteria, decision process, stakeholders, timeline, and financial/approval path in their own words.

Confirmation pattern:

> "Just so I understand, for this to be a possible solution, it needs to [criteria], involve [people/process], fit [timeline], and make financial sense by [budget/ROI logic]. Is that correct?"

**Fail condition:** criteria are vague, decision makers are unknown, timeline is unclear, budget/ROI path is unspoken, or the seller is guessing what matters. If the gate fails, do not pitch yet; keep discovering.

**How it feeds the Sales Equation:**

| Buying requirement | Helps answer |
|---|---|
| Required outcomes, features, constraints | Why Your Product / Service |
| Trust, proof, implementation, support needs | Why You / Company |
| Timeline, deadline, fiscal or seasonal trigger | Why Now |
| Budget, approval path, payback, ROI | Why Spend the Money |
| Alternatives being compared | Why Your Industry Solution |

### Component 1: identify the decision process

Map the real path to approval.

| Subcomponent | What to learn | Questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal process** | Required steps, meetings, purchasing rules, legal/procurement path. | "How is a decision like this one made in your organization?" "What happens after you gather the information?" "What steps will you go through as you evaluate suppliers?" |
| **Decision makers** | Who can authorize the purchase. | "Who is involved in making this decision?" "Who else is part of this decision process?" |
| **Influencers** | Who can shape, block, or support the decision without final authority. | "Will anyone else be helping with this decision?" "Who else will be affected by this?" |
| **How buy-in is achieved** | Whether you need a meeting, internal champion, special presentation, business case, pilot, or proof package. | "Would it be useful to include [stakeholder] in the next conversation?" "What would the team need to see to feel confident?" |
| **Internal advocate** | If you cannot meet all decision makers, who represents you internally and what they need. | "If you were sharing this internally, what questions would you expect?" "What would help you make the case?" |

### Component 2: establish decision criteria

Decision criteria are the standards buyers will use to judge whether the solution is right and better than alternatives. They have three parts.

| Criteria part | What to capture | Questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Product/service parameters** | Required capabilities, outcomes, constraints, integrations, service levels, risk controls. | "When evaluating [category], how will you know if an option is right?" "What would a potential solution need to include?" |
| **Time frame** | Implementation deadline, milestone dates, urgency, dependency on fiscal/calendar events. | "When are you looking to have this in place?" "Is there a specific date driving this?" |
| **Finances** | Budget, restrictions, approval process, funding source, fiscal timing. | "Is there a predetermined budget?" "Who approves the spend?" "If funds are not set aside, how would they be obtained?" |

### Shaping criteria ethically

If buyers are unsure what they need, you may educate them with useful criteria, but you must ask for buyer agreement instead of imposing your preference.

Pattern:

> "Many clients like you have found that [criterion] matters because [reason]. Have you considered that? Is that something you would want in a potential solution?"

## Information confirmation statement

After identifying decision criteria, confirm them and gain buy-in. This makes misunderstandings visible and helps the buyer take ownership of the criteria.

Template:

> "Just so I understand, for anything to be a possible solution for you, it needs to [criteria in buyer words]. Is that correct?"

Then ask for the next commitment:

> "If we can show you how we meet those criteria, would it make sense to [next step]?"

## Discovery flow

1. **Open with context.** "What made this worth discussing now?"
2. **Find buyer problems.** Use insight-led first-level questions to identify the issue.
3. **Map cause, scope, and damage.** Use second-level questions before offering solutions.
4. **Elicit dominant buying motives.** Use third-level gain/loss questions only after the buyer has explained why the problem matters.
5. **Map buying requirements.** Capture process, decision makers, influencers, buy-in path, criteria, timeline, and finances.
6. **Pass the Buying Requirements Gate.** Use the information confirmation statement and obtain buy-in before presenting or recommending a solution.
7. **Convert to the Sales Equation.** Link buyer problems to Why Change/Why Now, dominant motives to Why Spend the Money, and confirmed requirements to Why Industry Solution, Why You/Company, Why Product/Service, and close path.

## Layered question bank

| Level | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **First-level** | Facts, thoughts, behaviors, situations, process. | "What is your current process?" "How do you decide on a vendor?" |
| **Second-level** | Ask the buyer to assess or explain their first-level response. | "Why is that important?" "How has that affected the team?" "May I ask why you chose that vendor?" |
| **Third-level** | Ask the buyer to reveal emotional motive: fear of loss or desire for gain. | "If we solved that, what would it mean for you?" "If it stays unresolved, what happens?" |

The notes cite Chapter 5: first-level questions reveal "thoughts, facts, behaviors, and situations"; second-level questions guide buyers in "assessing and explaining" first-level responses; third-level questions reveal dominant buying motives.

## Layer gates

1. **Level 1 gate:** get a concrete first-level response: a fact, thought, behavior, situation, process, vendor, criterion, timeline, or stakeholder.
2. **Level 2 gate:** use that exact response as the object of the follow-up; ask the buyer to assess or explain it before adding your interpretation.
3. **Level 3 gate:** once the buyer has explained importance, impact, or meaning, ask about desire for gain or fear of loss.

Pattern: **"You said [first-level response]. How/why [assess or explain it]?"** Then: **"If that changed / stayed unresolved, what would that mean?"**

## Buyer-language rule

Every artifact should reuse the buyer's exact words. Replace seller language like "increase efficiency" with buyer language like "stop reps wasting two hours reconciling spreadsheets before Monday pipeline calls."

## Qualification red flags

- Buying Requirements Gate not passed → do not present yet; criteria, decision process, timeline, or money path are still unclear.
- No clear problem → weak **Why Change**.
- No cause, scope, or damage → problem is too vague to create urgency.
- No time pressure or consequence → weak **Why Now**.
- Buyer cannot state gain/loss → weak **Why Spend the Money**.
- No decision owner, influencer map, or process → buying requirements missing.
- No agreed criteria → seller cannot prove fit or neutralize competitors.
- Timeline and finances are unspoken → close path is speculative.
- Criteria do not match your distinct value → risk of losing to a better-fit alternative.
