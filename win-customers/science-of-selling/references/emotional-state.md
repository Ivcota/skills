# Buyer Emotional State

Use this when a deal feels irrational, a buyer says it "doesn't feel right," the call energy drops, or a message creates pressure. The structure notes cite Chapter 4: "The brain uses emotions to assign value and mark something as good or bad." Positive emotion increases comprehension and receptiveness; negative emotion makes perception more cynical.

## Operating rule

Do not advance the sale while the buyer is in a negative emotional state. First identify it, then shift it, then return to the relevant Why.

## Emotional-state checklist

| Signal | Positive / neutral | Negative / risky |
|---|---|---|
| Voice | Curious, engaged, normal pace | Flat, sharp, rushed, skeptical |
| Posture | Leaning in, open, relaxed | Leaning back, crossed arms, turning away |
| Face | Attentive, expressive | Tense, blank, frowning, distracted |
| Participation | Asks questions, elaborates | Short answers, objections without detail |
| Decision behavior | Compares thoughtfully | Avoids, stalls, says "something feels off" |

The notes quote Chapter 4: "The key to discerning your prospective customers' emotional states is to focus on their nonverbal behaviors."

## Repair strategies

| Strategy | When to use | Script |
|---|---|---|
| **Emotional cognition** | Buyer is flat or skeptical but not hostile. | "Can I share why I believe so strongly in this? It's because [buyer-relevant outcome]." |
| **Hawthorne callout** | Buyer seems distracted, tense, or negative. | "You seem a bit concerned/distracted. Is everything okay?" |
| **Positive topics** | Early rapport or after tension. | Bring up genuine positive topics: family, hobby, recent win, shared interest, visible office cue. |
| **Change nonverbal behavior** | Buyer is physically closed off. | "Would you mind leaning in for a second so I can show you this chart? It answers the concern you raised." |
| **Pause / reset** | Conversation is pressured or confused. | "Let's slow down. I don't want this to feel rushed. What part should we unpack first?" |

## Emotional cognition

Project genuine positive belief more strongly than the buyer's negative state. The application notes provide this pattern: "The reason I believe so strongly in [offer/component] is because [buyer-relevant emotional outcome]."

Boundary: the guardrails cite Hoffeld warning not to exaggerate emotional delivery; positive emotion should enhance credibility, not distract from it.

## Hawthorne effect

Call attention to the state with compassion. The notes cite: "John, you seem distracted today. Is everything okay?" The guardrails add that it must be done with "a concerned tone" or the buyer may become combative.

Good:

- "You seem hesitant. Is there something I haven't addressed?"
- "It sounds like this may be creating more concern than clarity. What feels off?"

Bad:

- "Why are you being negative?"
- "You don't seem to get it."

## Positive topics

Use genuine positive topics to lift state before hard decisions. The application notes mention looking for hobbies, family, vacations, memberships, awards, and visual clues. Do not force small talk; use it only when it is natural and buyer-specific.

## Change nonverbal behavior

The structure notes quote: "if a person's nonverbal behavior shifts, so too will his emotional state." Use small, relevant movement prompts: look at a chart, compare two options side by side, write criteria together, or switch from abstract talk to a concrete artifact.

## Emotional-state audit question

Before asking for a commitment, ask yourself:

> Is this buyer emotionally able to say yes right now?

If not, repair state before asking. A technically correct close delivered into a negative state creates resistance.
