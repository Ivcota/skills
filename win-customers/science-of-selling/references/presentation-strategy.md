# Presentation Strategy

Use this when building sales decks, demos, webinars, proposals, or price conversations. Chapter 9 gives five science-based presentation strategies: **less is more, anchoring, mirroring, picture superiority, and stories**.

## Strategy 1: Less really is more

The application notes cite Chapter 9: "Think through what your buyers need to be able to confidently make a positive buying decision. Anything more than that—cut."

Checklist:

- Keep only content that supports a Six Why.
- Remove slides that are merely interesting.
- Replace feature lists with buyer-motivator links.
- Use fewer options; too many choices can block decision-making.

## Strategy 2: Anchoring

Anchors shape how price and value are perceived. The application notes say to anchor price by recapping ROI, avoided cost, or larger comparable projects before showing the number.

Pattern:

1. Restate the buyer's cost of inaction.
2. Restate the gain or avoided loss.
3. Then disclose price.
4. Ask a commitment question.

Example:

> "Earlier we calculated that the current process costs roughly $9,500 per week in downtime. The implementation is $18,000, so the payback is about two weeks if we remove that downtime. Does that match how you're thinking about the investment?"

Boundary: anchors must be credible. Do not use irrelevant high numbers just to make price feel smaller.

## Strategy 3: Mirroring

The EPUB Chapter 9 defines mirroring as when "one person emulates aspects of another person's verbal or nonverbal behavior." It increases rapport, trust, likability, perspective-taking, and emotional recognition when done subtly.

Use:

- Match pace and level of detail.
- Reflect important buyer terms.
- Mirror posture lightly when natural.
- Match analytical buyers with evidence; visionary buyers with outcomes and stories.

Do not caricature the buyer or copy obvious gestures. If they notice it, you overdid it.

## Strategy 4: Picture superiority effect

The guardrails cite a failure case: a presenter used slides with "nothing but text" and then read them, making the presentation boring and hard to follow.

Use visuals for:

- before/after states,
- process diagrams,
- cost-of-delay charts,
- comparison tables,
- implementation timeline,
- customer story snapshots.

Rule: the slide should make the idea easier to picture, not give you text to read.

## Strategy 5: Use stories

The EPUB Chapter 9 says people are not simply buying you or the product; "they are buying the stories they believe about them." Stories shift emotional state, make value memorable, and help buyers imagine the outcome.

Story structure from the application notes:

1. Problems the customer had before purchasing.
2. How they decided on your solution and doubts overcome.
3. Results after purchasing.

Story opener pattern:

> "You remind me of one of my customers, [Name/role]. They said the same thing you said..."

## Six story rules

Use the Chapter 9 story guidance as a practical checklist:

1. Remove distracting details.
2. Use characters similar to the buyer.
3. Make the situation easy to imagine.
4. Include the problem before purchase.
5. Include the decision/doubt moment.
6. End with concrete results tied to the buyer's motivator.

The notes cite Hoffeld warning that distracting details pull attention away from the story's point. Keep stories lean.

## Presentation audit

For each slide, section, or demo step, ask:

1. Which Why does this support?
2. Which buyer motivator does it link to?
3. Is this best shown visually, verbally, or as a story?
4. Does it reduce cognitive load?
5. What commitment should follow it?

If a slide has no answer, cut it.
