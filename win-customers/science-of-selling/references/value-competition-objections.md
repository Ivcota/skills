# Value, Competition, and Objections

Use this when turning discovery into a pitch, differentiating against alternatives, engaging a buyer before asking for time, or handling objections. Chapter 7's governing rule: **value is defined by buyers**. Create value with buyers by linking every claim, gift, label, competitor contrast, and objection answer to what the buyer already said matters.

## Chapter 7 map

| Framework | Use when | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **Social exchange / value-before-ask** | Asking for time, information, a meeting, or a next step. | A buyer-relevant reason the interaction is worth the buyer's cost. |
| **Primary Buying Motivator Statement** | Demonstrating value after discovery. | A value claim tied to buyer words. |
| **Reciprocity** | Opening doors or increasing receptiveness. | A useful insight/resource given before the ask. |
| **Labeling** | Helping buyers act consistently with useful behavior. | Expectation label or positive label. |
| **Inoculation** | Differentiating against competitors or protecting the sale. | Buyer verbalizes a counterargument against inferior alternatives. |
| **Objection process** | Buyer resists, delays, objects, or gives a vague reason. | Objection mapped to a rejected Why, answered, and recommitted. |

## Social exchange: value before ask

The notes cite Chapter 7: social exchange theory says relationships continue when perceived value exceeds perceived cost. If buyers ignore calls, cancel meetings, or stall, assume the interaction feels too costly relative to value.

Use this rule before every request:

1. **Name the buyer-relevant value.** What will the buyer gain from the interaction even if they do not buy?
2. **Tie it to a known or likely motivator.** Use their problem, decision criteria, role pressure, or industry concern.
3. **Make the cost small.** Short call, focused question, specific next step.
4. **Then ask.** Do not ask the buyer to invest time on the hope that value may appear later.

Pattern:

> "I wanted to share [insight/resource] about [buyer-relevant problem]. It should help you [useful outcome] whether or not we work together. Would [small next step] make sense?"

## Value creation workflow

1. **Repeat the buyer's primary buying motivator.** "Earlier you mentioned [buyer words]."
2. **Name the relevant capability.** "Our [feature/process/service] does [mechanism]."
3. **Link capability to motivator.** "That helps by [specific buyer outcome]."
4. **Ask for impact.** "How would that affect [metric/team/risk]?"
5. **Get commitment.** "Would that meet the requirement you described?"

## Primary Buying Motivator Statement

The application notes cite this pattern from Chapter 7:

> "[Buyer], earlier you mentioned [PBM in buyer's words]. As I [demonstrated/mentioned], our [feature/process] will [meet PBM]."

Use this instead of generic feature-benefit claims.

Bad:

> "We have automated reporting and dashboards."

Good:

> "Earlier you said Monday pipeline reviews take your managers two hours because the data is scattered. Our automated reporting pulls CRM, email, and call data into one Monday-ready dashboard, so managers can spend the meeting coaching instead of reconciling. Would that solve the reporting issue you described?"

## Reciprocity workflow

The notes cite Chapter 7: reciprocity creates a felt debt and increases buyer receptiveness. Use it by giving buyer-relevant value before asking for action.

1. **Create a useful asset or insight.** Report, checklist, benchmark, diagnosis, comparison, risk list, or data point.
2. **Make it relevant to the buyer's motivator.** The gift must help with a problem or requirement they care about.
3. **State its value without cheapening it as a generic freebie.** If credible, name the monetary, effort, or expertise value.
4. **Give before asking.** Let the buyer receive value first.
5. **Ask for a small next step.** The ask should feel proportional to the value given.

Template:

> "The reason for my call is I wanted to share [valuable asset] about [buyer problem]. We normally [sell/use this internally] for [value], but I wanted to give it to you at no cost because rather than just tell you [claim], I'd like to show you so you can see for yourself. Would it make sense to [small next step]?"

Guardrail: reciprocity must be useful to the buyer. Do not use fake gifts, inflated value, or irrelevant bait.

## Labeling workflow

Chapter 7 describes labeling as assigning a standard of behavior, then requesting behavior consistent with it. Use labels to reduce confusion or invite helpful action; do not use them as flattery detached from truth.

### Expectation label

Use when the buyer is stuck comparing options or unsure how to decide. Normalize the situation, then describe the event that will make the decision clear.

Template:

> "A lot of people in your situation have a hard time deciding between [options], but what tends to help is [comparison/action]. Once they [see/do action], they usually know which option is best for them."

Example:

> "A lot of teams get stuck comparing platforms from feature lists, but what usually helps is seeing both workflows side by side against your launch deadline. Once they see that comparison, the trade-off becomes clear."

### Positive label

Use when the buyer or influencer has already shown a useful behavior you want them to continue: candor, expertise, responsiveness, internal advocacy, or helpfulness.

Template:

> "You [specific positive behavior]. I really appreciate [how it helped]. Could I ask you for [request consistent with that behavior]?"

Example:

> "You know the internal approval process better than anyone I've spoken with, and your guidance has helped us avoid guessing. Could I ask you what finance will need to see before they approve this?"

## Distinct value test

Distinct value must pass both tests:

1. **Buyer-desired:** the buyer cares about it.
2. **Unique:** competitors or alternatives cannot credibly offer the same thing.

The notes cite Chapter 3: "It's the unique value that a buyer desires and will receive from your company, product, or service."

## Competitor map

Do not only map direct vendors. Chapter 3 notes: "a competitor is anything or anyone competing for the buyer's business."

| Competitor type | Examples | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Status quo | Do nothing, delay | "What happens if you keep the current process?" |
| Internal workaround | Build in-house, spreadsheet, admin labor | "Have you considered solving this internally?" |
| Outside category | Consulting instead of software, hiring instead of automation | "What other types of solutions are on the table?" |
| Direct vendor | Similar product/service | "Who else are you comparing?" |
| Budget competitor | Another project competing for money | "What else could this budget go toward?" |

## Inoculation workflows

The notes cite Chapter 7: inoculation neutralizes competitors by having buyers form counterarguments first.

### After stating distinct value

1. Identify distinct value the buyer cares about.
2. Confirm it matters: "Is this something you would want in a solution?"
3. State uniqueness: "One thing unique about us is [distinct value]."
4. Prompt counterargument: "If someone proposed a solution without [distinct value], how would you respond?"
5. Let the buyer verbalize why the inferior alternative is weaker.

### After the sale

Use this to protect the buyer's decision from competitor poaching.

1. Ask why they chose you: "Out of curiosity, what were the primary reasons you chose to move forward with us?"
2. Let them verbalize their reasons.
3. Name the likely competitor contact: "It is likely [competitor] will contact you in the next few days."
4. Prompt the counterargument: "What will you say if they contact you?"
5. If they waver, address the issue before it becomes churn or a lost deal.

## Objection-to-Why map

| Objection | Likely rejected Why | Response direction |
|---|---|---|
| "We're fine / not interested." | Why Change | Surface problem, cause, scope, and cost of status quo. |
| "Maybe later." | Why Now | Show cost of delay while preserving choice. |
| "We'll build it ourselves." | Why Your Industry Solution | Compare risks, time, expertise, and outcomes of DIY/internal path. |
| "I don't know your company." | Why You / Company | Add trust, proof, expertise, references. |
| "Your product lacks X." | Why Product / Service | Reconnect to decision criteria; clarify distinct value or disqualify. |
| "Too expensive." | Why Spend the Money | Ask buyer to state gain/loss; anchor price against ROI or avoided cost. |
| "I need to think about it." | Unclear commitment or emotional state | Ask what needs thought; map to a Why; repair emotion if needed. |

## Objection handling rules

Before using the five-step process, follow Chapter 7's setup rules:

1. **Identify objections quickly.** Objections become harder to resolve the longer they sit, especially after price or the final ask.
2. **Monitor emotional state.** Objections can pull buyers into negative emotion; refocus them on benefits, desired outcomes, or previously stated motivators before pressing forward.
3. **Follow the process.** Do not improvise from hope. Use the same sequence each time.

## Objection process

The application notes cite the five-step sequence from Chapter 7: "Softening statement → isolate objection → map to Six Whys → answer with evidence/story → gain commitment."

Use:

1. **Soften:** "That makes sense." / "I understand why you'd ask that."
2. **Isolate:** "Other than [objection], is there any other reason you wouldn't move forward?"
3. **Map:** decide which Why is rejected. If ambiguous, ask commitment checks for the Whys not yet confirmed.
4. **Answer root:** use evidence or a third-party story.
5. **Regain commitment:** "Does that address the concern?" then ask the relevant commitment check.

### Answer ingredients

Chapter 7 emphasizes two answer types:

| Ingredient | Use when | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | The buyer needs new information to make a new decision while saving face. | Contrast the objection with stronger proof: "I understand the cost concern. The reason clients still move forward is [evidence/ROI/risk reduction]." |
| **Third-party story** | The buyer needs proof from a similar customer, not another seller claim. | "A similar [buyer] had the same concern. They chose [action], and the result was [specific outcome]." |

After answering, ask for the commitment tied to the rejected Why, not a generic "make sense?"

## Boundary

If the objection reveals a real mismatch, do not manipulate around it. Use the Science of Selling to clarify the decision, not to force a bad-fit sale.
