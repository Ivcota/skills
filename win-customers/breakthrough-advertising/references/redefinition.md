# Redefinition (Technique #4) — deep dive

> Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Ch. 10 (book pp. 153–164).

## Core principle

Redefinition is "the process of giving a new definition to your product. It says that the product is *this* rather than *that*. Its objective is to remove a roadblock to your sale — if possible, before the prospect even knows it exists" (p. 154). Where Gradualization works "by its arrangement of facts and phrases," Redefinition works "by its rearrangement of perspective" (p. 164). Same facts, new frame. You reach for it when a product carries built-in handicaps that "actually repel the prospect" (p. 153). One mechanism handles all three handicap types through three sub-techniques: Simplification, Escalation, and Price Reduction.

## The three drawback categories (pp. 153–154)

> "Time and time again, you are going to have to sell a product that has built-in handicaps. That — along with its promises and its functions — also has certain aspects to it that actually repel the prospect" (p. 153).

> "No product, of course, is perfect. If only for the reason that he must pay money for what you have to sell, your prospect starts with a basic minimum of resistance against buying your product" (p. 153).

The three categories of drawback (pp. 153–154):

1. The product that is (or sounds) too complicated — too hard to use.
2. The product that is not important enough — appeal not statistically broad enough.
3. The product that just costs too much.

Each maps one-for-one onto Simplification, Escalation, and Price Reduction.

## Lifebuoy / B.O. — concept-judo (p. 154)

Lifebuoy carried a "horrible medicinal odor" that couldn't be removed without killing its cleaning power. Redefinition flipped the liability into an asset: the prospect's *own* body odor became the enemy, and the medicinal smell became the proof of "odor-destroying power."

> "This is the simplest, and often the most effective kind of redefinition. A simple concept-judo. A complete reversal. Turning a liability into an asset, with a single idea" (p. 154).

The pattern: the offending feature is not denied, minimized, or apologized for — it is reassigned to a new role where it is the asset itself.

## Simplification (p. 155)

### TV Repair Manual — full simplification (pp. 155–158)

The original ad failed because its headline "Do your own TV repairs" was rejected as too difficult by the average set owner. The breakthrough ad never says "repairs" until the ground has been prepared. Instead it executes a three-step redefinition of the word "repairs" itself (pp. 157–158):

1. Compare the TV set to the human body, equating minor maladjustments with the running-nose warning signs of a cold — making the technical commonplace and familiar (p. 157).
2. Continuously describe these maladjustments as "warning signals" and "symptoms" rather than "breakdowns" or "repairs," so they sound easily corrected (p. 157).
3. State outright that they can be corrected by "making a few minor adjustments, on the outside controls of your set" (p. 157).

> "Therefore, 'repairs' are redefined as 'adjustments.' Troubles on the TV screen are redefined as 'warning signals' or 'symptoms.' And 'repair calls' or 'breakdowns' are carefully segregated into the least-likely-to-occur 5% of all possible TV troubles" (pp. 157–158).

The general rule that the example proves:

> "Innovation without acceptance is valueless. The more people know that something is difficult, and the more revolutionary (and therefore different) your product is — the more resistance you will meet from them in accepting it. You must, therefore, lay a base for acceptance by redefining the entire field for them, before you bring in your product" (p. 158).

When a product is hard, the answer is "twofold: 1. Redefine (as this chapter shows you). 2. Mechanize the new simplicity (as you'll see in the next chapter)" (p. 158). Redefinition without Mechanization invites disbelief.

### Sherwin Cody — "Only 15 Minutes a Day" (pp. 159–160)

Prospects were "absolutely convinced that good English was too hard for them." The copy redefined the field with simple, astonishing facts that shrank the territory:

- 69 words make up more than half of all speech and letter writing (pp. 159–160).
- Only one dozen fundamental principles of punctuation cover the field (p. 159).
- Twenty-five typical errors constitute nine-tenths of all everyday grammar mistakes (pp. 159–160).

> "Thus the complicated becomes simple — the hard becomes easy" (p. 160).

The pattern: name the small, finite count that already covers most of the field. Effort collapses against a number the prospect can hold in his head.

## Escalation (p. 160)

> "Here you are dealing with a product which works, and which is acknowledged to be easy enough to use — but which simply does not have an appeal broad enough to assure it of a mass market. Your job here is to escalate your product. To give it more importance in your prospect's eyes" (p. 160).

> "You do this again by Redefinition. You broaden the horizon of benefits of the product. You redefine the role that the product plays in the prospect's life" (p. 160).

### Word Power Machine (pp. 160–161)

A second-generation English-course ad. The first-generation pitch — good English as an end in itself — had exhausted its market.

> "Good English must cease to be an end in itself. It must be redefined, to become instead a means to a more important end — one which is desired by far more people" (p. 160).

The dominant benefit shifts to vocabulary as a *persuasion tool to win over other people*: "the blazing new self-confidence you need to make anybody like you — to win people over irresistibly to your point of view…" (p. 161).

### Three uses of escalation (pp. 161–162)

1. **Increase benefit appeal** — shift to a more desirable benefit area. Good English → power over other people.
2. **Increase importance of the product** — "showing that something the prospect wants very much hinges directly upon the performance of your product" (p. 161). Spark plug example: "You pay $2,000 … $3,000 … $4,000 for your car. And a single 99¢ part robs you of the real power and enjoyment that car should give you" (p. 161). Cost-to-stake ratio.
3. **Show the product is needed constantly**, not occasionally. Handling-people course: "Your Entire Life is Spent Trying to Get Others to Do What You Want — Without Friction" (p. 162). Universalize the use case.

## Price Reduction (p. 162)

> "Here you have the product which, quite simply, costs too much. Your job is to make that price seem less. You do it by a very simple act of redefinition" (p. 162).

> "Why does the product cost too much? Because it's being compared with other products in the same field. And how do you whittle away, psychologically, at this price? By switching the comparison, and relating it to some other, more expensive standard" (p. 162).

### SA Fire Injectors phrase-by-phrase (pp. 162–164)

A $1.49 spark-plug ad — 1.5x the field standard, 2x the discount price. The copywriter made the price feel cheap in two paragraphs of psychological redefinition. The ten bargain-modifiers Schwartz dissects (pp. 163–164):

1. **"practically made by hand…"** — sets up the hand-made comparison standard.
2. **"would have to sell for as high as $5 each…"** — the upward comparison anchor.
3. **"30 or 40 dollars…"** — price repeated twice, first per plug then for the entire set, so the prospect "winces at the $40 figure."
4. **"get the price down so low…"** — "low" is now legitimized by the hand-made set-up that preceded it.
5. **"would pay for themselves 12 times in one year of driving…"** — "Not only value, but reward. Not only low-priced, but gas-saving."
6. **"astonishing proposition…"** — the price now sounds so low that the bare statement might cause surprise.
7. **"If you will check your car's performance … and tell your friends and neighbors about them…"** — a condition introduced; you must perform an action to *get* the now-low price; favor framing.
8. **"prepared to do…"** — favor, allowance, discount connotation.
9. **"You can have…"** — not "you can buy"; "He is letting you have the plugs at the low price. You are getting a bargain."
10. **"only $1.49 each…"** — the classic modifier; the tenth bargain-phrase in two paragraphs.

> "Most of it — perhaps all of it — is never consciously noticed by the prospect. He simply realizes that he feels that a bargain is being offered to him" (p. 163).

## Copy patterns

- Liability-into-asset flip ("concept-judo") — Lifebuoy's medicinal odor → odor-destroying power (p. 154).
- Redefine the loaded word before you use it ("repairs" → "adjustments"; "breakdowns" → "warning signals"; p. 157).
- Shrink the field with concrete numbers ("only 69 words…", "only 12 principles…", "25 typical errors…"; p. 159).
- "Only 15 Minutes a Day" headline pattern — quantify the smallness of effort (p. 159).
- Escalate by redefining the *end* (good English ceases to be the end; becomes a tool to win people over; p. 160).
- Cost-to-stake ratio — pit a small price against a large prior expenditure ("$2,000 car / 99¢ part"; p. 161).
- Universalize the use case ("Your Entire Life is Spent…"; p. 162).
- Switch the comparison standard upward (compare your $1.49 product to a $5 hand-made or $30–$40 set; p. 162).
- "You can have" rather than "you can buy" — gift framing (p. 164).
- Stack favor connotations: "we decided to get the price down," "prepared to do," "only…" (pp. 163–164).

## Ethical posture

Redefinition without Mechanization is the failure mode:

> "A new breakthrough is not merely accepted because its manufacturer says so. Its claims for ease and simplicity must be proved, in the ad, or the reader will simply shrug his shoulders and say 'it's just another copywriter gone wild'" (p. 158).

The discipline: every redefinition must be paired with a Mechanization that proves it. Calling repairs "adjustments" only works if the manual actually delivers adjustments the reader can perform on the outside controls of his set. Calling a $1.49 plug a bargain only works if the hand-made comparison is real. Schwartz treats Redefinition as perspective work, not deception work — the rearrangement must be defensible if the reader looks twice.
