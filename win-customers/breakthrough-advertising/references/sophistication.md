# Sophistication of the Market + Headline Verbalization + Creative Planning — deep dive

> Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Ch. 3 (pp. 37–50), Ch. 4 (pp. 51–58), Ch. 5 (pp. 59–68).

## Why Sophistication is the third diagnostic

Sophistication is the third of the three diagnostic questions a copywriter must answer before writing a headline — after Mass Desire (Ch. 1) and State of Awareness (Ch. 2). The question itself is short: **"How many similar products have they been told about before?"** (p. 37).

Schwartz frames it as the easiest of the three to research: "A few hours research should give you samples of every competing ad in the field — if there are any" (p. 37). It matters because the same claim that breaks through a virgin market becomes invisible to a market that has already heard it ten times. Markets progress through five stages roughly in order; the copywriter's job is to diagnose the current stage and pick the matching headline strategy (pp. 37–50).

Critically, Sophistication is a state of the *market*, not the product. The product hasn't changed; what's changed is how much the market has heard.

---

## The Five Stages

### Stage 1 — First in the market (p. 37)

**Schwartz's heading.** "If You Are First in Your Market" (p. 37).

**Definition.** "You are dealing with prospects that have no sophistication about your product at all… your story is brand-new to them" (pp. 37–38). "You are dealing with a market where you are first. Therefore they have no previous information about similar products. Therefore they are completely unsophisticated" (p. 38).

**How "first" happens** (p. 38):
- **Technological breakthrough** — women's hair sprays.
- **A radically better product** — long-playing records.
- **A familiar product at an explosively low price** — Model T Ford.
- **Applying an existing product to a new market** — Ovaltine repositioned in the Twenties from insomnia aid to body-builder for skinny children.
- **A new medium** — Revlon's sponsorship of *The $64,000 Question*.
- **Discovering a previously unnoticed performance** — Lifebuoy's "B.O."

**Strategy.** "Be simple. Be direct. Above all, don't be fancy. Name either the need or the claim in your headline — nothing more. Dramatize that claim in your copy — make it as powerful as possible. And then bring in your product; and prove that it works." (p. 39)

**Example.** The reducing field's original headline: **"NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!"** (p. 39).

**Copy pattern.** State the raw need or raw claim. No mechanism, no embellishment. "Nothing more — because nothing more is needed" (p. 39).

**Transition into Stage 2.** As soon as your claim works, a competitor enters and copies it. Pure repetition won't move them, so they enlarge it.

---

### Stage 2 — Second in market, claim still working (p. 39)

**Schwartz's heading.** "If You're Second, Do This" (p. 39).

**Definition.** The direct claim has already been made; mere repetition won't work. "If you're second, and the direct claim is still working — then copy that successful claim — but enlarge on it. Drive it to the absolute limit. Outbid your competition." (p. 39)

**Strategy.** Take the proven claim and push it to the outer limits of legality and believability — bigger numbers, bolder promises, broader comparisons. Schwartz calls this the **"enlarged-claim technique"** (p. 40).

**Examples** (pp. 39–40):
- Reducing: **"LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS — OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!"**
- Reducing: **"I AM 61 POUNDS LIGHTER … NEVER A HUNGRY MINUTE."**
- Garden: **"PICK 25–50–100 ROSES FROM THIS ONE MAGNIFICENT PLANT!"** then **"SIX HUNDRED MUMS FROM A SINGLE BUSH!"** then **"WHO EVER HEARD OF 17,000 BLOOMS FROM A SINGLE PLANT?"**
- Cars: the Chrysler 300, "pegged just at the limit of believability" (p. 40).

**Copy pattern.** Same claim as Stage 1, escalated by quantity, speed, scope, or comparison.

**Transition / believability collapse.** "The successful claim is overworked; enlargement piles on enlargement. New competitors enter the field — each trying to promise more. Headlines double and triple in size. Words begin to lose their meaning — 'whiter-than-whites' appear. The prospect becomes confused — then skeptical. Believability is shattered; claims are automatically discounted 50% by their readers" (p. 40). Sales curve "begins to turn down-down-down" (p. 41). At this point Stage 2 is exhausted; the only way forward is a new mechanism.

---

### Stage 3 — Introduce a new mechanism (p. 41)

**Schwartz's heading.** "The Third Stage of Sophistication" (p. 41).

**Definition.** "Your prospects have now heard all the claims — all the extremes… How are they to distinguish one product from the mass? How do you break through to reach them?" (p. 41).

**The restorative power of the market.** Critically, the desire still exists. Reducing, spark plug replacement, cars, homes, and appliances are markets that "continually renew" themselves (p. 41). "But the desire never fades. The dissatisfaction builds up… these women are hoping to find a new product — a new headline — that promises them a new way to satisfy that age-old desire" (p. 41).

**Strategy.** "What this market needs now is a new device to make all these old claims become fresh and believable to them again. In other words, A NEW MECHANISM — a new way to making the old promise work. A different process — a fresh chance — a brand-new possibility of success where only disappointment has resulted before" (p. 42). "Here the emphasis shifts from what the product does to HOW it works. Not accomplishment, but performance becomes dominant. The claim remains — but now it is reinforced by the mechanism that accomplishes it" (p. 42).

**Examples** (pp. 42–44):
- **"FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!"**
- **"FIRST WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING!"**
- Body copy from a Stage-3 reducing ad: "Released for the first time! The amazing scientific discovery that melts up to 37 POUNDS off men and women — without starvation diets, without a single hungry moment — without even giving up the foods you love!" (p. 43)

**Copy pattern.** Headline = mechanism. Body = restated claim, elaborated. "First the mechanism is brought into the headline to establish a point of difference — to make the old claims fresh and believable again. And then — once the prospect is told that here is a brand-new chance for success — then the claim can be restated in full" (p. 43).

**Transition into Stage 4.** "Within a few months, the Third Stage of Sophistication passes into a Fourth Stage — a new stage of elaboration and enlargement. But this time, the elaboration is concentrated on the mechanism, rather than on the promise" (p. 44).

---

### Stage 4 — Elaborate the mechanism (p. 44)

**Schwartz's heading.** "The Fourth Stage" (p. 44).

**Definition.** A new stage of elaboration and enlargement, but concentrated on the mechanism rather than on the promise (p. 44).

**Strategy.** "If a competitor has just introduced a new mechanism to achieve the same claim as that performed by your product, and that new-mechanism announcement is producing sales, then you counter in this way. Simply elaborate or enlarge upon the successful mechanism. Make it easier, quicker, surer; allow it to solve more of the problem; overcome old limitations; promise extra benefits." (p. 44) Schwartz notes this is structurally "a stage of embellishment similar to the Second Stage of Sophistication described above. The same strategy will be effective here" (p. 44).

**Example.** **"FIRST NO-DIET REDUCING WONDER DRUG!"** (p. 44).

**Copy pattern.** Take the now-accepted mechanism and remove a limitation, add a benefit, or speed it up.

**Transition / collapse.** "But, unfortunately, so will the same limitations. The Fourth Stage of Sophistication, like the Second Stage which it resembles, eventually pushes itself out of the realm of believability. At this point, further elaborations become ineffective" (pp. 44–45). Two alternatives then: find yet another acceptable new mechanism (cycling back through Stage 3), OR move to Stage 5.

---

### Stage 5 — Identification / How to Revive a Dead Product (p. 45)

**Schwartz's heading.** "How to Revive a Dead Product" (p. 45).

**Definition.** "Eventually, of course, no new mechanism will gain acceptance. The market will have grown tired of your promises and the mechanisms by which they are accomplished. Your prospects will have been glutted by advertising. You will have reached the Fifth Stage of Sophistication — the most difficult — where the field is said to be exhausted — where competitors are dropping out of the market en masse" (p. 45). "In this Final Stage of Sophistication, your market no longer believes in your advertising, and therefore no longer wishes to be aware of your product" (p. 45).

**Strategy.** "The emphasis shifts from the promise and the mechanism which accomplishes it, to identification with the prospect himself. You are dealing here with the problem of bringing your prospect into your ad — not through desire — but through identification. (See Chapter 8.)" (p. 45).

**Cross-reference to Awareness Stage 5.** Schwartz is explicit: "The problems are the same. The strategy is the same" (p. 45). Sophistication-Stage-5 and Awareness-Stage-5 collapse onto the same headline tactic — the Identification headline.

**Examples.**
- **Postum** — the headline **"WHY MEN CRACK …"** is "an outstanding example of a product which had lost its market because of such a Fifth Stage of Sophistication, and then gained it back by a brilliant use of the identification headline" (p. 45).
- **Marlboro** — for cigarettes in the early Fifties, the Marlboro "Virile Men" ads worked the same way, projecting visual identification "without mechanisms or claims, without even headlines" (p. 47).

**Copy pattern.** Lead with who the prospect is (or aspires to be), not what the product does. Identification > desire.

---

## Worked example: the Cigarette Industry through all 5 Stages (pp. 46–49)

Schwartz calls cigarettes "the classic example of an industry which encountered all five Stages of Sophistication — and overcame them" (p. 46).

**Stage 1 — taste, enjoyment, pleasure** (p. 46):
- **"I'D WALK A MILE FOR A CAMEL!"**
- **"CHESTERFIELD — THEY SATISFY!"**

**Stage 2 — broader comparisons** (because pleasure can't be measured directly) (p. 46):
- **"LIGHT UP A LUCKY, AND YOU WON'T MISS THE SWEETS THAT MAKE YOU FAT!"**

**Stage 3 — new mechanisms** (pp. 46–47):
- **"LUCKIES — THEY'RE TOASTED!"**
- **"PALL MALL'S GREATER LENGTH FILTERS THE SMOKE FURTHER!"**
- **"CAMELS — PROTECT YOUR T-ZONE!"**

**Stage 4 — elaboration on accepted mechanisms** (p. 47):
- **"PHILIP MORRIS — ALL THE HARSHNESS BAKED OUT!"**
- **"CHESTERFIELD — REGULAR AND KING-SIZED TOO!"**
- **"NINE OUT OF TEN DOCTORS PREFER LUCKIES!"**

**Stage 5 — identification** (p. 47): government banned the health claims, mechanisms exhausted; Motivation Research showed how to reach the market via "strong visual identifications with the virility that the public had accepted in a cigarette," e.g. Marlboro Virile Men, with imitations from Chesterfield and Camel.

### Sub-problem 1 — Doubling the market post-WWI (p. 48)

Recruiting women smokers was a Fifth-Stage identification problem solved by the beach/moonlight ad with the line **"BLOW SOME MY WAY."** Schwartz: "Nothing more needed to be said. A vast new market — opened up with four words" (p. 48).

### Sub-problem 2 — Filter cigarettes re-trace all 5 stages (p. 49)

The late-Fifties cancer scare "opened up a vast new market" for filter cigarettes (p. 48). Filter cigarettes then re-traced the entire five-stage cycle within a few decades:

- **Stage 1:** **"KENT'S MICRONITE FILTER TRAPS TARS BEFORE THEY REACH YOUR LIPS!"**
- **Stage 2:** **"20,000 FILTER TRAPS IN VICEROY!"**
- **Stage 3:** **"PARLIAMENT — THE MOST IMPORTANT ¼ INCH IN SMOKING TODAY — NO FILTER FEEDBACK!"**
- **Stage 4:** **"TAREYTON — DUAL FILTER FOR DOUBLE THE PLEASURE!"**
- **Stage 5** — "in an industry-wide stroke of genius — right back to the flavor again": **"WINSTON TASTES GOOD LIKE A CIGARETTE SHOULD!"**, **"IT'S WHAT'S UP FRONT THAT COUNTS!"**, **"L & M HAS FOUND THE SECRET THAT UNLOCKS THE FLAVOR!"**

Closing line: "And so it goes. In industry after industry. The same life cycle for each market. The same deadly challenges. The same willingness to adapt rather than perish." (p. 50)

## Schwartz's Personal Note (p. 50)

Schwartz closes the chapter with an explicit ethics caveat — the only one of its kind in the book:

> "In this book I have tried to write a scientific study of advertising, without troubling the reader with whatever personal ethics I myself may observe. Every copywriter who has ever sweated for days to create a new approach will know how it feels to see that approach copied overnight by a competitor. I share every ounce of that feeling. But such events happen every day. And they are effective. Therefore, examples such as those detailed above must be listed, in all objectivity, as a business strategy that has and will solve competitive problems in a competitive industry. I include them here — not as recommendations, but as possible strategies to be chosen or rejected." (p. 50)

## The 38 Ways to Strengthen Your Headline (Ch. 4)

### What Verbalization is (pp. 51–52)

The technique is called **VERBALIZATION** — "the art of increasing the impact of a headline by the way in which it is stated" (p. 51). Once you know *what* you want to say, you must decide *how* to say it. "If you are the first in your field, there is no better way" than to state the claim in its barest form ("Lose Weight," "Stop Corns") — but where you are competitive, or the thought is too complicated to be stated simply, "you must reinforce that claim by binding other images to it with the words in which you express it" (pp. 51–52).

Verbalization accomplishes three purposes (p. 52):
1. **Strengthen the claim** — by enlarging upon it, measuring it, making it more vivid.
2. **Make the claim new and fresh** — by twisting, changing, presenting it from a different angle, turning it into a narration, challenging the reader with an example.
3. **Pull the prospect into the body of the ad** — by promising information, questioning, partially revealing mechanism.

These are added "variations, enlargements, or embellishments to the main headline claim" — bound in by sentence structure (p. 52). Schwartz frames them as **"guideposts for your own thinking,"** not formulas, and notes "every good copywriter invents a few himself" (p. 52).

### The 38 Ways

1. **Measure the size of the claim** (p. 52) — "20,000 FILTER TRAPS IN VICEROY!"; "I AM 61 POUNDS LIGHTER…"; "WHO EVER HEARD OF 17,000 BLOOMS FROM A SINGLE PLANT?"
2. **Measure the speed of the claim** (pp. 52–53) — "FEEL BETTER FAST!"; "IN 2 SECONDS, BAYER ASPIRIN BEGINS TO DISSOLVE IN YOUR GLASS!"
3. **Compare the claim** (p. 53) — "SIX TIMES WHITER WASHES!"; "COSTS UP TO $300 LESS THAN MANY MODELS OF THE LOW-PRICED THREE!"
4. **Metaphorize the claim** (p. 53) — "BANISHES CORNS!"; "MELTS AWAY UGLY FAT!"
5. **Sensitize the claim** — make the prospect feel, smell, touch, see or hear it (p. 53) — "TASTES LIKE YOU JUST PICKED IT!"; "THE SKIN YOU LOVE TO TOUCH!"
6. **Demonstrate the claim by showing a prime example** (p. 53) — "JAKE LAMOTTA, 160 POUND FIGHTER, FAILS TO FLATTEN MONO PAPER CUP!"; "AT 60 MILES AN HOUR, THE LOUDEST NOISE IN THIS ROLLS ROYCE IS THE ELECTRIC CLOCK!"
7. **Dramatize the claim, or its result** (p. 53) — "HERE'S AN EXTRA $50, GRACE — I'M MAKING BIG MONEY NOW!"; "THEY LAUGHED WHEN I SAT DOWN AT THE PIANO — BUT WHEN I STARTED TO PLAY…"
8. **State the claim as a paradox** (p. 53) — "HOW A BALD-HEADED BARBER SAVED MY HAIR!"; "BEAT THE RACES BY PICKING LOSERS!"
9. **Remove limitations from the claim** (p. 54) — "SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY!"; "YOU BREATHE NO DUSTY ODORS WHEN YOU DO IT WITH LEWYT!"
10. **Associate the claim with values or people the prospect wishes to be identified with** (p. 54) — "MICKEY MANTLE SAYS: CAMELS NEVER BOTHER MY THROAT!"; "9 OUT OF 10 DECORATORS USE WUNDA-WEAVE CARPETS FOR LONG LIFE AT LOW COST!"
11. **Show how much work, in detail, the claim does** (p. 54) — "NOW! RELIEF FROM ALL 5 ACID-CAUSED STOMACH TROUBLES — IN SECONDS!"; "RELIEVES CONGESTION IN ALL 7 NASAL PASSAGES INSTANTLY!"
12. **State the claim as a question** (p. 54) — "WHO ELSE WANTS A WHITER WASH — WITH NO HARD WORK?"; "COULD YOU USE $25 A WEEK EXTRA INCOME?"
13. **Offer information about how to accomplish the claim** (p. 54) — "HOW TO WIN FRIENDS AND INFLUENCE PEOPLE!"; "HERE'S WHAT TO DO TO GET RID OF PIMPLES FAST!"
14. **Tie authority into the claim** (pp. 54–55) — "BOSS MECHANIC SHOWS HOW TO AVOID ENGINE REPAIR BILLS!"; "HERE'S WHAT DOCTORS DO WHEN THEY FEEL ROTTEN!"
15. **Before-and-after the claim** (p. 55) — "BEFORE COLDENE A CHILD GOT OVER A COLD AFTER 5 DAYS OF ACHING, SNEEZING, WHEEZING, DRIPPING, SUFFERING, COUGHING, CRYING, GAGGING, SPITTING."; "WITH COLDENE A CHILD GETS OVER A COLD IN FIVE DAYS!"
16. **Stress the newness of the claim** (p. 55) — "ANNOUNCING! GUIDED MISSILE SPARK PLUGS!"; "NOW! CHROME PLATE WITHOUT HEAT, ELECTRICITY, MACHINERY!"
17. **Stress the exclusivity of the claim** (p. 55) — "OURS ALONE! PERSIAN LAMB ORIGINALS — $389.40!"; "ONLY GLEEM HAS GL-70 TO KEEP TEETH CLEAN ALL DAY LONG WITH ONE BRUSHING!"
18. **Turn the claim into a challenge for the reader** (p. 55) — "WHICH TWIN HAS THE TONI? AND WHICH HAS THE $15 PERMANENT?"; "DOES SHE OR DOESN'T SHE? HAIR COLORING SO NATURAL ONLY HER HAIRDRESSER KNOWS FOR SURE!"
19. **State the claim as a case-history quotation** (p. 55) — "LOOK, MOM — NO CAVITIES!"; "WOULD YOU BELIEVE IT — I HAVE A COLD!"
20. **Condense the claim — interchange your product and the product it replaces** (p. 56) — "NOW! A RING AND PISTON JOB IN A TUBE!"; "POUR YOURSELF A NEW ENGINE!"
21. **Symbolize the claim — replace the direct statement or measurement of the claim with a parallel reality** (p. 56) — "STARTING NEXT TUESDAY, THE ATLANTIC OCEAN BECOMES ONLY ONE-FIFTH AS LONG!"
22. **Connect the mechanism to the claim in the headline** (p. 56) — "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!"; "FEEDS WASTE GAS FUMES BACK INTO YOUR ENGINE!"
23. **Startle the reader by contradicting the way he thinks the mechanism should work** (p. 56) — "HIT HELL OUT OF THE BALL WITH YOUR RIGHT HAND, SAYS TOMMY ARMOUR!"
24. **Connect the need and the claim in the headline** (p. 56) — "THERE IS ONLY ONE SOLUTION TO AN ADVERTISING PROBLEM: FIND THE MAN!"
25. **Offer information in the ad itself** (p. 56) — "WHY MEN CRACK…"; "WHAT EVERYBODY OUGHT TO KNOW ABOUT THIS STOCK AND BOND BUSINESS!"
26. **Turn the claim or the need into a case history** (p. 56) — "AUNT MEG, WHO NEVER MARRIED…"; "AGAIN SHE ORDERS — A CHICKEN SALAD, PLEASE!"
27. **Give a name to the problem or need** (p. 57) — "WHEN YOU'RE WEARY WITH DAY-TIME FATIGUE, TAKE ALKA-SELTZER."
28. **Warn the reader about possible pitfalls if he doesn't use the product** (p. 57) — "DON'T INVEST ONE CENT OF YOUR HARD-EARNED MONEY UNTIL YOU CHECK THIS GUIDE!"
29. **Emphasize the claim by its phraseology — by breaking it into two sentences, or repeating it, or a part of it** (p. 57) — "A MAN YOU CAN LEAN ON! THAT'S KLOPMAN!"; "NOBODY BUT NOBODY UNDERSELLS GIMBEL'S!"
30. **Show how easy the claim is to accomplish by imposing a universally-overcome limitation** (p. 57) — "IF YOU CAN COUNT TO ELEVEN, YOU CAN INCREASE YOUR SPEED AND SKILL AT NUMBERS!"
31. **State the difference in the headline** (p. 57) — "THE DIFFERENCE IN PREMIUM GASOLINES IS RIGHT IN THE ADDITIVES!"
32. **Surprise your reader into realizing that former limitations have now been overcome** (p. 57) — "SEE WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU CRUSH A HARTMAN DC-8? NOTHING!"
33. **Address the people who can't buy your product** (p. 57) — "IF YOU'VE ALREADY TAKEN YOUR VACATION, DON'T READ THIS. IT'LL BREAK YOUR HEART."
34. **Address your prospect directly** (p. 58) — "TO THE MAN WHO WILL SETTLE FOR NOTHING LESS THAN THE PRESIDENCY OF HIS FIRM."
35. **Dramatize how hard it was to produce the claim** (p. 58) — "WHEN JENS FINISHED DESIGNING THIS CANDLEHOLDER WE HAD TO INVENT A WHOLE NEW KIND OF CANDLE."
36. **Accuse the claim of being too good** (p. 58) — "IS IT IMMORAL TO MAKE MONEY THIS EASILY?"
37. **Challenge the prospect's present limiting beliefs** (p. 58) — "YOU ARE TWICE AS SMART AS YOU THINK."
38. **Turn the claim into a question and answer** (p. 58) — "YOU DON'T KNOW WHAT'S UNDER THE HOOD AND YOU COULDN'T CARE LESS AS LONG AS YOUR CAR RUNS SMOOTHLY. WHO SHOULD YOU SEE IF IT DOESN'T?… SOMEONE WHO CARES — UNITED DELCO."

Closing line: "And so on: an infinite number of variations. Try to create your own — tomorrow." (p. 58)

## Creative Planning (Ch. 5)

### The three levels of creativity (pp. 59–61)

**1. Word-Substitute Technique — Echo Ads** (p. 59). "The shallowest, and the most widespread and ineffective." The copywriter consults a list of proven and successful headlines, pulls out the original product name, and substitutes his own. Result: "I'D WALK A MILE FOR A CUP OF BLANK COFFEE!"; "WHY HAVEN'T BOAT OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS?"; "FLOATS UGLY PIMPLES RIGHT OUT OF YOUR SKIN!" (pp. 59–60). Schwartz's verdict: "Echo Ads — ads that remind people of some other product. They pay no attention to the unique product-market-timing relationship that exists at the moment. Therefore they lose all the strengths that are wrapped up in that relationship. They are the kind of ads that clients write, rather than copywriters." (p. 60)

**2. Formulas** (p. 60). "Deeper and more difficult." The copywriter has memorized a list of rules or principles, and tries to "pour his headline into them like he'd pour hot lead into a mold. Such rules usually concern the way a headline is expressed. They list methods of strengthening the verbalization of the headline idea, and here they have a perfectly-valid use. Several examples of these principles are given in Chapter 4." (p. 60) — perfectly valid for verbalization, dangerous for ideas.

**3. The analytical / probing approach** (pp. 60–61). "The third, analytical approach that we have outlined above — with no answers; only guideposts and questions — offers the only way." "It means that a solution which has cost you days and weeks of painful effort, and which has done its job perfectly — can be used only once. It means that there are no creative shortcuts — that the effort must be duplicated with each individual ad. But fortunately the techniques of probing can be learned and perfected; intuition can be sharpened; a sensitivity can be developed for picking out the vital fact from a maze of information." (pp. 60–61)

Schwartz's warning to the experienced: "Many copywriters grow old, or tired, or afraid. They stop searching for the unique solution in every problem. From this moment on, they begin to copy instead of create. And most pathetic of all, many begin to copy themselves… But it won't work. Copying can be done by any cub. All this process does is bring talent down to the level of file-cabinet mediocrity. The true copywriter must argue with success — he must push on past it every time he faces a new product. In advertising, as well as in science and in art, the solution to the unique lies only in itself." (p. 61)

### On Motivation Research (pp. 61–63)

"As we have repeated throughout this first part of the book, the copy writer's primary job is to know his market. Many times, he has to know more about that market than the market knows about itself." (p. 61)

MR can be used in two ways: (1) as a source of information about hidden, verbally-unacceptable, or unknown desires — their drift, momentum, the taboos that limit them, splits in the market, points of difference, early reactions to the writer's own phraseology (pp. 61–62); (2) as a way of testing the writer's own hunches over time (p. 62).

**Direction not destination.** "But a motivation research finding is not a headline, nor even the central theme of an ad, nor will it ever be. Like any other fact, it is a direction. First it tells you where not to go, to avoid wasting your time. And then it indicates the general area of your solution. But the transformation of those facts into an idea, and the expression of that idea in the strongest possible form, still requires as much creative talent as any other starting point. The source of an idea, no matter how profound, is still only the beginning. The copy writer has to take it from there." (p. 62)

**Discourse with the market.** Schwartz frames the writer-market relationship as ongoing: "he will engage in a kind of discourse with his market, in which he feeds that market ideas, and it feeds back to him reactions to those ideas… he will pick up a continuous flow of the most vital information… much more of it will be in negative form — failures, roadblocks, limitations to the response from his ads."

**The why-questions.** Out of questions like "What causes one woman to make most of her clothes at home, and another woman to use her sewing machine only for minor repairs?"; "How can we convince more people that it's safe to buy through mail order?"; "Why will men instantly buy an automatic potato peeler — and women send it right back to the store again?" — "is born a new idea, a new theme, and a new headline, perhaps even a new campaign." (pp. 62–63)

### Product personality and the dominant trait (pp. 63–64)

"One of the most potent discoveries of motivation research is that a product, or a store, or a whole group of products has a distinct and complete personality to the consumer. This personality is a complex quality, embracing many traits. In the case of the Cadillac, for example, it consists of quality, prestige, performance, appearance, comfort, resale value, freedom from repairs and much more." (p. 63)

The load-bearing rule: **one trait must dominate.** "One of these traits will always be the most effective in summarizing and expressing this personality. In the case of Cadillac, it has always been, and will always be, quality. This summary trait is featured in a series of headlines, or headline illustrations — perhaps blended with one or two of the other traits to give it variety but always strengthening those other traits by interrelating them to this one dominant quality. Thus the personality is simplified, symbolized and sharpened to grasp the reader." (pp. 63–64)

### The only prevention headline that sells (p. 64)

"Many copy writers believe that no prevention headline (because it treats a problem that may occur and that is not actually plaguing your prospect at this moment) can ever be effective. The reason they give for this failure is the inability of the prospect to identify himself with a problem that has not already forced itself upon him.

This is true — but only for those problems which affect him personally. He is perfectly capable of imagining such problems afflicting his loved ones, his friends, his wife and children, even his nation. This is why decay-preventing toothpaste sold so well when the ads focussed the decay, not on the parent, but on the children. This is why life insurance can be sold, not by picturing the prospect's death, but the horrors inflicted upon his wife and children if insufficient money is left over to take care of them.

To sum up: A man will not visualize future disasters occurring to himself, but he is perfectly capable of visualizing, and buying preventatives from, the image of such future problems affecting others around him." (p. 64)

### Splinter markets (p. 65)

"Throughout this book, we have assumed that each competitor in a market will try to advertise to that market as a whole." But the assumption is wrong in two respects (p. 65):

1. **A market is not all of one piece.** The reducing market splits into "those who want to lose weight for appearance reasons, and those who must lose weight because of their health. The same general appeal — LOSE WEIGHT — will be effective with both. But certainly the mechanisms should vary — speed and ease in the first — safety and permanence in the second."
2. **Smaller budgets compete on sociology, not geography.** "A small company with a limited budget may avoid competing with larger rivals for the core of the market, and may concentrate its attack on a smaller segment of that market. This is usually done regionally. But it may be even more effective if it is based on the sociology of the market rather than its geography."

**Worked example — reducing aid for the health segment.** Abandon the appearance segment; appeal only to the health segment. "This deliberate focussing of the appeal would alter every aspect of the campaign, from the headline, through identification copy, through mechanism and substantiation — right down to the selection of media and channels of distribution. (Health and geriatric magazines, and distribution through doctors rather than drug stores.)" (p. 65)

**Invading the majority later.** Eventually, if the splinter strategy works, the advertiser can invade the majority market — using the success of the limited campaign as a point of difference: "the fact that women who were forced to lose weight permanently relied on this product more than twice as often as any other, etc." (p. 65)

### In Summary (pp. 66–68)

The first five chapters describe a process that "may take days, or weeks, or months to complete" (p. 66). The two-step kernel:

- **Step 1 — thorough analysis of the market:** measure breadth and depth, identify the gigantic emotional forces, define and focus those forces in terms of a single image or desire or need, channel them toward your client's product (p. 66).
- **Step 2 — meticulous study of the product:** physical and functional, all satisfactions and performances, focused to a single image, a single identification, a single claim that taps the greatest emotional force in the market (p. 66).

The combination yields "the theme of your ad — the desire your market demands and its satisfaction… the need your market feels and its solution… the identification your market gropes for and its expression" (p. 66). Then you explore market maturity (awareness + sophistication) to find "the point of entry for your headline — the point of contact — the point of greatest interest and acceptance on the part of your prospect. It may be located anywhere — in your product itself, in its price, in its performance, in the satisfaction your product promises, in the need your market demands from your product, or only in the market itself. But, wherever it is, this is the point where you start. Here the searching and the planning stop. Here the words begin." (p. 66)

**The five-to-ten words rule.** "At the end of that time, you may have written five or ten words. These five or ten words will make up about 90% of the value of your ad. If you are right, they may start a new industry. If you are wrong, nothing you write after them will save your ad. Copy writing — on the idea level — is an all or nothing profession. You build your idea, piece by piece… you crowd it into five or ten words… and then it stands or dies by itself. And everything that follows it, stands or dies with it. This is why we have devoted so much time to this planning process." (p. 67)

**The cardinal rule of creativity.** "What you are looking for in this product and this market is the one element that makes them unique. The idea you want — the headline you want — the breakthrough you want — are all wrapped up inside that product and that market. Nowhere else. And no outside ride [sic — likely 'rule'] — no outside formula will give them to you. You are facing a product-market-timing relationship that never happened before — it is unique. And the solution you need is just as unique." (p. 67)

**Compasses, not molds.** What Part 1 has provided: "not molds, but compasses. Not formulas to copy word by word, or rules to rigidly follow. But rather guideposts to set your thinking in the right direction." Two classes: (1) analytical procedures — break the problem down, find the most important part, start the solution there; (2) build-up procedures — remove limiting factors, add anything that strengthens the idea (pp. 67–68). "If we wrote literature instead of advertising, these two processes would be called conception and execution." (p. 68)

**Hand-off to Part 2.** The headline is the end of one road and the beginning of another — "the invitation to your ad — the hand you extend to your prospect for your product. Your job is now to make that introduction worth while — for both parties." Body-copy techniques are "directed toward one end: building conviction — not only instilling the desire for your product, but actually strengthening that desire with the belief that that product will DO what the prospect wants." (p. 68)
