# Mass Desire — deep dive

> Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Ch. 1 (book pp. 3–12) and Ch. 6 (book pp. 71–76).

## The thesis

Advertising power does not come from copy — it comes from desires that already exist in the market. The copywriter never *creates* mass desire (no advertiser can afford to); the job is to *channel and direct* a pre-existing public want onto a particular product.

> "Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product. This is the copy writer's task: not to create this mass desire — but to channel and direct it." (p. 3)

> "The power, the force, the overwhelming urge to own that makes advertising work, comes from the market itself, and not from the copy." (p. 3)

> "You cannot create it, and you cannot fight it. But you can — and must — direct it, channel it, focus it onto your particular product." (p. 4)

---

## The Amplification Effect

A market is *built* by social, economic, and technological forces "far greater than advertising itself can command" (p. 5). When advertising taps an already-existing desire, it inherits that built-up force as free leverage. When it tries to create one, it has to pay for every dollar of output with a dollar of input.

> Advertising that taps a built-up mass desire "commands an economic force hundreds of times more powerful than the mere number of dollars the advertiser can spend on it… the reason that $1 spent on such advertising can create $50 or even $100 in sales." (p. 5)

> When advertising tries to *create* a desire instead of channel it, "it is no longer advertising but education… it can produce at best only one dollar in sales for every dollar spent. No single advertiser can afford to educate the American public." (p. 5)

**The Edsel proves the rule.** "Here was a good car, backed by a deluge of fine advertising, that died trying to fight the overwhelming switch in demand to a cheap, simple, inexpensive-to-run compact car." (p. 4) Money + product quality + creative agency = catastrophe, when aimed against the tide.

The 1948 Chrysler short/squat car bucked the public's demand for "longer, lower, wider" cars: "A multimillion-dollar campaign was prepared by one of the most creative agencies in America. But the results — against the tide of mass desire — were catastrophic." (pp. 3–4)

By contrast, the 1954 horsepower wave (Twin-H Hudson Hornet, twin-exhaust Cadillac, Chrysler 300) rode the new desire and earned millions. Ford bet on safety, hit "a wall of disinterest," reversed course the next year, and "produced, and advertised, the highest-horsepower engines in their history." (p. 4)

## Defining a market

> "We can define this Mass Desire quite simply. It is the public spread of a private want." (p. 4)

A market is born "the moment a private desire is shared by a statistically significant number of people, large enough to profitably repay selling these people" (p. 5). Two thresholds must be crossed at once: statistical significance, and profitability.

---

## The three dimensions of every mass desire (p. 7)

Every mass desire can be scored on three axes. Schwartz's analogies make each one concrete.

1. **Urgency** — degree of demand to be satisfied; intensity.
   > "Constant arthritic pains compared to a minor headache." (p. 7)

2. **Staying power** — degree of repetition; inability to become satiated.
   > "Raw hunger compared to a craving for gourmet foods." (p. 7)

3. **Scope** — number of people who share the desire.
   > "The number of men willing to pay $10 for an automotive accessory that saves gas — as compared to those willing to pay the same price for one that merely prevents future repair bills." (pp. 7–8)

The three dimensions are how you compare the 2–4 candidate desires every product touches and pick the one with the most power *right now*.

---

## Permanent forces vs forces of change (p. 6)

**Permanent forces.** *Mass Instinct* — the desire of women to be attractive, of men to be virile, of both to keep their health. Plus *mass technological problems* — bad TV reception, corroding mufflers, slow-acting aspirin. "The instinct never fades — the desire never changes." (p. 6) With instincts, the copywriter's job is "to distinguish his product from the others… to create a fresh appeal — to build a stronger believability — to shift desire from the fulfillment offered by one product to that offered by another." (p. 6)

**Forces of change.** "The beginning, the fulfillment, and the reversal of a trend" — style shifts (the swimming pool over a bigger car), the Fifties horsepower appeal collapsing into gas-economy demand. (p. 6)

> "Here the copy writer is dealing with the straws in the wind that may indicate a hurricane. Here he needs sensitivity, foresight, intuition. He must be able to see and catch the rising tide when it's almost imperceptible — sense which of the several appeals that are built into his product he should stress at any particular moment, and when to shift to another — and, always, how to be there first." (p. 6)

**Mass Education** is the third driver (p. 7) — the school primer, movie screen, TV stars, presidential candidates, group pressure, and "the sum total of all advertising — in its unconscious, unplanned and overall effect of multiplying people's dreams and desires, and thus raising their standard of living." (p. 7) Education multiplies desires; it does not create them in any single ad.

## The three-stage channeling process (pp. 7–9)

1. **Choose the most powerful desire that can possibly be applied to your product.**
   > "Every product appeals to two, three or four of these mass desires. But only one can predominate; only one can reach out through your headline to your customer. Only one is the key that unlocks the maximum economic power… Your choice among these alternate desires is the most important step you will take in writing your ad. If it is wrong, nothing else that you do in the ad will matter." (p. 8)

2. **Acknowledge that desire — reinforce it — and/or offer the means to satisfy it — in a single statement in the headline.** The headline is the bridge between prospect and product, and where it begins is determined by what the prospect already knows:
   > "If he is aware of your product, and realizes that it can satisfy his desire, your headline starts with your product. If he is not aware of your product, but only of the desire itself, your headline starts with the desire. And, if he is not yet aware of what he really seeks, but is concerned only with a general problem, your headline starts with that problem and crystallizes it into a specific need." (p. 8)

3. **Take the series of performances built into your product — what your product does — and show your prospect how those performances inevitably satisfy the desire.** (pp. 8–9)

## Physical product vs functional product (pp. 9–12)

> "In reality, every product you are given to sell is actually two products. One of them is the physical product — the steel, glass, paper or tobacco that the manufacturer has shaped into a particular pattern… The other is the functional product — the product in action — the series of benefits that your product performs for your consumer, and on the basis of which he buys your product." (p. 9)

> "The physical product does not sell. People do not buy the steel in a car, the glass in a vase, the tobacco in a cigarette, or the paper in a book." (p. 9)

> "What they are really paying you for is what the product will do." (p. 9)

> "No physical part of your product can ever become a headline. No one will buy the size of your client's plant, the weight of your client's steel, the care of your client's construction." (p. 9)

Physical facts are not useless — they get used *later*, to justify price, document quality, assure continued performance, sharpen the mental picture, and give the claim "a fresh new basis for believability" (pp. 9–10).

**Worked examples of physical-fact-as-believability-document:**

- **Rolls-Royce.** Physical fact ("every metal part of the chassis is shielded from every other metal part by a protective coat of rubber") used to *document* the functional promise of "perfect riding silence." (p. 10)
- **Helena Rubinstein face cream.** Physical fact ("contains the placenta of living animals") used to make the skin-impurity-removal claim believable. (p. 10)
- **Ceramic mufflers, chemically-protected paper, quick-frozen food.** Physical features re-stated as *durability of performance over time*. (p. 10)

The general pattern: `[physical fact] means [continued functional benefit].` (e.g., "Ceramic mufflers mean no repair bills for the life of your car.")

The auto-industry desire inventory (p. 11) — every car offers transportation, dependability, economy, power, recognition, value, novelty, plus dozens more "hidden" desires uncovered by motivation research — illustrates the cardinal rule:

> "Every product gives you dozens of keys. But only one will fit the lock. Your job is to find that one dominant performance — squeeze every drop of power out of it in your presentation — and then convince your reader that that performance and that satisfaction can come only from your product." (p. 12)

## The three components of the prospect's mind (Ch. 6)

The body copy's job is to alter the prospect's vision of reality and create "a new world for your prospect — a world in which your product emerges as the fulfillment of the dominant desire that caused this man to respond to your headline" (p. 71). To create that world, the copy must "expand or alter one or more of the three dimensions of his already-existing mental world" (p. 71): Desires, Identifications, Beliefs.

### Desires (pp. 72–73)

> "These are the wants, needs, cravings, thirsts, hungers, lusts, etc. that drive your prospect through life. They are physical — such as the desire to be thin, or strong, or healthy, or free from acne, corns, bad breath or what have you. They are material — such as the desire to possess money, or a big car, or a beautiful dress. They are sensual — such as the thirst for a cold glass of beer, or the need for a tired body to stretch out on a soft bed." (pp. 72–73)

> "They have, of course, tremendous driving power. And they already exist. You cannot create them, diminish them, or battle them. But you can expand them, sharpen them, channel them, and give them a goal." (p. 73)

> "Your first task — and in some ads your only task — is to make your prospect want. To sharpen his desire. To picture every moment of its fulfillment. To let him see it, feel it, touch it, sit in it, listen to his friends rave about it. To make him visualize the wonderful new world your product offers him so strongly that he practically lives in it — and then to offer him that product." (p. 73)

The technique that does this — Intensification — is Ch. 7's subject.

### Identifications (pp. 73–74)

> "These are the roles your prospect wants to play in life, and the personality traits he wants your product to help him build, or project." (p. 73)

> "These longings for identification — longings for a sharply-defined personality — longings for social status — are, of course, not material or physical or sensual at all. They complement and intensify the physical desires — add another dimension to them — by making each purchase serve a double duty. Thus, not only does a woman buy a low-calorie food to become thin, but in so doing she also builds again a radiant, attractive, youthful personality. And not only does a man buy a car for the power, speed and transportation it will give him, but equally as much for the projection of prestige, success and ready-cash-to-burn that this purchase communicates to his neighbors." (p. 73)

> "Call them what you will — goals, hopes, dreams, ambitions, envies, admirations, phantasies or objectives — these subtle, symbolic, never-openly-spoken projections of our own self-images are immensely powerful sales forces. Your task is to put them directly behind your product. To make him feel the prestigious and select group he joins when he becomes a user of that product. To picture for him the people who live in your product's world today." (pp. 73–74)

### Beliefs (pp. 74–75)

> "These are the opinions, attitudes, prejudices, fragments of knowledge and conceptions of reality that your prospect lives by. This is the world of emotionalized reason that he inhabits — the way he accepts or rejects facts and builds up his universe, the types of thinking he uses to arrive at decisions, the ideas and values which give him comfort and which he believes are permanent and true." (p. 74)

> "These ideas may be shallow or profound, valid or false, perfectly logical or mere wishful thinking. But it is not advertising's mission to argue with them. And no one advertiser can change them." (p. 74)

> "Advertising, like science, must accept reality as it exists, not as it might wish it to exist. Only then can it alter reality — not by smashing into it head-on — but by exploiting its tendencies and giving direction to its energies." (p. 74)

> "Believing is a process — a process of fitting new facts into certain established patterns of thought and conviction. People believe in certain ways. These beliefs form a filter through which your product-information must pass or be rejected. And their already-established patterns of reasoning create habit-channels along which your copy must build its conviction — or die." (p. 74)

> "You start with these beliefs as a base. You build up from them by using his kind of logic, not your own, to prove that your product satisfies his desires — to prove that your product works — to prove that his kind of people rely on your product — to prove that no other product satisfies his needs as well." (pp. 74–75)

### Synthesis (p. 75)

> "There you have them. Desires…. Identifications…. Beliefs. Each of them composed of equal parts of emotion and thought. The three dimensions of your prospect's mind — the raw materials with which you will work."

In writing the ad, "you will weave them into each other — to create a simple, fluid path of thought from the beginning to the end of your ad. This technique — Blending and Reinforcement — will be the subject of Chapter 14."

## Worked examples in Schwartz's text

- **1948 Chrysler short/squat car** (pp. 3–4). Role: the headline cautionary tale. Bucking the "longer, lower, wider" public demand with a multimillion-dollar campaign produced "catastrophic" results. Demonstrates that creative quality and budget cannot overpower a mass desire pointed the other way.

- **1954 horsepower wave / Hudson Hornet / Chrysler 300 / Edsel** (p. 4). Role: side-by-side counterexample. Hudson, Cadillac and Chrysler rode the new horsepower desire and earned millions. Ford lost a year on safety, then reversed and produced "the highest-horsepower engines in their history." The Edsel was "a good car, backed by a deluge of fine advertising, that died trying to fight the overwhelming switch in demand to a cheap, simple, inexpensive-to-run compact car."

- **Liquid-meal-in-a-glass for weight loss** (p. 13). Role: the canonical opening example for pairing a known mass desire (lose weight) with a single dominant performance ("delicious, filling, already measured for you, as easy and pleasant to drink as a chocolate malted").

- **Helena Rubinstein face cream** (p. 10) and **Rolls-Royce** (p. 10). Role: physical-fact-as-believability-document. Used to prove that physical product details earn their keep only when they back a functional promise.

## Common failure modes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Fighting the trend instead of riding it. | "He can only exploit it. And he dies when he tries to run against it." (p. 3) | "Direct it, channel it, focus it onto your particular product." (p. 4) |
| Trying to *create* a desire (= education, not advertising). | "It can produce at best only one dollar in sales for every dollar spent. No single advertiser can afford to educate the American public." (p. 5) | Rely on the social/economic/technological forces already creating the desire, then channel it. (p. 5) |
| Choosing the wrong dominant desire for the headline. | "If it is wrong, nothing else that you do in the ad will matter." (p. 8) | Score the 2–4 candidate desires on urgency × staying power × scope; pick the one with the most power *right now*. (pp. 7–8) |
| Featuring multiple performances at once. | "Your headline is limited by physical space… the span of his attention will admit only one thought to penetrate his indifference during that glance." (p. 12) | "Find that one dominant performance — squeeze every drop of power out of it." (p. 12) |
| Headlining the physical product instead of the functional benefit. | "No physical part of your product can ever become a headline." (p. 9) | Lead with what the product *does*; use physical facts later to justify price, document quality, assure continued performance, sharpen the mental picture, and give a "fresh new basis for believability." (pp. 9–10) |
| Asking the headline to do the full selling job. | It puts the full selling burden on "approximately 10% to 20% of the total physical space of your ad." (p. 14) | The headline's only job is "to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence of your ad." (p. 14) |
| Arguing with the prospect's beliefs head-on. | "It is not advertising's mission to argue with them. And no one advertiser can change them." (p. 74) | "Exploit its tendencies and give direction to its energies… use his kind of logic, not your own." (pp. 74–75) |

## Ethical posture

In this range Schwartz does not lay down explicit "don't manipulate" guardrails. The closest things are two craft disciplines that double as honesty constraints:

- **Believability via real physical fact.** Claims must be *documented* — "the longer the car, the more tubes in the television set, the more stitches per inch in the suit… *if* that product first delivers the performance that your prospect demands" (p. 9). Intensification works only with images you "can legitimately present" (p. 78).
- **Accept reality as it exists.** "Advertising, like science, must accept reality as it exists, not as it might wish it to exist. Only then can it alter reality — not by smashing into it head-on — but by exploiting its tendencies and giving direction to its energies." (p. 74)

Beyond that, Ch. 1 and Ch. 6 contain only believability-and-channeling arguments — *channel, don't fight; document, don't fabricate* — not a moral framework. Ethical guardrails specific to particular techniques appear later in the book (e.g., the Personal Note on p. 50, the "never attack without solving" rule on p. 177); they are not present in this range.
