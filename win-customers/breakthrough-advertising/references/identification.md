# Identification (Technique #2) — deep dive

> Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Ch. 8 (book pp. 107–128).

## Core principle

Identification is the second mechanism of persuasion. It builds a "saleable personality" into the product so that owning it lets the prospect express the character role he wishes he had, or display the achievement role he has won (or wants to win).

It works because identification is "a special form of desire — the desire, not for satisfaction, but for recognition" (p. 115). Where Desire (Ch. 7) sells what the product *does*, Identification sells *who the prospect becomes* by owning it.

The price-parity / role-difference rule: when products do "the same job as [their] competitors, and [are] so priced that price is no longer a factor, then the prospect's choice will almost overwhelmingly depend on the difference in role that your product offers him" (p. 115). Identification carries the sale once function and price are at parity.

## Character Roles (pp. 110–112)

Character roles are *self-assigned, ambiguous, subconscious* identities the prospect projects onto himself. They are "not created by society but by the prospect himself, and since they are almost never claimed openly but only hinted at, implied and prompted, therefore they can never truly be tested or measured, and they are ambiguous. They are subject to error. And, more important, they are subject to fantasy" (p. 112).

"Great areas of these character roles literally exist in the subconscious. They are never given definite words. They are not stated or discussed, but subtly expressed in symbols and images" (p. 112).

Because they're never tested, "your prospect is far more ready to believe in the character roles you assign to him, than he is to believe in either your product's performance claims, or the achievement roles it may offer him" (p. 112).

**Test-pilot example.** Virility implied by "a test pilot smoking a cigarette" — "the prospect can easily persuade himself that the same action, smoking, performed on the same product, the cigarette, conveys to him at least some of the implied virility of the genuine possessor" (p. 112). The character role's superpower is "ease-of-acceptance, this consolation-without-cost feature" (p. 112) — no verbal claim, no test, no proof required.

## Achievement Roles (p. 113)

"These are the status roles . . . class roles . . . position roles that are created by every society on earth, and offered to the men and women who can earn them" (p. 113). Hundreds of them, "usually expressed by nouns, with the nouns serving as titles."

Schwartz's lists (mid-1960s framing):
- For men: "Executive" . . . "Home Owner" . . . "$20,000-a-year-Man" . . . "Five Handicap" . . . "Man-On-His-Way-Up" . . . "Block Chairman" (p. 113).
- For women: "Wife," then "Fashion Setter" . . . "Career Woman" . . . "Good Mother" . . . "Civic Leader" . . . "Power-Behind-the-Throne" . . . "Patron of the Arts" (p. 113).

Each role is "an achievement to be won, and held, and — most of all — displayed" (p. 113). Because the achievements themselves are "immaterial and invisible — mere titles, roles, bundles of privilege," they "must first be translated into physical symbols of success, for everyone around us to see" (p. 113).

**The display-via-possessions rule:** "the easiest and most universally acknowledged symbols of success in America today are the products we can buy . . . In America today we are known — not only by the company we keep — but by the products we own" (p. 113).

**The wedding-ring + Ford → Buick → Cadillac progression (p. 114).** The newly-wed receives a wedding ring "which has absolutely no function other than to define her" (p. 114); she discards a closet of clothes "that still fitted her physically, but not socially — in order to replace them with a new material personality, her trousseau" (p. 114). "Man-On-His-Way-Up . . . trades in his Ford for a Buick, and, when he becomes an 'Executive,' trades in his Buick for a Cadillac" (p. 114). Together these possessions construct a "material personality, that we carry with us wherever we go, and whose function it is to define us, instantly, to whomever we meet" (p. 114).

## The most generally compelling American roles (p. 117)

Schwartz's working list:

> "the wish to be virile or feminine — the wish to be exciting, unique, fun-filled and adventurous — the wish to be friendly and well-liked — the wish to be important, influential and correct — the wish to be modern and up-to-date — and, above all, the wish to be successful, to make something of our lives" (p. 117).

## The two-step image build (pp. 117–119)

Every product arrives with its own personality the moment you receive it. "It has its own characteristics. And these characteristics may contradict what most Americans want — or simply not include them — or simply not include as many of them as you want" (p. 117).

Starting-image inventories (p. 117):
- A cigarette is not, by itself, a symbol of success.
- A piston ring is not, by itself, a symbol of virility.
- Household appliances are not, at first glance, symbols of femininity.

**Step 1 — Identify the primary image** the product already has in the prospect's mind (cigarettes = virile; piston rings = precision-machined, mechanical beauty; appliances = time-saving) (pp. 117–118).

**Step 2 — Use the primary image as raw material.** Build "new, double, triple and quadruple images, that draw in more of these most-wanted roles into your product-personality, and multiply its identification appeal" (p. 118). Two ways:
1. **Intensify the primary image** — emphasize and dramatize it if it's already favorable, tone it down if it's negative or neutral (p. 118).
2. **Use the primary image as a logical link / bridge** to bring in other, more favorable images (p. 119).

## The Marlboro build (p. 118)

Primary image already accepted: male virility. "The sheer physical act of smoking — of 'playing with fire' — of 'breathing fire' — has been for centuries an assertion of manhood and of daring" (p. 118).

Marlboro intensified the primary image three ways:
1. Presented men who were themselves virile.
2. In situations/occupations that demand virility.
3. The "Creative Gamble" of putting a tattoo — "one of the most primitive symbols of virility known to history" — on those men's hands.

Result: "A single dominant emotion — virility — symbolized three ways. The impact of repetition reinforced through variation" (p. 118).

## The Chesterfield "Blow Some My Way" 1926 build (pp. 119–120)

Unfavorable primary image: cigarettes were "a man's product" — and the goal was to sell to women. Schwartz's rule forbids erasing it. The man had to be retained, but subdued.

"His figure is darkened, almost blended into invisibility with the background . . . the arrangement of his hands and face as the light plays over them — all direct the attention of the viewer past the man himself and into the focal point of the picture, the woman sitting beside him" (p. 120).

He becomes "a mere suggestion of man, leading the viewer into a far more appealing overall image — that of a handsome young couple, alone together on a moonlit beach, heightened emotionally with the carefully-blended-in suggestions of escape, intimacy, and a sense of shared daring" (p. 120).

The retained primary image (man-smoking) becomes the *acceptability-bridge* that lets the woman enter the scene: once she accepts the scene, "Blow some my way" becomes a natural action she might take.

## The substitution rule (p. 119)

> "people just won't believe that a product is what it isn't. You cannot contradict accepted images or beliefs in advertising. This is not advertising's role. Nor is it really necessary" (p. 119).

Instead, "incorporate them in a larger, overall image — lower their emotional intensity — and use them as readily-accepted bridges to lead your prospect into far more compelling appeals" (p. 119).

## The Limit (p. 121)

> "There is only one limit to the number, or the range of favorable images that you can add to that product personality. Simply that you must always include the primary image as their base. They must emerge from that primary image, and they must be logically consistent with its broadest definition" (p. 121).

Marlboro's later layered images, all built atop the virile primary: tuxedo for success, boats / planes / horseback for adventure, a young girl for romance, a dark background or briefcase for affairs of importance, a rare book or antique telescope for culture (p. 121).

## Verbal vs visual caveat (p. 123)

Stated in words, the more elaborate role-images "must take on a measure of disbelief, which we express by saying they're 'corny.' However, expressed in visual terms, where they can be symbolized, and therefore implied rather than named, they enter into our minds unnoticed, and we accept them without question" (p. 123).

Rule: corny when stated, accepted when shown.

## Limits to Identification (p. 124)

> "Many campaigns have collapsed because they have asked their market to identify themselves with an unbelievable image. For example, the deodorant-soap campaign of several years ago that used an elite, society image to sell the mass market. The men and women who composed the market could not make the jump between themselves and the characters pictured in the ad. And they not only refused to believe the suggested identification, but the disbelief spread to the performance claims themselves, and killed the sales" (p. 124).

Two diagnostic questions before image-building (p. 124):
1. "What do people already believe about the personality of your product? Do they believe, today, that it has the character traits that you say it has? And — if they do — can they identify themselves, their lives, their present position in society, or their next step up in society with these traits?" If yes/yes, "you can use the image exactly as you've conceived it" (pp. 124–125).
2. "What other primary image do I have to use as a believability-bridge to connect what my prospect already believes with what I want him to believe when he finishes my ad?" (p. 125).

## The believability bridge (p. 125)

> "If you demand that your prospect jump across a believability-chasm, your ad will fail. If, however, you build a bridge of ideas or images across that chasm — starting on his side — then he will let you lead him almost anywhere" (p. 125).

If the prospect doesn't believe the product has those traits, start with what he already believes and build up. If he believes the traits exist but not that they relate to his life, "insert a first-seen image that he can immediately identify with, and use that acceptable identification as a hook to hang my target-image on to" (p. 125). Marlboro example: men who'd never identify with a yachting costume or opera hat accepted those once introduced via the acceptable virility concept of the male model smoking the cigarette (p. 125).

## Identifications from the Physical Product (pp. 125–128)

Every product is two products: "the functional product — what the product does for the consumer. And there is the physical product — what the consumer actually gets" (p. 125). The physical product offers three image-source areas (p. 126):
1. Its appearance.
2. Its components and structure.
3. The technical background from which it emerged.

**Car-glamour borrowing (p. 126).** "Borrow from the glamour cars — the severe streamlining and stabilizing fin of the racing car — the hub caps and wheel wells of the sports car — the exhaust ports and roof line of the custom car. And you blend their feeling of excitement and power and distinction into the family sedan" (p. 126).

**Micrin mouthwash — the doctor-bottle case (p. 127).** For chemical / non-mechanical products, "their physical appearance, to the manufacturer, is completely arbitrary . . . their physical package can take any shape you wish" (p. 127). Johnson & Johnson shaped Micrin's container like the glass containers "found only in doctors' offices and operating rooms . . . Use this shape to house an ice-blue liquid, that in itself suggests medical cleanliness, and you have a product that says instantly, 'This is doctor's-liquid . . . This is medicine. This works'" (p. 127). "The container itself becomes the finest image-illustration that the agency could use in creating their ad" (p. 127).

**Electronic-component appeal (pp. 127–128).** Give products with electronic components "an electronic flavor to both [their] appearance and [their] advertising. Show the radar installations, guided missiles, jet aircraft that use the same parts. Life for the average man is dull, completely lacking in adventure. Offer to let him participate in the frontier explorations of our world, and you have welded a tremendously potent appeal onto your product" (pp. 127–128).

## Hypothetical worked example: piston-ring ad (pp. 121–123)

Starting inventory is bleak: piston rings are "mechanical, precision-made, unseen. Nothing exciting here. It also has a primary situation — replacement, and always by a mechanic, and usually only when there's trouble" (p. 122).

Schwartz dramatizes those constraints upward without violating them:

- **Car** becomes a Mercedes SL-300 sports car (p. 122).
- **Garage** becomes a "sports-car shop . . . Neat, clean, precise. With high-power equipment . . . gleaming chrome and steel" (p. 122).
- **Mechanic** is "not young, not old, simply mature. Rugged, knowledgeable, precise" (pp. 122–123).
- **Owner** is "young, wiry, virile, but he too has been around" (p. 123) — a non-professional driver who races for sport (the roll-over bar tells us so) and carries "his success with a complete casualness (the absence of any special driving outfit — just slacks and a sport shirt)."
- **Relationship** between the two men: "Knowledge complementing skill . . . Comfort, understanding, teamwork — leading to mutual achievement" (p. 123).
- **Action.** Replacing rings — but not because of trouble. "Because this mechanic is installing these new rings in this car as high-performance equipment, exactly as he would install a supercharger to increase its horsepower" (p. 123).

Outcome: "It is a picture, a situation and a mood that invites participation . . . He will wish to share in this world. And he will buy the product that gives him this world — that offers it as a bonus to all its functional and physical satisfactions" (p. 124).

Every layer is logically consistent with the primary image (precision-machined, mechanical, replacement-by-mechanic) — Schwartz never substitutes; he intensifies and bridges.

## Common pitfalls

- **Unrealistic identification.** Asking the market to identify with an image they cannot reach (the deodorant-soap collapse, p. 124). Disbelief spreads from the role to the performance claims and kills the sale.
- **Substituting a more flattering image for the primary one.** "People just won't believe that a product is what it isn't" (p. 119). The fix is incorporation, not replacement.
- **Demanding the leap without a bridge.** If the believability-chasm is wide, build a first-seen image starting on his side (p. 125).
- **Verbalizing what should be shown.** Stating role-claims explicitly triggers the "corny" reaction; the same images shown visually slip past disbelief (p. 123).
- **Adding new images that abandon the base.** Every additional image must logically emerge from the primary image, or the structure collapses (p. 121).

## Ethical posture

Schwartz's framing is descriptive, not prescriptive. Character roles work *because* they are "subject to fantasy" and never tested (p. 112) — he treats this as a mechanism, not as deception to be defended.

The substitution rule — "people just won't believe that a product is what it isn't" (p. 119) — is framed as **ineffectiveness**, not ethics. The implicit guardrail is structural: identification only works when the prospect can actually reach the role via the product, and when the additional images stay logically consistent with the primary image (p. 121). Where identification is forced past those limits, the campaign collapses on its own (p. 124).
