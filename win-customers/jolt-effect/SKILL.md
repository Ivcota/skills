# JOLT Effect

JOLT deals with **FOMU**: the buyer’s fear of messing up. Once the buyer accepts that change is needed, stop adding pressure, options, and information. Build decision confidence by judging indecision, recommending a path, limiting exploration, and taking risk off the table.

## Run sequence

1. **Choose the branch.** Classify the request as teach, apply, diagnose, or forecast coaching. Completion: the answer format matches the branch instead of giving generic sales advice.
2. **Separate status quo from indecision.** Decide whether the buyer rejects change or fears deciding. Completion: name the primary blocker and the evidence for it.
3. **Judge indecision.** Score the buyer as low, medium, or high indecision, then identify the source: valuation problem, lack of information, or outcome uncertainty. Completion: the diagnosis includes a level, source, symptoms, and whether the deal is worth continuing.
4. **JOLT the deal.** Apply the matching move: offer your recommendation, limit the exploration, or take risk off the table. Completion: the output includes a seller action and buyer-facing language.
5. **Secure a confident next commitment.** Ask for the smallest decision that advances the buyer toward a safe purchase. Completion: the next step is a decision, not a vague check-in.

## Blocker diagnosis

| Buyer state | What it means | Seller move |
|---|---|---|
| **Status quo** | Buyer does not believe change is necessary. | Teach commercial insight, quantify cost of inaction, and reframe the problem. |
| **Indecision** | Buyer believes change is needed but fears choosing wrong. | Reduce perceived decision risk with JOLT. |

Status-quo language: “not a priority,” “we are fine for now,” “maybe next year,” “I do not see the business case.”

Indecision language: “we need to think,” “send more info,” “we are comparing options,” “what if rollout fails,” “we need everyone aligned.”

## J — Judge indecision

Qualify for **ability to decide**, not only ability to buy.

| Level | Signals | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | Clear owner, stable criteria, focused questions, accepts tradeoffs. | Proceed and keep the process simple. |
| **Medium** | Repeated validation, new stakeholders, extra research, anxiety about consequences. | JOLT deliberately. |
| **High** | No owner, changing criteria, endless exploration, no history of decisions, refusal to trade off. | De-risk radically or qualify out. |

Judge with these prompts:

- “When your team has made decisions like this before, what slowed things down?”
- “What would make this feel like a safe decision?”
- “What are you most worried could go wrong?”
- “Who needs to feel confident before this moves forward?”
- “What would be enough information to decide?”

Completion: never advise a close until the buyer’s indecision level and source are explicit.

## Three sources of indecision

| Source | Buyer fear | Symptoms | Primary move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Valuation problem** | “Which option is best?” | Option comparison, package confusion, shifting criteria, stakeholder preference fights. | Offer your recommendation. |
| **Lack of information** | “What if we do not know enough?” | More demos, more references, more documents, no decision after each answer. | Limit the exploration. |
| **Outcome uncertainty** | “What if it fails?” | Implementation fear, ROI doubt, political risk, past vendor scars. | Take risk off the table. |

## O — Offer your recommendation

Indecisive buyers need guidance, not neutrality. Give a firm, reasoned recommendation and explain the tradeoff.

Pattern:

1. Restate the buyer’s priority.
2. Name the options briefly.
3. Recommend one path.
4. Say what not to choose and why.
5. Ask for reaction.

Useful language:

- “Given your team size and timeline, I recommend starting with [option].”
- “I would not start with [larger option] because it adds rollout risk before you have adoption proof.”
- “If I were in your seat, I would optimize for [buyer priority], not [tempting but secondary criterion].”
- “The tradeoff is [cost], but it protects you from [bigger risk].”

Completion: the buyer can repeat the recommended path and why it fits their situation.

## L — Limit the exploration

More information can increase decision risk. Help the buyer reach **enough certainty**, not perfect certainty.

Pattern:

1. List the remaining decision questions.
2. Remove requests that will not change the decision.
3. Curate the smallest proof set.
4. Define the decision threshold.
5. Tie the next step to that threshold.

Useful language:

- “Happy to send that. Before I do, how would it change the decision?”
- “It sounds like there are two questions left. If we answer those, would the team be ready to decide?”
- “Another demo may add noise. The real question is whether implementation is safe.”
- “At this point, I would avoid adding more vendors unless the decision criteria have changed.”

Completion: the seller and buyer agree what remaining information is sufficient to decide.

## T — Take risk off the table

Do not dismiss fear. Build a safety net that lowers downside without overpromising.

Risk types:

| Risk | Buyer fear | De-risking moves |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | “What if this wastes budget?” | Smaller start, phased spend, opt-out, success gate. |
| Performance | “What if it does not work?” | Proof of concept, relevant proof, success metrics. |
| Implementation | “What if rollout fails?” | Mutual action plan, onboarding plan, named owner, training. |
| Political | “What if I look bad?” | Executive alignment, internal business case, stakeholder-ready summary. |
| Opportunity | “What if we choose wrong?” | Clear tradeoffs, decision criteria, reference customer with similar context. |

Useful language:

- “Let’s make this feel less like a leap.”
- “The safest path is a [time-boxed pilot / phased rollout] with success criteria before expansion.”
- “Here are the three places implementations usually get stuck, and how we prevent each one.”
- “We can structure the first phase so your downside is limited.”

Completion: the buyer can name what protects them if the decision does not go perfectly.

## Branches

### Teach: explain JOLT

Explain only the parts relevant to the user’s question: FOMU, status quo vs indecision, the three sources, or the four moves. Completion: ask one check-for-understanding question or give one practical example.

### Apply: build a sales asset

For a call plan, follow-up email, close plan, or mutual action plan:

1. State the likely blocker.
2. Write the JOLT diagnosis.
3. Choose the O, L, or T move.
4. Draft buyer-facing language.
5. Define the next confident commitment.

Completion: the artifact reduces decision risk rather than adding pressure.

### Diagnose: stuck deal or message

Return this format:

1. **Primary blocker:** status quo or indecision.
2. **Indecision level:** low / medium / high.
3. **Indecision source:** valuation, information, outcome uncertainty, or mixed.
4. **Evidence:** buyer language or deal behavior.
5. **JOLT move:** what to do next.
6. **Talk track:** exact words to use.
7. **Qualification call:** continue, de-risk, or exit.

Completion: the user knows whether to keep pursuing the deal and what to say next.

### Forecast coaching: inspect pipeline risk

Score each open opportunity:

| Question | Score 1-5 |
|---|---|
| Buyer agrees change is needed. |  |
| Decision owner is clear. |  |
| Criteria are stable. |  |
| Indecision source is known. |  |
| Seller has made a recommendation. |  |
| Exploration has a defined endpoint. |  |
| Downside risk is reduced. |  |
| Next step is a decision. |  |

Completion: flag risky “commit” deals where interest exists but decision confidence is weak.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Treating every stall as status quo resistance | Diagnose whether the buyer wants change but fears deciding. |
| Adding more ROI pressure | Lower fear of purchase, not just fear of inaction. |
| Giving every option | Recommend the safest fitting path. |
| Sending every requested resource | Ask how the resource changes the decision. |
| Saying “there is no risk” | Name the risk and build a safety net. |
| Accepting vague next steps | Convert check-ins into specific decision commitments. |
| Chasing high-indecision buyers forever | De-risk radically or qualify out. |
