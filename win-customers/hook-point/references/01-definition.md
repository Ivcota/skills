# 01 — Definition

What a Hook Point actually is, what it is *not*, and the historical proof that the principle predates Instagram by a hundred years.

## The full taxonomy

From Ch. 1 — *"A Hook Point can be comprised of text (e.g., a phrase, title, or piece of copy), an insight (from statistics or a professional's point of view, a philosophy, or a person's thought), a concept/idea or a format (e.g., an image or video), a personality or performance (e.g., music, sports, acting, or a cadence), a product/service, or a combination of some or all of these elements."*

Six forms a Hook Point can take:

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| **Text** | Halbert's perfume headline; the title *The 4-Hour Workweek*. |
| **Insight** | Hopkins' "FILM, a dangerous coating that robs teeth of their whiteness." |
| **Concept / format** | Tesla Cybertruck's silhouette; a meme card; a 5-second teaser. |
| **Personality / performance** | Bill Murray inside Jeep's Groundhog Day spot; Cristiano Ronaldo for CR7. |
| **Product / service** | Netflix's no-late-fees DVD delivery — the product *was* the hook. |
| **Combination** | Most modern campaigns (Nike's Kaepernick ad fuses text + personality + values). |

The form is whatever stops the scroll. The job is identical across all six: *grab attention in three seconds or less, online or off.*

## Hook Point vs USP vs tagline vs mission vs purpose

Ch. 1 — *"I've been asked if a Hook Point is the same thing as a unique selling proposition (USP), tagline, mission statement, or a brand's purpose. The answer is: 'Sometimes.'"*

| Term | Definition (Kane / cited sources) | Same as Hook Point when… |
|---|---|---|
| **USP** | "The unique benefit exhibited by a company, service, product, or brand that enables it to stand out from competitors." | The benefit itself is what makes people stop. Netflix's "DVDs to your door, no late fees" was *both* USP and Hook Point. |
| **Tagline** | "A short, memorable phrase… [that conveys] the main sentiment or feeling that you want people to associate with your brand." | The tagline itself grabs attention. Nike's "Just Do It" is the tagline; not the hook — the rotating athlete sponsorships are. |
| **Mission statement** | The values of a company. Nike's: "to bring inspiration and innovation to every athlete in the world." | Almost never the hook. Too abstract for 3 seconds. |
| **Brand purpose** | The reason the company exists. Nike's: "to unite the world through sport to create a healthy planet, active communities, and an equal playing field for all." | Almost never the hook for the same reason. |

The Disney example clarifies the line: Disney's USP is "they bring families together through experiences and content" — but the Hook Points are Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge, Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm. The USP is *general*; the hooks are *specific* enough to stop attention.

The Nike example clarifies it further. Tagline = "Just Do It." USP = the shoes. Mission = inspire athletes. Hook Points = LeBron James, Serena Williams, Kevin Hart, Michael Jordan, Colin Kaepernick, Vaporfly. Same brand, one core, dozens of rotating hooks.

**Practical implication for your copy:** don't ship your mission statement as a headline. Mission and purpose live *behind* the hook to give it meaning once attention is captured.

## A Hook Point is not clickbait

Ch. 1 — *"Clickbait captures attention but lacks substance. Using a Hook Point is a better and more powerful choice because it's always attached to an authentic and compelling story that provides value and builds trust and credibility."*

Lupinacci's diagnosis of BuzzFeed: *"running click-bait about the world's largest rubber band ball wasn't actually content worth spending time with and didn't help build a brand."*

The Ian Malcolm test (Lupinacci): *"This copy will get people to click on our ad, so we could create it — but should we create it? Is this how we want to invest in our brand?"*

## The underdog-vs-incumbent playbook

Hook Points are how small companies beat big ones.

**Netflix vs Blockbuster (Ch. 1).** Netflix's first hook: "DVDs to your door, no late fees." Then original content (*House of Cards*, *Orange Is the New Black*, *Stranger Things*). Then binge-watching (releasing all episodes at once). Kane: *"Blockbuster failed because they didn't come up with any original hooks — they simply tried to copy Netflix's, did so with less finesse."* Blockbuster, once $8.4B, filed bankruptcy 2010. Netflix grew to $140B market cap.

**Amazon's hook stack (Ch. 1).** In order: world's largest bookstore → one-click checkout → Prime → Kindle → Sunday delivery → Echo. Took share from Borders, Radio Shack, Payless, Toys-R-Us, Circuit City, Sears. The lesson: not one hook — a *succession* of hooks. As one decays, the next launches.

**Pepsodent (Hopkins, 1920s, Ch. 1).** Hook: *"FILM, a dangerous coating that robs teeth of their whiteness."* Daily brushing went from 5% to 65% of the population in 10 years. Pepsodent was the bestselling toothpaste for 30+ years. The hook didn't just sell toothpaste — it created the category of daily oral hygiene.

## Historical proofs — pre-digital hooks that still teach

These exist to show that the principle is old, the urgency is new.

**Tova Borgnine perfume (Halbert, 1977, Ch. 1).** Headline: *"Tova Borgnine Swears Under Oath That Her New Perfume Does Not Contain an Illegal Sexual Stimulant."* Subhead: *"Wife of famous movie star agrees to give away 10,000 samples of her new fragrance just to prove it's safe to wear in public."* 7,000+ at the LA launch. Revenue $20K/mo → $800K/mo. Time magazine coverage. Craig Clemens' read: the hook tapped the truth that "people wear perfume to impress and attract the opposite sex" and took the idea to a transgressive extreme.

**Timberland boots (Lupinacci, Ch. 1).** Headline: *"Your eyes are frozen. Your skin has turned black. You're technically dead. Let's talk boots."* The copywriter looked up the definition of hypothermia and dramatized the symptoms. Lupinacci: people "didn't even need to read the rest of the copy" — the hook alone delivered the value.

**Jeep "Groundhog Day" (Super Bowl 2020, Ch. 1).** Hook elements: Super Bowl date falls on Groundhog Day + Bill Murray (star of *Groundhog Day*) + Jeep Rubicon. Lupinacci, Jurkovac, and Doug Scott all cite it as the Super Bowl winner. The hook works because Bill Murray "embodies the Jeep brand — he's true to himself and a little bit quirky."

## The dark side — hooks can be used to damage

Ch. 1 documents Edward Bernays' 1929 "Torches of Freedom" campaign for Lucky Strike. Bernays got debutantes to light cigarettes at the New York Easter Day Parade as a feminist "freedom" gesture, tipped off the press, and got *New York Times* coverage. Female smoking rose 7% that year. Kane uses this case to set the ethical guardrail: *Hook Points can change the world for better or worse. The Ian Malcolm filter applies — could vs should.*

## The Taylor Swift case — same hook, three audiences

Ch. 1 — Kane sold the same website-builder technology to the record label, Swift's father, Swift's mother, and Swift herself by *changing the Hook Point per audience*:

- **Borchetta and Swift's father:** money saved + dropping the 90% bounce rate on the merch store.
- **Swift's mother:** trust + ease of use.
- **Swift:** creative control without code; she could change the entire backdrop with a click.

The technical product was identical. The hook moved.

## What this means for your copy

1. Decide which of the six forms your hook is. If you can't name the form, the hook isn't sharp enough.
2. Check whether you accidentally shipped your mission statement. Replace with a specific, attention-stopping element.
3. If you're the underdog, look for the *no-late-fees* of your category — the friction your incumbent can't remove without cannibalizing themselves.
4. Apply the Ian Malcolm filter before publishing. Could you ship this? Should you?
