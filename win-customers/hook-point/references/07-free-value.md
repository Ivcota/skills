# 07 — Free Value

The fastest way to accelerate demand is to give value before asking for the sale. Ch. 7 frames this as both a moral and a tactical position.

## The core principle

Ch. 7 — *"People love to buy but hate to be sold to."*

Kane on elevator pitches: *"The definition of a pitch is: 'To present or advertise, especially in a high-pressure way.' Essentially, you're almost trying to force someone to buy something. Instead, use the tools I've given you throughout this book to articulate your value proposition. Replace pitching with the use of Hook Points and storytelling."*

The reframe: *"There's a huge difference between language that's designed to sell and language that expresses value."*

## Jay Shetty — the canonical free-value engine

Ch. 7. *"Shetty has some of the most viewed people on the internet internationally"* with **4B+ views and 24M+ followers globally.** Roland Frasier asked Shetty on *Business Lunch* about virality.

**The five themes that make content shareable:**

> Adventure. Comedy. Emotion. Inspiration. Surprise.

Shetty's read: *"Virality is in direct correlation to how often a video is shared, and that people share a video not because it makes them think, but because it makes them feel."*

**Shetty's three-part formula for why his videos perform** (verbatim, Ch. 7):

1. **Real-life experience.** *"Shetty comes up with video concepts from actual experiences or conversations he's had with others."* One of his top videos came from a 35-year-old who said it was too late to take risks; Shetty made a video debunking that myth.
2. **Scientific backing.** *"He wants to present ideas that are credible and verifiable, so he always adds in data to support his messages."*
3. **Poetic + simplistic language.** *"We remember lyrics more than prose. Speaking poetically and with a cadence allows people to remember your videos and quote them to their friends."*

For your work: stop posting pure logic or pure inspiration. The trinity is *real story + data + cadence*.

## Lady Gaga — Mother Monster

Ch. 7 — Gaga gave her LGBTQ + outcast audience a *name for themselves.* She is "Mother Monster"; her fans are "little monsters." Kane: *"The term little monsters originated from her fear of death, alcohol, and drugs, but it's been a perfect marketing tool to appeal to the outcasts, misfits, LGBTQ community, and other marginalized groups."*

The free value: a *community identity*. She gave them a label that made them feel powerful in the way they already felt different. *"She's empowered her fans and has provided them with the tremendous value of feeling strong enough to stand up for who they are."*

She also showed up: National Equality March, Human Rights Campaign gala, the meat dress protesting the military's anti-LGBTQ policy. The free value is repeated *behavioral* signaling, not one campaign.

Apply to your audience: what's a *name* you can give the people you're writing for? What identity can they wear back to you?

## Authentic vulnerability as a hook — Lakhiani

The Foreword (Ch. — `part0006.txt`) and Ch. 7 reference Vishen Lakhiani / Mindvalley. Lakhiani's *most-engaged* posts weren't business wins — they were:

- His parents losing their home in a fire.
- His marriage ending.

The lesson: revelation people don't expect from a public figure *is* the hook. Brendan Kane is explicit that authenticity beats polish for engagement when the platform rewards shares-per-view.

Mindvalley's separate Adam Roa video (Ch. 3) uses the same principle — the meme-card hook *"Buy this car, you'll get girls. Buy this bra, you'll get guys."* opens with a shocking, almost-clickbait line, but the story is the opposite: don't buy into consumerism to attract mates. Authenticity is the payoff that justifies the hook.

## Google "Loretta" — authentic emotional utility, not borrowed interest

Ch. 7. Google's Super Bowl ad: a widower uses Google Assistant to find old photos of his late wife and remember her.

Lupinacci's read: *"The ad speaks to the fact that oftentimes our memories outlive the people, places, or things that we remember."*

What makes it work — what Lupinacci calls **branded utility**: *"It's not using 'borrowed interest,' meaning it doesn't attempt to merely entertain viewers with a reference to popular culture."* The ad has *"an authentic reason to engage in a conversation about love, loss, memory, and nostalgia"* because the product is *literally* the mechanism.

Anti-pattern Lupinacci names: a yogurt brand tying itself to a man's deceased wife. The product has no role in the story. Borrowed interest collapses.

Test for your copy: does the product *do the work* in the story, or is it bolted on?

Lupinacci's line: *"Brand is the reward you get for keeping the promises you've made."*

## Peter Park — the helipad as a free-value hook

Ch. 7 — Park's LA gym has a helipad on the roof. *"It's a unique, differentiating element of his business because there are very few gyms in the world that have a helipad that you can work out on."*

Kane and Park's plan: shoot workout videos on the helipad with the LA skyline as backdrop. The hook draws people in for the experience; once there, Park and his trainers prove the real value with the actual training; some convert to private clients. The viewers' own selfies become further hooks — word-of-mouth flywheel.

Free value form: *spectacle the customer wants to participate in.* They get a great photo. You get distribution.

## Information as free value — content marketing

Ch. 7 — Latham Arneson on Patagonia's "experience letters" in their magazines: customers learn about adventures, see how Patagonia gear was used, end up wanting the gear *because* of the knowledge.

*"You can provide knowledge around any product or service."* Worked examples Kane names:

- Tax adviser → tips on managing money throughout the year (Kane cites the YouTube channel *ClearValue Tax*).
- Yoga teacher → newsletter on benefits of poses or meditation.
- Sleep doctor → Michael Breus's free Dropbox handout after every speech.

## "Give away your best stuff" — Vaynerchuk's posture

Ch. 7 — *"Too many businesses operate out of the scarcity mindset that if they put out content for free, people won't want to pay for their products and services, when actually, the opposite is true — the more valuable the content you put out there, the more people will want to hire you or buy your product or service."*

Vaynerchuk gave away an 88-page content strategy document he could have charged $1,500 for. *"The smartest companies give away a lot of their best stuff."*

Tai Lopez (Ch. 7) hooks with the Lamborghini and the mansion. Keeps the audience with *"You must read these books!"* + actionable lessons. Catchphrase: *"Knowledge is better than college."*

## The Cold Outreach example — give value first

Ch. 7 — Kane's email template for cold outreach (he names: Disney, Xbox, Fox; $15M+ in deals closed this way):

> Hi, [person's name],
>
> I first want to say congrats on all of your success at [company name]. What you were able to achieve with [cite a specific project, product, or campaign] is truly remarkable.
>
> Because you're an expert in the digital field, I wanted to let you know about a new tech platform we've launched that provides exact data on what all of your competitors are spending on social channels…
>
> The intriguing part of the platform is that all of this data can then be mined and used to increase the quality score of your own videos, which in return drops the cost per view of your own campaigns…
>
> We're currently using this new technology with [list client names]. Because you're always on the cutting edge of digital, I wanted to forward this info along, as I thought it might be helpful. I would be happy to introduce you to my company's CEO if you're interested in learning more.

Structure: **specific praise → free insight / data → social proof → low-friction offer.** Of ~20 sends to this kind of template, one closed; that one became millions in business.

## Impact Arcs — the giving-first sales formula

Ch. 4 attribution, applied in Ch. 7. Craig Clemens' four-step copywriting structure:

| Step | What | Worked example (art investing) |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Ask** | A "yes" question | "Do you want to make maximum return on your money that is just sitting in the bank?" |
| **R — Reveal** | Show you were in their position; learned something | "I used to look for places to invest my money, but I didn't know I could get a large return by buying art." |
| **C — Call** | Name the discovery that brought you out | "I discovered a book by J Paul Getty… I started using the principles, and over the last five years I've averaged a 30 percent return annually." |
| **S — Send** | Call to action | "I put the strategies up on a web page that is totally free…" |

Can be deployed in a sentence, a paragraph, a 90-second video, or an hour-long talk.

## What free value buys

Kane's Jon Seifert / Ogilvy case (Ch. 7): Kane offered to *outperform* Ogilvy's best CNBC interview (2M views). The interview hit 2.1M. Kane: *"By providing value, you create win-win situations, which helps you help other people. And usually when you truly help people, they want to repay you, which is why providing value is a great tool for acquiring and keeping new business."*

The exchange Kane is naming: free value creates a reciprocity asymmetry. The recipient feels obligated to repay — with attention, with introductions, with money.

## Free-value checklist for your copy

- [ ] Did I name a specific community identity, or treat the audience as anonymous?
- [ ] Did I lead with a real story from someone's life — mine or theirs?
- [ ] Did I back the claim with data or a source?
- [ ] Did I write in cadence and short rhythm, not prose paragraphs?
- [ ] Is the product the *mechanism* of the story, or is it bolted on?
- [ ] If a stranger read only this post / video / email and never bought, did they still get something?
- [ ] Did I pitch in the first message? (If yes, rewrite. Lead with insight.)
- [ ] Did I touch at least one of: adventure, comedy, emotion, inspiration, surprise?
