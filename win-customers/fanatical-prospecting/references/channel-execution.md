# In-Person, Email, and Text Execution

## Core concept

Adapt the same prospecting spine—objective, relevant message, direct ask, next action—to each channel's economics and social expectations. In-person offers richer information but costs travel time; email must survive delivery, opening, and conversion; text is highly visible but most intrusive without familiarity. (Blount, chs. 18-20, pp. 201-244.)

## In-person prospecting

### Hub-and-spoke

1. Begin with preset appointments as hubs. (Ch. 18, p. 205.)
2. Use the CRM to find nearby prospects. (Ch. 18, p. 205.)
3. Plot a small cluster around each hub. (Ch. 18, p. 205.)
4. Choose the route with the least windshield time. (Ch. 18, p. 205.)
5. Protect time before or after the appointment and complete the route. (Ch. 18, p. 205.)

### In-person call

1. Approach with confidence. (Ch. 18, pp. 209-211.)
2. Identify yourself and state the reason. (Ch. 18, pp. 209-211.)
3. Gather information through conversation and observation. (Ch. 18, pp. 209-211.)
4. Ask for the chosen objective. (Ch. 18, pp. 209-211.)
5. Turn around objections without arguing. (Ch. 18, pp. 209-211.)

Prepare research, questions, materials for an immediate sale, and the CRM follow-up before entering. Use in-person as a supplement where territory density and deal economics justify it. (Ch. 18, pp. 201-213.)

## Email prospecting

### Three cardinal rules

1. **Delivered:** Use one-to-one messages, avoid suspicious bulk patterns, minimize unnecessary links/attachments, and maintain accurate addresses. (Ch. 19, pp. 217-218.)
2. **Opened:** Build familiarity, write a short relevant subject, and test patterns with the user's data. (Ch. 19, pp. 219-223.)
3. **Converted:** Personalize enough to make the message feel relevant and ask for a defined outcome. (Ch. 19, p. 224.)

### AMMO planning

- **Audience:** Who is receiving it and what is their role, style, context, and familiarity? (Ch. 19, pp. 225-227.)
- **Method:** Standalone, sequence, nurture, strategic campaign, or cross-channel touch? (Ch. 19, pp. 225-227.)
- **Message:** What will connect and compel action? (Ch. 19, pp. 225-227.)
- **Outcome:** What exact response or step should occur? (Ch. 19, pp. 225-227.)

### Hook-Relate-Bridge-Ask

```text
Subject: [short, relevant hook]
[Name],
[Hook: role, trigger, issue, or achievement.]
[Relate: demonstrate knowledge of their situation without pretending certainty.]
[Bridge: connect the issue to verified value.]
[Ask: propose one low-risk action and a specific time.]
```

Source: ch. 19, pp. 228-233.

## Text prospecting

Use text most often after another channel creates familiarity. Good use cases include anchoring a networking conversation, responding to a trigger with a relevant resource, nurturing a known qualified prospect, or recognizing an achievement without an immediate pitch. (Ch. 20, pp. 238-244.)

Seven structure rules:

1. Identify seller and company. (Ch. 20, p. 244.)
2. Manage tone carefully. (Ch. 20, p. 244.)
3. Be direct and brief. (Ch. 20, p. 244.)
4. Avoid unclear abbreviations and slang. (Ch. 20, p. 244.)
5. Use transparent links. (Ch. 20, p. 244.)
6. Proof before sending. (Ch. 20, p. 244.)
7. Track response and conversion. (Ch. 20, p. 244.)

## Channel selector

| Situation | Preferred use |
|---|---|
| Dense local territory, easy access | Clustered in-person qualification |
| Repeatable role/segment message | Personalized email from a reusable structure |
| Known prospect, recent positive contact | Brief text follow-up |
| Cold high-value account | Layer phone, voicemail, email, social, and selective in-person |
| Unknown database record | Efficient phone/email qualification before expensive field work |

## Copy patterns

- **In-person identity:** "I'm [seller] with [company]. The reason I stopped by is [objective]." (Ch. 18, pp. 209-211.)
- **In-person question:** "Can you tell me how you currently handle [decision area]?" (Ch. 18, pp. 206-211.)
- **Email relate:** "[Peers in role] tell me [relevant pressure]." (Ch. 19, pp. 228-233.)
- **Email ask:** "Why don't we schedule a short call to learn about [their situation]? How about [time]?" (Ch. 19, pp. 228-233.)
- **Text follow-up:** "[Seller] from [company] here. I enjoyed our conversation about [topic]." (Ch. 20, pp. 238-244.)
- **Text resource:** "[Trigger/resource] made me think of [their situation]: [transparent URL]." (Ch. 20, pp. 238-244.)

## Cross-channel workflow

1. Select one objective and target level. (Ch. 9, pp. 71-83.)
2. Choose the lowest-cost channel likely to create the required interaction. (Chs. 4 and 18-20, pp. 20-24, 201-244.)
3. Add a second channel only when it creates familiarity, information, or access. (Chs. 12-13 and 19-20, pp. 96-131, 214-244.)
4. Keep the bridge consistent while adapting length and tone to the medium. (Chs. 14-15 and 18-20, pp. 132-177, 201-244.)
5. Log the touch and schedule the next action in the CRM. (Ch. 11, pp. 92-95.)
6. Stop a sequence that produces annoyance without new evidence; revise the target, message, or timing. (Chs. 16, 19-20, pp. 178-192, 214-244.)

## Completion criteria

- Every channel earns its place through access, familiarity, information, or conversion. (Chs. 4 and 12-20, pp. 20-24, 96-244.)
- The message retains one objective across the sequence. (Chs. 9 and 14, pp. 71-83, 132-153.)
- Channel-specific boundaries are respected. (Chs. 17 and 19-20, pp. 193-200, 214-244.)
- Results can be measured by channel and sequence step. (Ch. 6, pp. 36-40.)

## Guardrails

- Do not roam a territory randomly or use in-person to avoid the phone. (Ch. 18, pp. 201-205.)
- Do not enter secure or restricted areas. (Ch. 17, p. 200.)
- Do not send bulk prospecting email from a personal address or obscure destinations. (Ch. 19, pp. 217-218.)
- Do not fabricate personalization or proof. (Ch. 19, pp. 224-233.)
- Do not cold-text by default; after limited nonresponse, shift channels. (Ch. 20, pp. 238-244.)
- Never text while driving. (Ch. 20, p. 244.)
