# Message Framework

## Core concept

A prospecting message must respect the interruption: get to the point, make the purpose transparent, connect the request to a prospect-centered reason, ask directly, and leave room for an answer. (Blount, ch. 14, pp. 132-153.)

## Why it works

Prospects give time for their reasons, not the seller's. A relevant "because" lowers the perceived risk of the next step, while emotional, insight, or tangible value helps the prospect see why the interruption matters. Directness and confident delivery reduce ambiguity. (Ch. 14, pp. 138-153.)

## Message spine

1. **Objective:** Decide whether the touch seeks an appointment, information, a sale, or familiarity. (Ch. 14, pp. 142-148.)
2. **Context:** Use the prospect's role, segment, known situation, or a verified trigger. (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)
3. **Bridge:** Give a relevant reason that connects their situation to useful value. (Ch. 14, pp. 140-146.)
4. **Ask:** Request one concrete action and make it easy to accept. (Ch. 14, pp. 147-152.)
5. **Silence:** Stop talking and let the prospect answer. (Ch. 14, p. 153.)

## Targeted and strategic bridges

| Bridge | Use when | Evidence level | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Targeted | Many similar prospects share a likely issue | Segment patterns and clearly framed hypothesis | Low; supports higher activity |
| Strategic | The account or stakeholder is unusually valuable | Account-specific research, trigger, language, or proof | Higher; reserve for conquest targets |

Source: ch. 14, pp. 142-146.

Never present a hypothesis as a known fact. Use language such as "many leaders in [segment] tell me..." or "I imagine [trigger] may be creating..." when the situation is inferred. (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)

## Three kinds of value

- **Emotional:** Relate to stress, fear, frustration, uncertainty, relief, security, or peace of mind. (Ch. 14, pp. 145-146.)
- **Insight:** Offer useful information, a fresh perspective, or relevant practices that create curiosity. (Ch. 14, pp. 145-146.)
- **Tangible:** Use relevant evidence, outcomes, or case material, especially for analytical stakeholders. (Ch. 14, pp. 145-146.)

Do not invent proof, results, or statistics. Use only the user's verified claims.

## Drafting template

```text
[Name], this is [seller] with [company].
The reason I'm [calling/writing] is [single objective].
[Targeted or strategic bridge in the prospect's language].
[Specific ask with a concrete next action or time].
```

## Review test

| Question | Failure | Revision |
|---|---|---|
| Is it quick? | Long setup or company history | Cut to identity and reason |
| Is the intention transparent? | Clever opener hides the ask | State the objective |
| Is it relevant? | Features dominate | Add a prospect-centered bridge |
| Is the value credible? | Unsupported claim | Remove or replace with evidence |
| Is the ask direct? | "Let me know" or vague interest | Offer one action and time |
| Does it sound human? | Corporate jargon or scripted cadence | Use plain spoken language |

## Copy patterns

- "The reason I'm [calling/writing] is [objective]." (Ch. 14, pp. 138-148.)
- "Many [peer group] are [emotion/problem] because [cause]." (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)
- "I noticed [verified trigger]; I imagine [carefully framed implication]." (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)
- "I've helped [similar group] [verified outcome]." (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)
- "I don't know whether we're a fit, but [low-risk next step]." (Ch. 14, pp. 142-148.)
- "How about [specific day] at [specific time]?" (Ch. 14, pp. 147-152.)

## Message-building workflow

1. **Choose one objective.** A message cannot be concise if it asks for several different outcomes. (Ch. 14, pp. 142-148.)
2. **Name the audience.** Identify role, segment, account value, familiarity, and likely context. (Ch. 14, pp. 138-146.)
3. **Select the bridge type.** Use targeted patterns for scale and strategic evidence for high-value accounts. (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)
4. **Choose the value form.** Decide whether emotional, insight, or tangible value best fits the stakeholder. (Ch. 14, pp. 145-146.)
5. **Write the ask first.** Make the desired action and effort explicit. (Ch. 14, pp. 147-152.)
6. **Draft backward.** Keep only context needed to make that ask relevant. (Ch. 14, pp. 132-152.)
7. **Read aloud.** Remove jargon, weak language, and anything that sounds like a feature pitch. (Ch. 14, pp. 132-153.)
8. **Verify claims.** Check triggers, customer evidence, results, and timing before use. (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)

## Output variants

| Request | Deliverable |
|---|---|
| New outreach | Final script plus labeled objective, bridge, and ask |
| Message audit | Revised version plus the top three resistance-causing issues |
| Coaching | A short talk track and rehearsal cues |
| Sequence | One objective per touch and a reason each channel earns its place |
| Executive outreach | Research facts, carefully framed hypothesis, proof, and low-risk ask |

Source: ch. 14, pp. 132-153.

## Completion criteria

- The message can be understood on first hearing or reading. (Ch. 14, pp. 132-153.)
- The prospect can identify why the contact is relevant. (Ch. 14, pp. 138-146.)
- The value claim is specific enough to matter and verified enough to trust. (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)
- The ask is direct, concrete, and proportionate to familiarity. (Ch. 14, pp. 147-152.)
- Silence or white space follows the ask rather than more pitching. (Ch. 14, p. 153.)

## Guardrails

- Avoid feature dumps, generic company claims, and long scripts. (Ch. 14, pp. 132-139.)
- Avoid passive asks that transfer scheduling work to the prospect. (Ch. 14, pp. 147-152.)
- Never make up a problem, trigger, customer result, or emotional state. (Ch. 14, pp. 142-146.)
- After asking, do not fill silence by pitching or preemptively answering objections. (Ch. 14, p. 153.)
