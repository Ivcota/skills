# RBOs and Gatekeepers

## Core concept

Prepare for the small set of responses that recur when a prospect is interrupted. Regain emotional control, disrupt the expected sales argument, and ask again. With gatekeepers, use respect, transparency, connection, and direct help-seeking rather than tricks. (Blount, chs. 16-17, pp. 178-200.)

## Classify the response

- **Reflex response:** An automatic learned script such as "not interested" or "we're all set." It may not express considered truth. (Ch. 16, pp. 182-183.)
- **Brush-off:** A conflict-avoidance move such as "send information" or "call later." (Ch. 16, pp. 182-183.)
- **Objection:** A more specific, reasoned constraint, often accompanied by a because. (Ch. 16, pp. 182-183.)

Common categories include no interest, no budget, too busy, send information, overloaded priorities, and "just looking." Build a short response library from the user's real calls. (Ch. 16, pp. 184-185.)

## Anchor-Disrupt-Ask

### Anchor

Use a short, practiced statement to stop the fight-or-flight reaction and restore poise. The anchor is for the seller's emotional control as much as the prospect's experience. (Ch. 16, pp. 186-189.)

### Disrupt

Respond differently from the expected argument. Agree where possible, remove pressure, ask for specificity, or state the obvious. The goal is attention and movement, not defeating the prospect. (Ch. 16, pp. 186-189.)

### Ask

Immediately request a specific commitment of time or information. If a second RBO appears, handle it once more; if resistance remains, move on graciously and return another day when appropriate. (Ch. 16, pp. 186-190.)

## Turnaround builder

| RBO | Anchor | Disrupt | Ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Busy | Agree that timing is difficult | Shift toward a more convenient slot | Offer a specific time |
| Not interested | Acknowledge that the reaction is common | Introduce one relevant reason to reconsider | Offer a short next step |
| Happy with provider | Affirm that satisfaction is good | Remove pressure to change; offer useful comparison or familiarity | Request a low-risk meeting |
| Send information | Acknowledge | Ask what information is specifically relevant | Request the detail or follow-up time |
| No budget/timing | Acknowledge the constraint | Gather the buying-window facts | Ask permission for a dated follow-up |

Each completed row must use the prospect's real language and the user's verified value claims. Source: ch. 16, pp. 184-190.

## Seven gatekeeper keys

1. **Be likable:** Positive, polite, and respectful. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
2. **Use please:** Ask courteously and directly. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
3. **Be transparent:** Give full name, company, and purpose. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
4. **Connect:** Treat the gatekeeper as a person and listen. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
5. **Avoid tricks:** Schemes damage credibility and future access. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
6. **Ask for help:** Use an honest request and humility. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
7. **Change the game:** Use legitimate alternative channels, timing, events, email, social, or a useful note. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-200.)

## Copy patterns

- "That makes sense." (Ch. 16, pp. 186-190.)
- "I figured you would be [busy/satisfied]." (Ch. 16, pp. 186-190.)
- "Tell me specifically what information would help." (Ch. 16, pp. 186-190.)
- "How about [specific day] at [time]?" (Ch. 16, pp. 186-190.)
- "Would you please connect me to [person], please?" (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
- "I'm trying to reach [role]. Could you help me?" (Ch. 17, pp. 195-200.)

## Preparation workflow

1. **Collect language.** Review calls and messages to identify the RBOs prospects actually use. (Ch. 16, pp. 182-185.)
2. **Group patterns.** Consolidate variants under a finite set of response categories. (Ch. 16, pp. 182-185.)
3. **Write the anchor.** Choose a short statement that restores composure. (Ch. 16, pp. 186-189.)
4. **Write the disrupter.** Agree, remove pressure, or request specificity instead of arguing. (Ch. 16, pp. 186-189.)
5. **Write the ask.** Request a concrete time or piece of information. (Ch. 16, pp. 186-190.)
6. **Rehearse.** Practice until the words sound natural and attention can remain on the prospect. (Ch. 16, pp. 184-185.)
7. **Set the exit.** After repeated resistance, end graciously and record the next appropriate action. (Ch. 16, pp. 186-192.)

## Script review

| Test | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Emotional control | Anchor is short enough to use under pressure |
| Disruption | Response does not resemble a standard sales argument |
| Relevance | Disrupter connects to a real reason or question |
| Movement | Ask requests a specific commitment |
| Respect | Prospect can decline without being shamed |
| Natural voice | Seller can deliver it without sounding memorized |

Source: ch. 16, pp. 184-190.

## Gatekeeper workflow

1. State full identity and company. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
2. Ask courteously for the named person or decision role. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-196.)
3. If blocked, ask for help without disguising the purpose. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-200.)
4. Listen for useful routing information and preserve the relationship. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-200.)
5. Use another legitimate channel or contact time when direct access remains unavailable. (Ch. 17, pp. 195-200.)

## Guardrails

- Do not argue, shame, bully, or try to prove the prospect wrong. (Ch. 16, pp. 182-190.)
- Do not use "I understand" as empty filler before returning to a pitch. (Ch. 16, pp. 186-189.)
- Do not conceal identity, fake a relationship, or manipulate a gatekeeper. (Ch. 17, pp. 193-200.)
- Do not bypass physical security or enter restricted areas. (Ch. 17, p. 200.)
