# Case Studies

Use these examples to diagnose patterns or explain a framework. Preserve the lesson; replace the original company and person details when adapting to a user's situation.

## 1. Warm calls still require interruption

**Situation:** New outbound reps were assigned former customers but delayed calling while trying to prepare for every possibility.

**Intervention:** A leader modeled direct dialing, showed that the initial irritation was manageable, and focused the conversation on the next buying window.

**Result in the source:** The team learned to initiate conversations and the reactivation program became an important sales initiative.

**Framework lesson:** Call reluctance often comes from the act of interruption, not from how cold the record is. Move from preparation into the first live attempt.

**Use it when:** A seller insists that a warmer list, more research, or a better script must arrive before activity begins.

**Source:** Blount, *Fanatical Prospecting*, ch. 3, pp. 17-18; EPUB `text/part0010_split_002.html`.

## 2. A closing problem was an earlier prospecting problem

**Situation:** A seller repeatedly worked stale opportunities and sought closing techniques when deals stopped moving.

**Intervention:** Review of earlier behavior revealed a period with little prospecting. The seller resumed daily activity instead of trying to force old deals.

**Framework lesson:** Under the 30-Day Rule, today's slump can originate in an earlier activity gap. Diagnose pipeline inflow before prescribing closing tactics.

**Use it when:** A user reports that nothing is closing, the forecast depends on a few aging deals, or reps keep recycling the same records.

**Diagnostic questions:**

- What did new-opportunity creation look like one or more sales cycles ago?
- How many current deals have a verified next step?
- How many fresh qualified opportunities entered this week?

**Source:** ch. 5, pp. 28-35; EPUB `text/part0012_split_002.html`-`part0012_split_005.html`.

## 3. Felt effort versus tracked effort

**Situation:** A rep described a day as full of rejection and believed he had made many calls.

**Intervention:** A review of the actual call log showed little activity. Early rejection had changed his mood and his estimate of effort.

**Framework lesson:** Emotion distorts self-assessment. Visible activity tracking keeps coaching grounded in reality.

**Use it when:** The seller says "I called all day" but cannot provide counts, conversion ratios, or list progress.

## 4. Concentration changed the phone block

**Situation:** An inside-sales team spread prospecting through an entire day and consistently missed both call and appointment targets.

**Intervention:** The team prepared lists, worked inside a short fixed block, removed distractions, and pursued a clear outcome.

**Result in the source:** The same reps produced much more activity and more appointments in the concentrated block than in their previous diffuse routine.

**Framework lesson:** Work contracts when time, task, and outcome are constrained. Prepare before the block and log after it.

**Use it when:** A seller is busy all day but generates few prospecting touches.

**Source:** ch. 8, pp. 57-62; EPUB `text/part0015_split_005.html`-`part0015_split_008.html`.

## 5. Random CRM activity versus the Prospecting Pyramid

**Situation:** A team opened the CRM, selected a broad geographic filter, and called whichever record appeared first.

**Intervention:** Prospects were segmented by qualification, potential, probability, buying window, and objective. The team started with top-of-pyramid opportunities and used remaining time to qualify lower levels.

**Framework lesson:** List quality and sequence determine much of a block's effectiveness. The right message cannot rescue a consistently poor list.

**Use it when:** Reps spend time hunting through records or set low-quality appointments merely to create a win.

**Source:** ch. 10, pp. 84-91; EPUB `text/part0017_*`.

## 6. A trigger created a strategic bridge

**Situation:** Public information showed that a high-value prospect was expanding its sales organization.

**Intervention:** The message referenced the verified event, carefully connected it to a likely onboarding pressure, offered relevant experience, and proposed a specific short meeting.

**Framework lesson:** Strategic prospects can justify account-specific research. A good bridge uses the prospect's context and language while separating known fact from informed hypothesis.

**Use it when:** Writing to an executive, conquest account, or stakeholder for whom generic segment messaging is too weak.

**Message skeleton:**

```text
I noticed [verified trigger].
I imagine it may be creating [carefully framed implication].
We've helped [relevant peer] [verified outcome].
How about [specific next action and time]?
```

**Source:** ch. 14, pp. 142-146; EPUB `text/part0021_split_005.html`.

## 7. Resistance moved when the seller stopped fighting

**Situation:** Prospects responded that they were busy, uninterested, or satisfied with a current provider.

**Intervention:** The seller anchored with acknowledgment, disrupted the expected argument by agreeing or removing pressure, then asked for a specific commitment.

**Framework lesson:** The seller cannot argue a prospect into feeling wrong. Surprise and relevance can create a second look; a new ask turns attention into movement.

**Use it when:** Objection scripts sound combative, defensive, or too long.

**Source:** ch. 16, pp. 186-190; EPUB `text/part0023_split_004.html`-`part0023_split_005.html`.

## 8. Hub-and-spoke reduced windshield time

**Situation:** Outside sellers drove around looking for prospects and produced very few face-to-face touches.

**Intervention:** Each scheduled appointment became a hub. The seller mapped nearby prospects, chose an efficient route, left time around the appointment, and logged findings.

**Framework lesson:** In-person prospecting is expensive. It belongs in a planned cluster and a balanced channel mix.

**Use it when:** A field seller says in-person is the only effective method or spends large parts of the day driving.

**Source:** ch. 18, pp. 201-213; EPUB `text/part0025_*`.

## 9. Hook-Relate-Bridge-Ask made email relevant

**Situation:** A prospecting email targeted a bank operating executive whose role carried known complexity and pressure.

**Intervention:** The subject and opening hooked on the role, the body related through peer experience, the bridge used role-specific outcomes, and the ask proposed a short conversation at a specific time.

**Framework lesson:** AMMO plans the email; Hook-Relate-Bridge-Ask writes it. The message should sound as if it could only sensibly go to that audience.

**Use it when:** An email begins with the seller's company, product, funding, awards, or request for an unspecified "quick chat."

**Source:** ch. 19, pp. 225-233; EPUB `text/part0026_split_005.html`-`part0026_split_006.html`.

## 10. Text worked as a familiar layer

**Situation:** A qualified prospect liked the solution but lacked urgency and did not want to absorb the implementation effort.

**Intervention:** The seller used periodic calls and email, social engagement, and selective text messages containing personally relevant information.

**Framework lesson:** Text is strongest when it adds value inside an existing relationship. It is weak and potentially intrusive as an isolated cold channel.

**Use it when:** Designing nurture for a known prospect who is qualified but outside the buying window.

**Source:** ch. 20, pp. 238-244; EPUB `text/part0027_split_005.html`.

| Combative objections | Stop fighting |

| Generic cold email | Hook-Relate-Bridge-Ask |
