# The Four Lead Getters — Customers, Employees, Agencies, Affiliates

Expansion of SKILL.md Section 7. Based on Alex Hormozi, *$100M Leads* (2023), Section IV (PDF pp. ~248–351). Page citations point to PDF page positions in the 248–351 span.

---

## Frame — What a Lead Getter Is

A **Lead Getter is another person who runs the Core Four for you** — warm outreach, post free content, cold outreach, and paid ads — instead of you doing it yourself. Hormozi states it plainly: *"Only two people can let strangers know about the stuff you sell: 1) You 2) Other people. There are more of them than you"* (p. ~249).

The Core Four is a thing *you do*. Lead Getters are *who you go get* to do the Core Four for you. As Hormozi puts it, *"The lead getters aren't part of the 'core four' because they're not things you do… But, you have to do the core four to get them"* (p. ~252). Lead Getters compound the Core Four — once recruited, they re-run all four advertising activities for you, and ideally recruit more Lead Getters in turn.

### The Leverage Math

Hormozi's leverage frame (pp. 250–251):

| Mode | Work | Leads | Leverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| You do the Core Four | HIGH | LOW | LOW |
| One Lead Getter does it | LOW | HIGH | HIGH |
| Lead Getters who recruit Lead Getters | LOW | HIGH | HIGHEST |

That stacked leverage is the actual mechanism behind the *$100M Leads* claim — advertising is what you do to *attract Lead Getters*, not just customers.

### The Order — Why Customers → Employees → Agencies → Affiliates

Hormozi places the four flavors *"in the order of what happens naturally"* (Section IV Conclusion, p. ~351):

1. **Customer referrals (Word of Mouth)** — start here because you already have customers.
2. **Employees** — the next bottleneck once referrals can't keep up with workload.
3. **Agencies** — once you can afford to pay for skill transfer.
4. **Affiliates & Partners** — the most leveraged and the last: *"only when a business owner has a handle on managing people inside of their business do they tend to get the guts to try and manage people outside their business"* (p. ~351).

Don't try to do them out of order or all at once.

## #1 Customer Referrals (Word of Mouth)

> *"The best source of new work, is the work on your desk."* — Charlie Munger (chapter epigraph, p. ~254)

### Definition

*"A referral happens when somebody, a referrer, sends an engaged lead to your business. Anyone can refer, but the best referrals come from your customers"* (p. ~261). Referrals are not a tactic you "do"; *"It's a way of doing business. And it starts with you"* (p. ~285). The referral system is the combination of *building goodwill* (so they want to refer) and *asking* (so they actually do).

### Why Referrals Work — The Mechanism

Two compounding effects (p. ~261):

1. **Higher LTGP** — referred customers buy more, more often, and pay upfront.
2. **Lower CAC** — a free customer costs less than a paid one.

The killer concept: *"Referrals are exponential"* (p. ~262). Core Four inputs scale roughly linearly. Referrals scale 1 → 3 → 9 → 27.

### The Referral Growth Equation

The single most important equation in the chapter (p. ~263):

> **% Clients Referred Monthly − % Clients Churned Monthly = % Monthly Compounding Growth**

Three regimes:

- **Referrals > Churn:** you grow without ads. The asset compounds.
- **Referrals = Churn:** you tread water.
- **Referrals < Churn:** you must advertise just to stay flat — Hormozi calls this the **"hamster wheel of death"** (p. ~264). You're paying for new customers only to replace the ones you lost.

The diagnostic implication: if your business is plateaued, compute this number first. Below zero means the leak is in the product or onboarding, not in the ads.

### The Six Ways to Give More Value (Build Goodwill First)

Upstream of asking is *building goodwill* — and Hormozi maps the six ways onto the parts of an ad (p. ~266). The Six Ways To Get More Referrals By Giving More Value:

| # | Ad Anatomy Part | What It Becomes for Referrals |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Call Outs** | Sell to better customers (the right ones refer the right ones) |
| 2 | **Dream Outcome** | Set better expectations (under-promise, over-deliver) |
| 3 | **Perceived Likelihood of Achievement** | Get more people better results |
| 4 | **Time Delay** | Get faster wins (first 48 hours matter most) |
| 5 | **Effort & Sacrifice** | Keep making your stuff easier to use |
| 6 | **Call To Action** | Tell them what to buy *next* (set up the next sale or ask) |

The point: referrals are downstream of value. *"Customers only refer when they think it's very likely their friend will have a good experience"* (p. ~285).

### The Seven Ways to Ask

The asking framework (pp. 273–280). *"There are three components to a referral program: how you give the incentive, what you incentivize with, and how you ask."*

| # | Type | One-Line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One-Sided Referral Benefit** | Pay your full CAC to either the referrer or the friend (not both). |
| 2 | **Two-Sided Referral Benefits** | Split the CAC between referrer and friend (the Dropbox / PayPal model). |
| 3 | **Ask At Sale (Point of Sale)** | Embed the ask in the contract / checkout while goodwill is fresh. |
| 4 | **Negotiation Chip** | Trade a discount for a 3-way intro to friends. |
| 5 | **Referral Events** | Limited-time "Bring-a-Friend / Spouse Challenge / Accountability Buddy / Coach Challenge" promos. |
| 6 | **Ongoing Referral Programs** | Constantly market the benefits of doing the program *with* others (always-on). |
| 7 | **Unlockable Referral Bonuses** | Refer or leave a testimonial → unlock VIP, courses, status, premium support. |

### Timing — When to Ask

Two anchors:

- **Right at the point of sale.** *"On the sales contract or checkout page, ask for some names and phone numbers of people they'd like to do this with"* (p. ~275). Hormozi cites a salesman who became top performer by always asking at signup; *"Half his sales were referrals"* (p. ~275).
- **Right after a win — within 48 hours.** Customers form their *"lasting impression of a business within the first forty-eight hours after they buy"* (p. ~268). Wins (and asks) belong inside that window, before the impression sets.

### The WHO-Not-IF Rule

Pro Tip: *"**Not 'If' But 'Who.'** Once someone is a customer, be more direct with your ask. Don't ask IF they know someone, ask WHO they know"* (p. ~275).

A representative ask under 25 words:

> *"People who do our program with someone else tend to get 3x the results. Who else could you do this program with?"* (p. ~275)

### Common Mistakes

1. **Believing your product is better than it is.** *"Everyone loves our stuff, we just need to get the word out!"* — Hormozi calls that the tell of an unremarkable product (p. ~265).
2. **Never asking.** *"Your customers, like any audience, can only know what to do if you tell them"* (p. ~272).
3. **Lowering price instead of raising value.** Rory Sutherland: *"Any fool can sell something for less"* (p. ~266).
4. **Obsessing over the front end and neglecting the back end** — customers fall off, never refer (p. ~271).
5. **Asking IF instead of WHO** (p. ~275).
6. **Treating referrals as a one-off promo** rather than a way of doing business (p. ~284).

### Ethical Guardrails

- **Match the incentive to the product** so you attract real buyers, not coupon hunters (p. ~282).
- **Disclose discounts honestly.** Script: *"Stacy got $100 off because she referred three friends. I'm happy to give you $100 if you refer me three friends. Who do you have in mind?"* (p. ~277).
- **Lower the friend's risk by delivering** — building goodwill is the ethical floor.
- **The maxim:** *"I am compensated tomorrow for the value I provide today"* (p. ~285).

## #2 Employees

> *"If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together."* (chapter epigraph, p. ~287)

### Definition

*"Lead-getting employees are people working in your business that you train to get you leads. They get you leads the exact same way you got your own leads in the beginning. They can run ads, they can make and post content, and they can do outreach. They can do any advertising you train them to do"* (p. ~290).

The reframe: *"Employees are just other people you let know about your stuff"* (p. ~294). Recruiting is the Core Four pointed at potential teammates instead of customers.

### When to Hire — The Profitability Rule

Hire an advertising employee if you can keep CAC within ~3x industry average and your LTGP : CAC stays at least 3:1.

> *"If your cost to get a customer is within 3x industry average then you're doing good enough. From there, you focus on bumping up your LTGP"* (p. ~298).

The math (p. ~298):

- Total Payroll ÷ Total Engaged Leads = Cost per Engaged Lead
- Cost per Engaged Lead × leads-to-customer ratio = CAC
- As long as full CAC is *"at least one-third of the profit you make over the lifetime, you're in good shape"* (p. ~299)

Said differently: don't hire until the math says they pay back at 3:1 LTGP:CAC.

### The Internal Core Four — Talent Acquisition Mirrors Customer Acquisition

The "Internal Core Four" diagram (pp. 295–296) maps customer-acquisition activities one-to-one onto talent acquisition:

| Customer Acquisition | Talent Acquisition |
|---|---|
| Warm outreach | Asking your network |
| Cold outreach | Recruiting |
| Post free content | Posting job openings |
| Paid ads | Promoting job postings |
| Customer referrals | Employee referrals |
| Affiliates | Associations / guilds |
| Agencies | Staffing firms |

The opening parable that motivates the framework: *"We get one qualified candidate per ten screening interviews… it takes forty interviews to get a single, low-skill, frontline worker"* (p. ~289). Hiring becomes its own bottleneck — and the Core Four solves it.

### The 3Ds — Document, Demonstrate, Duplicate

The chapter's main *training* IP (pp. 296–297):

- **Document** — write the checklist exactly as you do the job.
- **Demonstrate** — do it in front of them, step by step.
- **Duplicate** — they do it in front of you. You fix the *checklist*, not the human, until results match.

The framing flip: *"If they get it wrong or get confused then we got it wrong or made it confusing"* (p. ~297) — own the system, not blame the person.

Pro Tip: *"Give Short Windows For People To Prove Themselves… If you trained someone properly and they're still below expectations three weeks in, cut them"* (p. ~297).

### The Diagnostic Question

When something isn't working, ask:

> **"Do my engaged leads have the problem I solve and the money to spend?"**

- If **no** → it's an *advertising* problem (wrong audience).
- If **yes-but-they-don't-buy** → it's a *sales* problem.

*"Don't fire your sales guy if you've got advertising problems"* (p. ~299).

### Common Mistakes

1. **"If you want it done right, you gotta do it yourself."** This kept Hormozi competing with his own team and *"never made me more money"* (p. ~293).
2. **Hiring before fixing the screening bottleneck.** 1-on-1 screening capped him at one candidate per week (p. ~289).
3. **Punishing during training.** *"Avoid punishment or penalties of any type for doing stuff wrong during training. Reward the good stuff you want them to do more of"* (p. ~297).
4. **Optimizing for the right *result* before the right *behavior*.** *"Focus on your employee's ability to follow directions more than whether they get the right result"* (p. ~297). If they follow your checklist and still get the wrong result, your checklist is wrong.
5. **Firing the wrong function.** Use the diagnostic question first.

### Ethical Guardrails

- *"Reward the good stuff you want them to do more of and they'll do more of it. Learning a new skill is punishing enough, we don't need to add to it"* (p. ~297).
- *"If they get it wrong or get confused then we got it wrong or made it confusing"* (p. ~297) — own the system.
- *"Treat them well, don't burn them out, and give them what they deserve"* (p. ~301).
- *"Everyone is replaceable, especially me"* (p. ~294) — the owner is not exempt from the standard.

## #3 Agencies

> *"Everything is for sale"* (chapter epigraph, p. ~302)

### Definition

*"Advertising agencies are lead-getting service businesses. You pay them to run paid ads, do outreach, or package and distribute content"* (p. ~304).

### When to Hire — Two Required Conditions

Both must be true (p. ~308):

1. **You have money.** *"Good agencies cost money. So if you have no money, then agencies are out of the question. You've gotta learn through trial and error."*
2. **You want to learn a new method or new platform faster than DIY.** *"Hiring an agency is all about investing in important skills you can't really learn anywhere else."*

Hormozi's personal model: hire a *"good enough"* agency to learn the ropes of a new platform, then hire an *elite* agency at ~4x the price to maximize it, then drop both to consulting once your team beats theirs (p. ~310).

### The Skill-Transfer Opener (Verbatim)

The exact pitch (p. ~309):

> *"I want to do what you do in my business, but I don't know how. I'd like to work with you for 6 months so I can learn how you do it. Plus, I'll pay extra for you to break down why you make the decisions you do… Then… I'd like to change to a lower cost consulting arrangement."*

Note: pay *more* up front in exchange for explicit knowledge transfer and a transition to consulting. Hormozi reports it cost him *"eight hours and $6000 to learn a skill that's made me millions"* (p. ~314).

### The 10-Point Vetting Checklist

Hormozi's signals for a good agency (pp. 312–313):

1. Someone you know got good results with them.
2. Prominent companies got good results with them.
3. They have a waiting list.
4. Clear sales process that sets *realistic* expectations.
5. No short-term hacks; they talk long-term strategy and clear timelines.
6. They tell you exactly what they need from you, when, and how they use it.
7. *They* propose a regular meeting cadence and reporting options.
8. Updates in plain English with clear cost-vs-results tracking.
9. They make a good offer scored on the four value-equation levers (Dream Outcome, Perceived Likelihood, Time Delay, Effort & Sacrifice).
10. They are expensive — *"All good agencies are expensive… but not all expensive agencies are good"* (p. ~313).

### What to Avoid

- **Agencies you only know from their *own* paid ads or cold outreach.** *"They probably aren't as good as the ones who rely solely on word of mouth (and the best ones do)"* (p. ~312).
- **The "agency lifecycle" trap** (p. ~306): senior rep on sale → junior rep takes over → results dip → cancel → repeat with the next agency.
- **No deadline.** Without a clock you stay dependent forever.

### Common Mistakes

1. **Believing "I'll never have to learn this stuff because they can do it."** Always start with a purpose and a deadline (p. ~309).
2. **Hiring an agency you can't afford** (p. ~308).
3. **Letting the agency lifecycle run** — senior-to-junior bait-and-switch (p. ~306).
4. **Not interviewing enough agencies.** Pro Tip: talk to five or ten before buying so the difference between good and bad becomes obvious (p. ~313).
5. **Skipping the deadline.**

### Ethical Guardrails

- **Be upfront.** *"If you are upfront about your intentions and the agency agrees, you get the best of both worlds"* (p. ~310). Don't covertly extract knowledge — negotiate to pay extra for it.
- **Win-win frame:** *"Both businesses benefit. They get a customer they otherwise wouldn't have. And we get a money-making skill for life"* (p. ~314).
- **Don't impose this model** on agencies that explicitly sell long-term retainer service. *"Some people never want to learn — and for those people, agencies are great"* (p. ~311).

## #4 Affiliates & Partners

> *"Nothing makes friends like money"* (chapter epigraph, p. ~315)

### Definition — Affiliate vs. Partner

*"An **affiliate** is a lead-getter. They are an independent business that tells their audience to buy your stuff. Affiliates seem like referrals on the outside, but are much different under the hood. First, they have their own businesses and do their own advertising. Second, they agree to offer your stuff to their engaged leads in exchange for money, free stuff, or both"* (p. ~317).

The implicit distinction in this chapter:

- **Affiliates** = paid promoters with their own business and audience. Often a one-time launch relationship.
- **Partners** = integrated affiliates who blend your product into their core offering long-term. The integration step (p. ~334) covers this: *"Integration is the long term strategy for using affiliates to get enduring lead flow."*
- **Super-affiliates** = a higher tier — *"agency super-affiliates who brought agency leads"* (p. ~319). Affiliates who recruit *other* affiliates.

### The Six-Step Process

(pp. 320–334)

1. **Find Your Ideal Affiliate.** List businesses with a warm audience full of people like your customers. Categorize by *softwares, products, equipment, services, groups they belong to, and events they attended* (p. ~321).
2. **Make Them An Offer.** Advertise to potential affiliates the same way you advertise to customers. The value-equation pitch:
   > *"Make more money from your current customers… without needing to build, deliver, or provide customer support… so you can start selling tomorrow."* (p. ~323)
3. **Qualify Them — Make Them Buy.** Get them to invest. *"Make them a customer, and make them an expert"* (p. ~324). Two ways:
   - Make them buy and use the product.
   - Make them pay for certification/training. Hormozi recommends pricing at 10–20% of what an active affiliate makes in year one (p. ~327).
4. **Figure Out What To Pay Them.** See the commission structure below.
5. **Get Them Advertising — Launch.** Use the **Whisper-Tease-Shout** method (p. ~330):
   - **Whisper** = call-outs / curiosity, weeks/months out.
   - **Tease** = elements of value, ~14 days out.
   - **Shout** = call-to-action, scarcity, urgency, last 3 days.
6. **Keep Them Advertising — Integrate.** Three integration models (pp. 333–334):
   - (a) Affiliates *give away* your lead magnet with their core offer.
   - (b) Affiliates *sell* your lead magnet.
   - (c) Affiliates *sell your core offer* directly.

### The Three-Tier Commission Structure

*"I suggest paying affiliates based on your maximum allowable cost to acquire a customer (CAC)"* (p. ~328).

| Tier | Trigger | Payout (% of max CAC) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | They **agree** (sign up, buy products/certification) | 25% |
| 2 | They **activate** (finish certification, do a launch) | 50% |
| 3 | They **sustain** a level of performance | 100% |

The hidden benefit: average payout is much less than max CAC, so blended LTGP:CAC improves (e.g., from 3:1 to 4:1 in his worked example, p. ~329).

Pay them for what you actually want: *"I pay affiliates for two basic things: new customers, and repeat customers"* (p. ~328).

### "Sell 3, Get It Free" — Pay With Product

Pro Tip (p. ~330): pay with *product* at lower tiers. The affiliate sees retail value; you only spend cost. This raises the perceived payout while protecting margin — the affiliate sells three to "earn" one for free, and you bank the spread.

### Common Mistakes

1. **Launching to affiliates without making them invest first.** *"If they don't believe in your stuff enough to buy it, they probably shouldn't sell it"* (p. ~324).
2. **Paying everyone the full max CAC.** *"I used to give away the farm"* (p. ~328). Use tiers so weak affiliates don't drain the pool.
3. **Starting affiliates without integration.** You get a one-time launch spike, not a long-term lead source (p. ~333; mirrors the Prestige Labs $150K-then-$450K story, p. ~316).
4. **Skipping qualification.** Not all affiliates are created equal (p. ~328).
5. **Capping payouts.** *"I never cap payouts"* — capping demotivates the people sending you the most customers (p. ~334).

### Ethical Guardrails

- **"You gotta buy it to ethically sell it"** (p. ~324) — the affiliate must be an actual customer.
- **Treat affiliates like customers.** *"If you deliver more value to them than it costs them to get it (especially hidden costs), they'll get you more leads than you can handle"* (p. ~349).
- *"You are only as good as the goodwill you have with your affiliate partners"* (p. ~349) — both sides should be better off, not just you.
- **Be honest about the work.** Affiliates fail when you let them launch without preparing the audience. The Whisper-Tease-Shout sequence ethically warms the audience instead of cold-spamming.

## Section IV Conclusion — The Unifying Principle

> *"The last skill you ever need to learn is how to get other people to do everything you need for you"* (closing epigraph, p. ~349)

Hormozi's synthesis (pp. 349–351):

- All four Lead Getters do one thing — **they let other people advertise your stuff using the Core Four.** *"You get more leads for the work you do when you have help."*
- Each Lead Getter has a single key strength:
  - **Customer referrals** = exponential, low-cost growth.
  - **Employees** = your direct influence; the business runs without you.
  - **Agencies** = skills you keep forever.
  - **Affiliates** = an army that runs once you set it up.

### The Two Ways to Compound

> *"Find more people that never stop buying your stuff OR find more people who never stop selling it for you. Referrals are the former. Affiliates are the latter."* (p. ~349)

That single line is the deepest framing of the chapter. The first half of Section IV (Customers, Employees) compounds *demand*. The second half (Agencies, Affiliates) compounds *supply of advertising*. A complete *$100M Leads* machine does both.

### The Natural-Order Principle

The order is not arbitrary:

1. **Customers** — you already have them, so referrals cost nothing to start.
2. **Employees** — workload exceeds you before money does; hire next.
3. **Agencies** — once you have money, buy skill transfer with a deadline.
4. **Affiliates** — only after you can manage people *outside* your business.

Don't try to skip levels. *"Only when a business owner has a handle on managing people inside of their business do they tend to get the guts to try and manage people outside their business"* (p. ~351).

### Stick With One Thing Long Enough to Win

> *"Once you find something that works for you — stick to what you pick… It's normal to lose in the beginning. In fact, I expect to crack a new lead source in three to six months"* (p. ~351).

The deepest takeaway is the inversion of "advertising": **advertising is what you do to attract Lead Getters, not just customers.** Once Lead Getters are in place, they re-run the Core Four for you forever — and that is the actual mechanism behind the *$100M Leads* claim.
