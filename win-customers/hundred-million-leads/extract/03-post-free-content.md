# Post Free Content + Free Goodwill — Study Summary

## Definition

In Hormozi's Core Four advertising matrix, **Post Free Content** is the "1-to-∞ / Public" channel: broadcasting valuable content to people who don't yet know you (and to those who do), so that strangers convert into a "warm audience" you can later monetize. The compounding asset is not the content itself — it is the audience built from it. Hormozi states the realization explicitly: "the content you create isn't the compounding asset — *the audience is*. So even though the content may disappear in time, your audience keeps growing" (p. 113). The chapter opens by re-positioning content as advertising: "Posting free content grows your warm audience… constantly posting free content means you'll have a constantly growing audience of people more likely to buy your stuff" (p. 117). In Hormozi's hands, "free content" is not branding, art, or self-expression — it is paid-with-time advertising whose goal is engaged leads.

## Mechanism / Why It Works

Hormozi's audience-building thesis is leverage. Warm outreach is one-to-one and doesn't scale: "If we want to reach ten people, we have to repeat ourselves ten times. Lots of effort. By posting free content, we can say it once and reach all ten" (p. 116). Free content compounds in three directions: (1) the people who find it valuable join your warm audience; (2) those people share it, recruiting more strangers into your warm audience; (3) every other Core-Four channel (warm, cold, paid) converts better when prospects can verify you exist and are credible. "If you reach out to someone and they can't find content related to your services, they're less likely to buy. On the other hand, if they find lots of valuable content, they are more likely to buy" (p. 118).

Hormozi reinforces this with hard data from his own portfolio: a survey of his gym-coaching clients revealed *"78% of all clients had consumed at least TWO long form pieces of content, prior to booking a call"* (p. 142). His prior belief — that paid ads alone drove the business — was false; free content was nurturing the demand that paid ads merely captured.

The mechanism, summarized: content is a one-to-many goodwill deposit. Each unit hooks a stranger, retains them long enough to deliver a reward, and that reward is paid back as audience growth. Audience growth compounds. The bigger the audience, the more engaged leads per unit of new content. Hormozi flatly calls audience-building "the most valuable thing I've ever done" (p. 114).

## Hook–Retain–Reward Framework

Hormozi reduces all audience-growing content to one atomic structure he calls a **content unit**: "The smallest amount of material it takes to hook, retain and reward attention is a **content unit**. It can be as little as an image, a meme, or a sentence" (p. 120). Long content is just many content units linked together (p. 128). All three jobs can happen at once.

### Hook

The objective of the hook is "to give them a reason to redirect their attention from whatever they are doing towards us" (p. 121). The hook is engineered through three levers: **topic**, **headline**, and **format**.

For *topics*, Hormozi prefers personal experience because "no one else has lived your life but you," and divides them into five categories: **Far Past** (life lessons), **Recent Past** (recent meetings, conversations, calls — "There's gold in these conversations" p. 123), **Present** (capture ideas the moment they arrive), **Trending** (ride attention already moving), and **Manufactured** (create a new experience just for content, like "I lived on $100 for a month") (pp. 122–124).

For *headlines*, he extracts seven attention components from news media — Recency, Relevancy, Celebrity, Proximity, Conflict, Unusual, and Ongoing — and prescribes including "at least two in your headline" (pp. 125–126).

For *format*, content must "look like *what they expect will reward them*" on the platform — mismatch and "better-looking content will hook them before yours even has a chance" (p. 127). LinkedIn-formatted content fails on Tinder and vice-versa.

### Retain

"My favorite driver of retention is *curiosity*" (p. 130). Hormozi retains attention by embedding unresolved questions — explicit or implicit — into the viewer's mind. He names three favorite vehicles: **Lists** (e.g., "Top 10 Mistakes"), **Steps** (sequential actions that produce a result, e.g., "3 Steps to Creating a Great Hook"), and **Stories** (events real or imagined; "All three drive curiosity because people want to know what happens next" p. 131). Lists are flexible with implicit reward; steps are rigid with explicit reward; stories interweave both. The action step: "Embed questions in their minds to make them want to know what happens *next*" (p. 132).

### Reward

The hook and retain stages are wasted if you don't pay off the curiosity you created. Hormozi defines content quality in one underlined sentence: "How good your content is depends on how often it rewards your audience in the time it takes them to consume it" (p. 132). Think *value per second*. He notes "there is no such thing as too long, only *too boring*" (p. 132). Reward means satisfying the exact promise the hook made — give seven ways if you promised seven; deliver to the audience you hooked (don't promise spouse advice to single guys). The empirical test is brutal: "Here's how you know if you succeeded: *your audience grows*. If it's not growing, your stuff isn't that good. Practice and you'll get better" (p. 133).

## Audience-Quality Principle

Hormozi distinguishes the audience that grows from the audience that engages. The quality test is whether content is built *for strangers*. In a boxed Pro Tip, he warns: "If you want to *grow* your warm audience, then you need to make content assuming the people consuming it have never heard of you before… consider the alternative, you litter your content with 'inside jokes' that no one gets besides your audience. Cool for you guys, but no one else will feel welcome. And your audience growth will slow down" (p. 129).

Quality is also measured behaviorally, not vanity-statistically. Two metrics: **Total followers and reach** (How big) and **Rate of growth** (How fast, in % per month) (pp. 144–145). A 50% monthly growth rate from 1,000 to 1,500 is a stronger quality signal than a flat-but-large follower count. Engagement is implicit in the share-loop: shared content recruits new strangers; if it isn't being shared, the reward step failed regardless of follower totals. Hormozi explicitly differentiates by saying *the audience decides*, not the creator (p. 132). Vanity audiences are those that consume but don't share or convert; engaged audiences are the ones whose growth rate proves the content rewards strangers, and whose self-selection ("they ask you privately") proves it converts.

## Volume / Frequency

The volume principle is the chapter's blunt heart. Hormozi paid an influencer $120,000 for content advice and got one sentence: "Bro, anyone telling you there's some secret is trying to sell you something. We just put out as much as we possibly can… *You just gotta do more bro*" (p. 115). After 10x-ing his volume, his audience grew 10x in six months. "Volume works. Content works. A growing audience is the result" (p. 116).

He prescribes a daily checklist: "How Much: 100 min per day. How Many: As many times as the platform shows it. How Long: As long as it takes" (p. 152). The cadence is *every morning, 7 days a week*. The honest reference point: "I posted a new podcast twice a week for four years before even getting picked up on the Top 100 list" (p. 147). The principle reduces to an aphorism inside the chapter: "Most people don't make money because they can't wait 12 months" (p. 135).

## Platform Principle

Hormozi gives a meta-rule rather than tactics. There are two opposing scaling strategies: **Depth then Width** (maximize one platform, then add the next) and **Width then Depth** (be on every platform early, then deepen them all together) (pp. 139–141). Depth-first compounds faster but risks single-channel dependence; width-first reaches broader audiences faster but burns labor and risks "lots of bad content everywhere. Sucky fluff" (p. 141).

The meta-principle underneath both is that *format must match the platform's native expectations*: "Format your content for the platform first. Then, tweak it so it hooks your ideal audience. Use the best content on the platform that targets your market as your guide" (p. 127). Pick a platform where your audience already pays attention, study what already wins there, and only then layer your differentiation. Hormozi's own process is minimalist: pick topics (~1 hour), batch-record short clips twice a month, and record 2–4 longer videos on the same day to anchor longer-form content (p. 141).

## Free Goodwill

"Free Goodwill" is a short standalone chapter that is *itself* an instance of the "give until they ask" doctrine — Hormozi practicing what he just preached. After spending two chapters teaching content strategy, he stops to make a soft ask on behalf of someone the reader has never met: leave the book a review. He frames it as a low-cost goodwill act that compounds into business outcomes for a faceless entrepreneur ("one more small business provides for their community… one more dream come true" p. 156). The ask is structurally a demonstration: a 30-second action that costs the reader nothing but signals reciprocity to Hormozi. Why is it separate from "Post Free Content"? Because content (Post Free Content) builds *your* audience, while goodwill (this chapter) builds *someone else's* audience by asking your audience to invest in them. As Hormozi writes: "If you provide something of value to another person, it makes you more valuable to them. If you'd like goodwill straight from another entrepreneur — and you believe this book will help them — send this book their way" (p. 159). Free Goodwill is the chapter that earns the right to have asked, by modeling the "give in public, ask in private" principle (p. 137).

## Illustrative Hook Patterns

1. (p. 122) "I complained to a friend that I didn't have enough time to do something *while glued to my phone*." (Far Past hook — vulnerability + irony)
2. (p. 130) "7 Ways I invested $1000 in my 20s That Paid Off Big" (List hook — number + specificity + outcome)
3. (p. 124) "I lived on $100 for a month. Here's how." (Manufactured experience hook — extreme constraint + promise of method)
4. (p. 131) "My journey from sleeping on the bottom floor of a gym to the top floor of a 5-star hotel." (Story hook — transformation arc)

## Mistakes

- Posting "inside jokes" that only existing fans understand — slows growth (p. 129).
- Pre-scheduling posts; manual posting performs better because of the live feedback pressure (p. 144).
- Multiple CTAs at once — "instead of doing them all, they'll do none" (p. 145).
- Asking too frequently *inside* content — Hormozi's friend's podcast shrank after over-monetizing (p. 138).
- "How to" preaching when you're new — switch to "How I" so no one can question your experience (p. 142).
- Treating content as the asset; the audience is the asset (p. 113).
- Quitting before 12 months — most people fail because they "can't wait 12 months" (p. 135).
- Making general content when you should be making narrow "puddle" content (e.g., plumbing-in-one-town before general business) (p. 143).

## Ethical Guardrails

- **Always reward what you hook**: "You did a bad job of rewarding. People will not want to watch again, and certainly won't share it" (p. 133). Don't bait-and-switch on the headline's promise.
- **Give until they ask**: "The moment you start asking for money is the moment you decide to slow down your growth" (p. 137). Don't drain goodwill faster than you deposit it.
- **Maintain a 3:1+ give-to-ask ratio**: Television runs 3.5:1, growing platforms run far higher; "give far far more than you ask" (p. 134).
- **Give in public, ask in private**: "Let the audience self-select when they're ready to give you money" (p. 136). Don't make every public touchpoint a sales pitch.
- **Don't kill the golden goose**: "Don't be like that. Don't kill your golden goose. It's a balancing act. Over-give to protect your most valuable asset — the goodwill of your audience" (p. 138).
- **One CTA at a time**: "A confused mind doesn't buy" (p. 145). Don't stack like/share/subscribe/comment/buy in the same ask.
- **Talk about your experience, not their obligation**: Switch from "How to" to "How I" — "When you tell a stranger what to do, it's hard to avoid coming off preachy or arrogant" (p. 143). Don't moralize.
- **Make good content, not whining content**: "Make good stuff people like and reap the rewards rather than whining about people's 'short attention spans'" (p. 144). Don't blame the audience for your own failure to reward.
