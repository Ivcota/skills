# Section IV — Get Lead Getters

Source: Alex Hormozi, *$100M Leads* (2023), Section IV, PDF pp. ~248–351.

Personal study summary. Citations point to PDF page numbers (the file's printed page is unnumbered; references are to PDF page positions in the 248–351 span).

---

## Frame

### What Hormozi means by "Lead Getter"

A Lead Getter is *another person* who runs the Core Four (warm outreach, cold outreach, post free content, paid ads) on your behalf, instead of you doing it yourself. Hormozi states it bluntly: "Only two people can let strangers know about the stuff you sell: 1) You 2) Other people. There are more of them than you" (p. ~249). The Core Four is a thing *you do*; Lead Getters are *who you go get* to do the Core Four for you. As he puts it, "The lead getters aren't part of the 'core four' because they're not things you do… But, *you have to do the core four to get them*" (p. ~252). So Lead Getters compound the Core Four — once recruited, they re-run all four advertising activities for you, and ideally recruit more Lead Getters in turn.

The book's leverage argument: doing the Core Four yourself is "Work: HIGH, Leads: LOW, Leverage: LOW"; recruiting one Lead Getter is "Leverage: HIGH"; recruiting Lead Getters who recruit Lead Getters is "Leverage: HIGHEST" (pp. 250–251). That is the math behind the "$100M Leads machine."

### The order Hormozi prescribes

He gives four flavors and says he placed them "in the order of what happens naturally" (Section IV Conclusion, p. ~351):

1. **Customer referrals (Word of Mouth)** — start here because you already have customers.
2. **Employees** — the next bottleneck once referrals can't keep up with workload.
3. **Agencies** — you reach for them once you can afford to pay for skill transfer.
4. **Affiliates & Partners** — the last and most leveraged: "only when a business owner has a handle on managing people inside of their business do they tend to get the guts to try and manage people outside their business" (p. ~351).

## #1 Customer Referrals (Word of Mouth)

Chapter epigraph: *"The best source of new work, is the work on your desk."* — Charlie Munger (p. ~254).

### Definition

"A referral happens when somebody, a referrer, sends an engaged lead to your business. Anyone can refer, but the best referrals come from your customers" (p. ~261). Referrals are not a tactic you "do"; "It's *a way of doing business*. And it starts with *you*" (p. ~285). The referral system is the combination of *building goodwill* (so they want to refer) and *asking* (so they actually do).

### Mechanism — why referrals work mathematically

Two compounding effects (p. ~261):

1. **Higher LTGP** — referred customers buy more, more often, and pay upfront.
2. **Lower CAC** — a free customer costs less than a paid one.

The killer concept: "**Referrals are exponential**" (p. ~262). Core Four inputs scale roughly linearly; referrals scale 1 → 3 → 9 → 27. The **Referral Growth Equation** (p. ~263): % Clients Referred Monthly − % Clients Churned Monthly = % Monthly Compounding Growth. If referrals > churn you grow without ads; if equal, you tread water; if less, you must advertise just to stay flat — the "hamster wheel of death" (p. ~264).

### Referral framework — the seven ways to *ask*

(pp. 273–280) "There are three components to a referral program: how you give the incentive, what you incentivize with, and how you ask."

| # | Type | One-line definition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One-Sided Referral Benefit | Pay your CAC entirely to the referrer **or** entirely to the friend. |
| 2 | Two-Sided Referral Benefits | Split the CAC between referrer and friend (Dropbox / PayPal model). |
| 3 | Ask For A Referral Right When They Buy | Embed the ask in the contract / checkout while goodwill is fresh. |
| 4 | Add Referrals As A Negotiation Chip | Trade a discount for a 3-way intro to friends. |
| 5 | Referral Events | Limited-time "Bring-a-friend / Spouse Challenge / Accountability Buddy / Coach Challenge" promotions. |
| 6 | Ongoing Referral Programs | Constantly market the benefits of doing the program *with* others. |
| 7 | Unlockable Referral Bonuses | Refer or leave a testimonial → unlock VIP, courses, status, premium support. |

Upstream of asking is **building goodwill** via the *Six Ways To Get More Referrals By Giving More Value* (mapped to the parts of an ad, p. ~266): (1) Call Outs → Sell Better Customers, (2) Dream Outcome → Set Better Expectations, (3) Increase Perceived Likelihood of Achievement → Get More People Better Results, (4) Decrease Time Delay → Get Faster Wins, (5) Decrease Effort & Sacrifice → Keep Making Your Stuff Better, (6) CTA → Tell Them What To Buy Next.

### Timing principle — when to ask

Two anchors:

- **Right at the point of sale.** "On the sales contract or checkout page, ask for some names and phone numbers of *people they'd like to do this with*" (p. ~275). He cites a salesman who became top performer by always asking at signup; "Half his sales were referrals" (p. ~275).
- **Right after a win.** Customers form their "lasting impression of a business within the first forty-eight hours after they buy" (p. ~268), so wins (and asks) belong inside that window.

Pro Tip: "**Not 'If' But 'Who.'** Once someone is a customer, be more direct with your ask. Don't ask *IF* they know someone, ask *WHO* they know" (p. ~275).

### Representative ask example

> "People who do our program with someone else tend to get 3x the results. Who else could you do this program with?" (p. ~275, ≤25 words)

### Mistakes (one-liners)

1. Believing your product is better than it is — "*Everyone loves our stuff, we just need to get the word out!*" Hormozi calls that the tell of an unremarkable product (p. ~265).
2. Never asking. "Your customers, like any audience, can only know what to do if you tell them" (p. ~272).
3. Lowering price instead of raising value — Rory Sutherland: "Any fool can sell something for less" (p. ~266).
4. Obsessing over the front end and neglecting the back end so customers fall off and never refer (p. ~271).
5. Asking "if they know someone" instead of "who" (p. ~275).
6. Treating referrals as a one-off promo rather than a way of doing business (p. ~284).

### Ethics / guardrails

- **Match the incentive to the product** so you attract real buyers, not coupon hunters (p. ~282).
- **Disclose discounts honestly.** If a full-price customer finds out someone got $100 off, the script is "Stacy got $100 off because she referred three friends. I'm happy to give you $100 if you refer me three friends. Who do you have in mind?" (p. ~277).
- **Lower the friend's risk.** "Customers *only* refer when they think it's very likely their friend will have a good experience" (p. ~285) — building goodwill by *delivering* is the ethical floor.
- **The maxim:** "I am compensated tomorrow for the value I provide today" (p. ~285).

## #2 Employees

Chapter epigraph: *"If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together."* (p. ~287).

### Definition

"Lead-getting employees are people working in your business that you train to get you leads. They get you leads the exact same way you got your own leads in the beginning. They can run ads, they can make and post content, and they can do outreach. They can do any advertising *you train them to do*" (p. ~290). Frame: "Employees are just other people you let know about your stuff" (p. ~294) — recruiting is the Core Four pointed at potential teammates instead of customers.

### When-to-hire principle (the profitability rule)

The rule of thumb: an advertising employee is worth keeping if you can keep CAC within ~3x industry average and your LTGP : CAC stays at least 3:1.

> "If your cost to get a customer is within 3x industry average then you're doing *good enough*. From there, you focus on bumping up your LTGP" (p. ~298).

The math (p. ~298): Total Payroll / Total Engaged Leads = Cost per Engaged Lead → multiply by leads-to-customer ratio = CAC. As long as full CAC is "at least one-third of the profit you make over the lifetime, you're in good shape" (p. ~299). Said differently: don't hire until the math says they pay back at 3:1 LTGP:CAC.

### Hierarchy — order of hiring

Hormozi explicitly leaves "fancy" director-and-up roles out (Author Note, p. ~301). For ground-level lead-getting employees the implicit progression is:

1. **Frontline doers** (cold-callers / SDRs, content producers, ad operators) — the people doing the Core Four activities.
2. **A manager of those doers** once you've stacked enough of them. He describes the trade: "swap 200 hours of work per week for twenty hours of management. Then, you trade the twenty hours of managing for a manager, who costs you four hours per week to lead" (p. ~290).
3. **Internal recruiters / staffing function** (the "Internal Core Four" diagram, p. ~295) — once hiring itself becomes the bottleneck (the opening parable: "We get one qualified candidate per ten screening interviews… it takes forty interviews to get a single, low-skill, frontline worker," p. ~289).

The "Internal Core Four" maps customer-acquisition activities one-to-one onto talent acquisition: warm outreach → asking your network; cold outreach → recruiting; post content → posting job openings; paid ads → promoting job postings; customer referrals → employee referrals; affiliates → associations/guilds; agencies → staffing firms (pp. 295–296).

### Compensation / training principle

The chapter's main *training* IP is the **3Ds: Document, Demonstrate, Duplicate** (pp. 296–297):

- **Document** — write the checklist exactly as you do the job.
- **Demonstrate** — do it in front of them, step by step.
- **Duplicate** — they do it in front of you; you fix the checklist, not the human, until results match.

On pay specifically he doesn't prescribe a single formula in this chapter — the cost model he tracks is total payroll vs total engaged leads (p. ~298). The implicit compensation principle is: pay enough to keep CAC within 3x industry while preserving 3:1+ LTGP:CAC, and reward output (engaged leads, sales) rather than activity. Pro Tip: "Give Short Windows For People To Prove Themselves… If you trained someone properly and they're still below expectations three weeks in, cut them" (p. ~297).

### Mistakes

1. Believing "if you want it done right, you gotta do it yourself" — this kept him competing with his own team and "never made me more money" (p. ~293).
2. Hiring before fixing the screening bottleneck (the opening parable: 1-on-1 screening capped him at one candidate per week, p. ~289).
3. Punishing during training. "Avoid punishment or penalties of any type for doing stuff wrong during training. Reward the good stuff you want them to do more of" (p. ~297).
4. Optimizing for the right *result* before the right *behavior*. "Focus on your employee's ability to follow directions more than whether they get the right result" (p. ~297) — if they follow your checklist and still get the wrong result, your checklist is wrong.
5. Firing the wrong function. Diagnose with one question: "Do my engaged leads have the problem I solve and the money to spend?" — if no, it's an advertising problem; if yes but they don't buy, it's a sales problem (p. ~299). "Don't fire your sales guy if you've got advertising problems" (p. ~299).

### Ethics / guardrails

- "Reward the good stuff you want them to do more of and they'll do more of it. Learning a new skill is punishing enough, we don't need to add to it" (p. ~297).
- Frame: "If they get it wrong or get confused then we got it wrong or made it confusing" (p. ~297) — own the system, not blame the person.
- "Treat them well, don't burn them out, and give them what they deserve" (p. ~301).
- Replaceability cuts both ways: "Everyone is replaceable, especially me" (p. ~294) — the owner is not exempt from the standard he sets.

## #3 Agencies

Chapter epigraph: *"Everything is for sale"* (p. ~302).

### Definition

"Advertising agencies are lead-getting service businesses. You pay them to run paid ads, do outreach, or package and distribute content" (p. ~304).

### When to use — Hormozi's heuristic

Two conditions, both required (p. ~308):

1. **You have money.** "Good agencies cost money. So if you have no money, then agencies are out of the question. You've gotta learn through trial and error."
2. **You want to learn a *new method or new platform* faster than DIY.** "Hiring an agency is all about investing in important skills you can't really learn anywhere else."

His personal model: hire a "good enough" agency to learn the ropes of a new platform, then hire an *elite* agency at ~4x the price to maximize it, then drop both to consulting once your team beats theirs (p. ~310).

### Vetting framework — what to look for

Hormozi's checklist of ten signals for a good agency (pp. 312–313):

1. Someone you know got good results with them.
2. Prominent companies got good results with them.
3. They have a waiting list.
4. Clear sales process that sets *realistic* expectations.
5. No short-term hacks; they talk long-term strategy and clear timelines.
6. They tell you exactly what they need from you, when, and how they use it.
7. *They* propose a regular meeting cadence and reporting options.
8. Updates in plain English with clear cost-vs-results tracking.
9. They make a good offer scored on the four value-equation levers (Dream Outcome, Perceived Likelihood, Time Delay, Effort & Sacrifice).
10. They are expensive — "All good agencies are expensive… but not all expensive agencies are good" (p. ~313).

What to avoid: any agency you only know from their *own* paid ads or cold outreach — "they probably aren't as good as the ones who rely solely on word of mouth (and the best ones do)" (p. ~312). Also the "agency lifecycle" trap (p. ~306): senior rep on sale → junior rep takes over → results dip → cancel → repeat.

### Pricing / contract principle

Negotiate the relationship around skill transfer and a sunset clause. The exact opener (p. ~309):

> "I want to do what you do in my business, but I don't know how. I'd like to work with you for 6 months so I can learn how you do it. Plus, I'll pay extra for you to break down why you make the decisions you do… Then… I'd like to change to a lower cost consulting arrangement."

Note: he pays *more* up front in exchange for explicit knowledge transfer and a transition to consulting. He reports it cost him "eight hours and $6000 to learn a skill that's *made me millions*" in the founding story (p. ~314).

### Mistakes

1. Believing "I'll never have to learn this stuff because they can do it." Always start with a purpose and a deadline (p. ~309).
2. Hiring an agency you can't afford (p. ~308).
3. Letting the agency's "lifecycle" run — senior-then-junior bait-and-switch (p. ~306).
4. Not interviewing enough agencies. Pro Tip: talk to five or ten before buying so the difference between good and bad becomes obvious (p. ~313).
5. Skipping the deadline. Without a clock you stay dependent forever.

### Ethics / guardrails

- Be upfront. "If you are upfront about your intentions and the agency agrees, you get the best of both worlds" (p. ~310). Don't covertly extract knowledge — negotiate to pay extra for it.
- Win-win frame: "*both* businesses benefit. They get a customer they otherwise wouldn't have. And we get a money-making skill for life" (p. ~314).
- Recognize this isn't the right model for everyone — "some people never want to learn — and for those people - agencies are great" (p. ~311). Don't impose his model on agencies that explicitly sell long-term retainer service.

## #4 Affiliates & Partners

Chapter epigraph: *"Nothing makes friends like money"* (p. ~315).

### Definition — affiliate vs partner

"An **affiliate** is a lead-getter. They are an independent business that tells their audience to buy *your* stuff. Affiliates seem like referrals on the outside, but are much different under the hood. First, they have their own businesses and do their own advertising. Second, they agree to offer *your* stuff to *their* engaged leads in exchange for money, free stuff, or both" (p. ~317).

The implicit distinction in this chapter: **affiliates are paid promoters** with their own business and audience; **partners** are integrated affiliates who blend your product into their core offering long-term (the integration step at p. ~334 covers this — "Integration is the long term strategy for using affiliates to get enduring lead flow").

The book also names a higher tier — **super-affiliates**, "agency super-affiliates who brought agency leads" (p. ~319) — affiliates who recruit *other* affiliates.

### Recruiting framework — the six steps

(pp. 320–334)

1. **Find Your Ideal Affiliate** — list businesses with a warm audience full of people like your customers. Categorize by *softwares, products, equipment, services, groups they belong to, and events they attended* (p. ~321).
2. **Make Them An Offer** — advertise to potential affiliates the same way you advertise to customers; the value-equation pitch is "make more money from your current customers… without needing to build, deliver, or provide customer support… so you can start selling tomorrow" (p. ~323).
3. **Qualify Them** — get them to invest. "Make them a customer, and make them an expert" (p. ~324). Way #1: make them buy and use the product. Way #2: make them pay for certification/training (10–20% of what an active affiliate makes in year one is his recommended onboarding price, p. ~327).
4. **Figure Out What To Pay Them** — see commission principle below.
5. **Get Them Advertising — Launch.** Use the **Whisper-Tease-Shout** method (p. ~330): Whisper = call-outs / curiosity, weeks/months out. Tease = elements of value, ~14 days out. Shout = call-to-action, scarcity, urgency, last 3 days.
6. **Keep Them Advertising — Integrate.** Three integration models (pp. 333–334): (a) affiliates *give away* your lead magnet with their core offer; (b) affiliates *sell* your lead magnet; (c) affiliates *sell your core offer* directly.

### Commission principle

"I suggest paying affiliates based on your maximum allowable cost to acquire a customer (CAC)" (p. ~328). His preferred structure is **three-tier payouts** (pp. 328–329):

| Tier | Trigger | Payout (% of max CAC) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | They *agree* (sign up, buy products/certification) | 25% |
| 2 | They *activate* (finish certification, do a launch) | 50% |
| 3 | They *sustain* a level of performance | 100% |

The hidden benefit: average payout is much less than max CAC, so the blended LTGP:CAC improves (e.g., from 3:1 to 4:1 in his worked example, p. ~329). Add Pro Tip "Sell 3, Get It Free" — pay with *product* at lower tiers (you give retail value, but only your cost) (p. ~330). Pay them for what you actually want: "I pay affiliates for two basic things: new customers, and repeat customers" (p. ~328).

### Representative pitch example

> "Make more money from your current customers… without needing to build, deliver, or provide customer support… so you can start selling tomorrow." (p. ~323, ≤25 words)

### Mistakes

1. Launching to affiliates without making them invest first — "if they don't believe in your stuff enough to buy it, they probably shouldn't sell it" (p. ~324).
2. Paying everyone the full max CAC — "I used to give away the farm" (p. ~328); use tiers so weak affiliates don't drain the pool.
3. Starting affiliates without integration — you get a one-time launch spike, not a long-term lead source (p. ~333; mirrors his Prestige Labs $150K-then-$450K story, p. ~316).
4. Skipping qualification of who can become an affiliate. Not all affiliates are created equal (p. ~328).
5. Capping payouts. "I never cap payouts" — capping demotivates the people sending you the most customers (p. ~334).

### Ethics / guardrails

- "You gotta buy it to ethically sell it" (p. ~324) — the affiliate must be an actual customer.
- Treat affiliates like customers: "if you deliver more value to them than it costs them to get it (especially hidden costs), they'll get you more leads than you can handle" (p. ~349).
- "You are only as good as the goodwill you have with your affiliate partners" (p. ~349) — both sides should be better off, not just you.
- Be honest about the work. Affiliates fail when you let them launch without preparing the audience; the *whisper-tease-shout* sequence ethically warms the audience instead of cold-spamming.

## Section IV Conclusion — The Unifying Principle

Closing epigraph: *"The last skill you ever need to learn is how to get other people to do everything you need for you"* (p. ~349).

Hormozi's synthesis (pp. 349–351):

- All four Lead Getters do one thing — they let other people advertise *your* stuff using the Core Four. "*You get more leads for the work you do when you have help.*"
- Each Lead Getter has a single key strength: **Customer referrals** = exponential, low-cost growth; **Employees** = your direct influence, the business runs without you; **Agencies** = skills you keep forever; **Affiliates** = an army that runs once you set it up.
- **Two ways to compound a business:** "find more people that never stop buying your stuff *or* find more people who never stop selling it for you. Referrals are the former. Affiliates are the [latter]" (p. ~349).
- **Order of operations follows what happens naturally:** customers → employees → agencies → affiliates. Don't try to do them out of order or all at once.
- **Stick with one thing long enough to win.** "Once you find something that works for you — *stick to what you pick*… It's normal to lose in the beginning. In fact, I *expect* to crack a new lead source in three to six months" (p. ~351).

The deepest takeaway is the inversion of "advertising": advertising is what you do to *attract Lead Getters*, not just customers. Once Lead Getters are in place, they re-run the Core Four for you forever — and that is the actual mechanism behind the "$100M Leads" claim.
